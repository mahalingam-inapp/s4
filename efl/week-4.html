Week at a glance

Week 4 studies destructive human tendencies through three epic characters and then translates those lessons into organizational behaviour:

  • Ravana: unchecked ego, pride, self-delusion, refusal to listen, and the loss of dharma.
  • Duryodhana: jealousy, greed, entitlement, dishonour, stubbornness, and their widening consequences.
  • Shakuni: revenge, deceit, manipulation, tactical brilliance without ethics, and the self-defeating nature of vengeance.
  • Live session: ego versus the deeper self; healthy versus inflated ego; the psychology of envy and greed; deception and manipulation at work; the individual–team–organization ripple effect; and five management strategies for handling negative emotions.

The common pattern is:

Emotional wound or insecurity → distorted thinking → unethical choice → rejection of corrective advice → damaged relationships and institutions → eventual personal and collective downfall.

These stories are therefore not merely mythology. They are studies in psychology, ethics, leadership, decision-making, and organizational culture.

Video 1 — Ravana: The Psychological Fall of a King

1. Learning objectives

The session aims to:

  1. understand how Ravana’s pride led to his downfall;
  2. examine the chain of events from Sita’s abduction to the battle with Rama; and
  3. connect Ravana’s story with the consequences of ego in the modern world.

The opening proposition is that “evil” in the Puranas often reflects deeper emotional struggles or flaws that take control of action. A person is not necessarily devoid of ability or virtue; the tragedy occurs when an inner flaw begins to govern decisions.

2. Ravana before the fall: ability without inner mastery

Ravana, king of Lanka and antagonist of the Ramayana, was not presented as a one-dimensional villain. At his prime he was a mighty and respected ruler.

  • He was born to Sage Vishrava and the Rakshasi Kaikeshi.
  • He was intellectually brilliant from childhood.
  • He was a scholar versed in the Vedas and other sacred texts.
  • Under his father’s guidance, he mastered warfare and magic.
  • He combined military power, wisdom, scholarship, leadership, and devotion to knowledge.
  • Under him, Lanka became a prosperous, strong, and wealthy kingdom.
  • He earned the respect of his people and allies.

This makes his fall psychologically important: lack of talent did not destroy him. The problem was that growing power produced a feeling of invincibility. Superiority clouded judgment, and internal flaws redirected extraordinary potential toward destruction.

Exam insight: Competence does not guarantee ethical leadership. A highly capable leader who lacks humility and self-regulation can be more dangerous because greater ability amplifies the consequences of flawed choices.

3. The boon: quiet arrogance and the danger of dismissing the “ordinary”

Ravana undertook severe penance, cutting off and offering his heads one by one. Moved by his devotion, Brahma appeared and offered a boon. Ravana knew general immortality would not be granted, so he crafted a request for invincibility against gods, demons, celestial beings, serpents, and wild animals.

He deliberately omitted humans—and, in his thinking, monkeys—because he considered them too weak and ordinary to matter. This illustrates a particularly dangerous form of arrogance: not loud boasting, but the silent assumption that one already knows who or what is important.

That blind spot became his undoing. Rama, appearing as a human whom Ravana would have dismissed, ultimately defeated him, with the vanara forces playing a decisive role.

Management parallel (illustrative): A dominant firm may monitor large competitors but dismiss a small start-up, a junior employee’s idea, or a low-cost technology. The overlooked category can become the source of disruption. Strategic risk often lives inside an assumption that “this cannot threaten us.”

4. Sita’s abduction: the true beginning of defeat

The decisive turning point was not the battlefield but the abduction of Sita. Ravana already had a history of domination and arrogance, but this act revealed the governing condition of his mind.

Trigger and distorted response

Surpanakha, Ravana’s sister, was insulted by Rama and Lakshmana, and Lakshmana cut off her nose. Ravana did not respond with balance or dignity. He took the event personally. What might have begun as concern for his sister became a need to prove a point and emotionally wound Rama.

The abduction was therefore:

  • not sound strategy or statecraft;
  • not truly an act of protective love;
  • not a proportionate political response;
  • an act of revenge, control, and wounded pride;
  • based on the belief that power gives one the right to take what one wants.

Ravana did not pause to ask:

  • Is this right?
  • What consequences will follow?
  • Am I crossing a line from which I cannot return?

His arrogance had silenced judgment. From this point onward his decisions became reactive, desperate, and defensive. He was no longer truly ruling Lanka; he was protecting a false image of control.

A person need not lose a war to be defeated; losing oneself to pride is already defeat.

5. Pride versus dharma

Ravana’s inner fall began when pride became more important than dharma—right conduct, justice, moral duty, and an order larger than personal desire. This distinction is crucial:

  • Pride asks: How can I preserve my image and dominance?
  • Dharma asks: What is right, just, and responsible in this situation?

Once a leader makes self-image the highest value, evidence and ethics are treated as threats. Actions then serve reputation rather than purpose.

6. Blindness to advice and psychological isolation

Vibhishana, Ravana’s own brother, was among the wisest and most loyal people in his court. He saw the danger, warned Ravana about the consequences of abducting Sita, and urged him to return her to Rama to prevent a catastrophic war.

Ravana rejected the advice. Worse, he mocked Vibhishana as weak and indecisive. Ravana assumed his strength was unmatched and that Rama could not challenge him. Advice therefore appeared irrelevant.

This was not a single listening failure; it demonstrated psychological isolation:

  • Ravana’s inflated self-importance made disagreement intolerable.
  • Pride blinded him to the possibility that he could be wrong.
  • He rejected the people who cared enough to tell him the truth.
  • By closing himself to correction, he also closed the path to redemption.

Had he humbled himself and returned Sita, a different ending might have remained possible. Instead, he actively chose the conditions of his downfall.

Leadership use case: When a CEO labels risk officers or dissenting board members “negative,” warnings disappear from the discussion but not from reality. A leader surrounded only by agreement becomes informationally powerful-looking but strategically blind.

7. Rama and Ravana: a battle of inner worlds

The final conflict is more than a physical war between powerful warriors, weapons, and strategies. It is a conflict between values and mindsets.

Ravana Rama
Fights from ego and wounded pride Fights from dharma and justice
Reacts impulsively Responds with balance
Seeks control Shows restraint
Assumes the ordinary cannot threaten him Remains rooted in purpose
Ignores warnings Acts with clarity
Has power but no space for reflection Possesses inner steadiness
Becomes isolated Is aligned with a righteous cause

Ravana entered confidently because he had ruled Lanka, defeated many enemies, and possessed powerful boons. Yet he had failed to conquer his ego. He viewed Rama merely as a forest prince and could not accept that someone “ordinary” might threaten him.

Rama did not go to war merely to prove himself. His strength came from clarity rather than loud declarations. Ravana lost not because he lacked power, but because pride prevented listening, reflection, and change. Rama prevailed because righteousness was not displaced by ego.

8. Ravana’s story as modern psychology

Ego and pride remain visible in workplaces, politics, families, institutions, classrooms, and colleges. People often decide according to their need to be right rather than according to what is right.

Unchecked ego causes a person to stop:

  • listening;
  • learning;
  • accepting responsibility;
  • reflecting;
  • changing.

Arrogance and self-delusion are connected. Believing oneself always right or above others weakens contact with reality. News repeatedly shows talented people in power making poor choices, rejecting responsibility, and losing their position—not because they lacked technical skill, but because they would not change.

Ravana’s ego was not only love of power; it was refusal to believe he could be wrong. Everyday equivalents include defending an opinion after contrary evidence appears, refusing to apologize, or treating feedback as humiliation.

Unacknowledged mistakes are also internally costly. They can disturb sleep, relationships, confidence, and peace of mind. An “inner storm” develops when a person avoids doing what is right. Ravana fought Rama outwardly while also being at war with himself.

9. Self-awareness and transformation

Facing flaws is uncomfortable, but avoiding them is more painful. Recognizing a flaw and saying “I need to improve” is not weakness; it is the beginning of strength.

Individual transformation also has a social effect. When individuals become more reflective, they influence families, teams, and society. Personal growth therefore has collective consequences.

10. Practical methods for loosening ego’s grip

10.1 Catch ego in action

Notice recurring triggers and patterns, such as reacting strongly when challenged or disagreed with. Awareness must precede change.

10.2 Accept feedback

Ego especially dislikes honest and uncomfortable feedback, but growth requires it. Instead of immediate justification, ask: What can I learn from this? Criticism should be examined as information, not automatically treated as a personal attack. Feedback is meant to sharpen, not shrink.

10.3 Replace “I know” with “I am learning”

Ego wants to appear the smartest in the room and thrives on “I already know.” True intelligence remains open to new ideas, people, and perspectives. Curiosity removes the burden of always being right and enables exploration and growth.

10.4 Detach from the need to be right

Ask: Do I want to win this argument, or solve the problem? Many conflicts and lost opportunities arise because winning becomes more important than kindness, peace, or resolution. Letting go of the need to win is self-control, not weakness.

10.5 Acknowledge others’ strengths

In group projects, teams, and offices, insecure people may struggle to say, “That was a great idea” or “You handled that well.” Acknowledgment does not reduce one’s own worth; it shows enough confidence to lift others.

10.6 Apologize when necessary

“I was wrong” may feel like surrendering control, but a genuine apology demonstrates self-awareness and maturity. It moves a person from ego toward growth and connection. It makes one wiser, not smaller.

10.7 Focus on purpose, not position

Ask why an action is being taken: to serve a meaningful purpose, or to be seen in a particular way? Ego is obsessed with titles, recognition, position, and power. Values and purpose produce better decisions, relationships, leadership, and life.

Ego cannot be eliminated once and for all; it repeatedly returns. Progress occurs each time one chooses:

  • reflection over reaction;
  • curiosity over control;
  • purpose over pride.

11. Video 1 key takeaways

  1. Ravana possessed leadership, scholarship, devotion, and power; lack of ability was not his problem.
  2. Power gradually became invincibility and superiority in his mind.
  3. His boon exposed the strategic danger of dismissing supposedly weak actors.
  4. Sita’s abduction was a pride-driven attempt at revenge and control, not statecraft.
  5. His moral defeat began before the physical war, when pride displaced dharma.
  6. Rejecting Vibhishana’s advice psychologically isolated him and removed a path to redemption.
  7. Rama and Ravana represent purpose versus pride, balance versus impulse, and humility versus arrogance.
  8. Modern ego is visible whenever people protect being right at the cost of doing right.
  9. Self-awareness, feedback, curiosity, acknowledgment, apology, and purpose are correctives.

Video 2 — The Curse of Duryodhana

1. Learning objectives and central proposition

The session examines the events leading to Duryodhana’s downfall and derives lessons from greed and jealousy. Its opening principle is that evil may rise, but it does not endure without consequences.

2. Duryodhana’s role and strengths

Duryodhana was the eldest son of Dhritarashtra, the blind Kuru king, and leader of the one hundred Kaurava brothers. As eldest, he felt responsible for protecting his family’s place in the kingdom. He was a skilled warrior and brilliant strategist, feared and respected on the battlefield, with many qualities of a great military leader.

But responsibility was mishandled because ego, jealousy, and greed governed his decisions. He could not celebrate the Pandavas’ success as the success of relatives; he interpreted their respect and achievement as a threat.

3. Jealousy, greed, entitlement, and the refusal to share

Duryodhana’s downfall was rooted in:

  • jealousy of the Pandavas’ growing respect and success;
  • greed for complete power;
  • fear of losing control;
  • refusal to share the kingdom;
  • a sense of entitlement to the throne;
  • belief in his superiority to the Pandavas.

He chose conflict over compromise. His belief that the throne was destined for him justified, in his mind, extreme measures even at the cost of family peace. He used dishonesty, manipulation, violence, and rule-breaking rather than honourable means. Thus ego led to moral corruption.

His refusal to share and make peace escalated the conflict until the Kurukshetra war became unavoidable.

4. Disregard of wise counsel and lack of self-awareness

Duryodhana was surrounded by respected, experienced elders such as Bhishma, Drona, and Vidura. Like Ravana, he dismissed counsel. He considered their approach weak or soft, assumed he knew better, and followed an arrogance-driven path.

A major flaw was inability to recognize his own mistakes. Clouded judgment produced irrational choices, especially in confrontations with the Pandavas. Stubbornness and refusal to compromise repeatedly increased tension.

Comparison with Ravana: Both leaders possessed ability, had access to wise advisers, misread counsel as weakness, and preferred self-confirming pride to corrective truth.

5. The game of dice: jealousy operationalized as a rigged system

Duryodhana felt especially threatened after the Pandavas’ royal ceremony at Indraprastha. Jealousy led him to conclude that they did not deserve their success. Greed then converted the feeling into a plan to take their wealth and power.

With his uncle Shakuni, he arranged a dice game knowing it was rigged. The aim was not fair competition but the defeat of the Pandavas without conventional war.

The Pandavas lost:

  • their kingdom;
  • their wealth;
  • their freedom; and
  • eventually Draupadi, who was staked in the game.

This was a setup driven by desire for total control, not a legitimate loss.

6. Draupadi’s humiliation and curse

Duryodhana’s wrongdoing did not end with material dispossession. He allowed and encouraged Draupadi’s public humiliation in the royal court. Dushasana dragged her into the assembly and attempted to disrobe her before everyone.

The incident:

  • crossed moral boundaries;
  • violated Draupadi’s dignity;
  • personally insulted the Pandavas;
  • exposed complete lack of respect and fairness;
  • transformed political rivalry into profound moral injury.

Draupadi vowed vengeance and cursed Duryodhana and the Kaurava family: his pride and arrogance would cause the fall of the family and he would meet a miserable end. Duryodhana dismissed the warning because he believed himself invincible. The later destruction of his family fulfilled the warning in the narrative’s moral logic.

7. Karna: loyalty, recognition, pride, and moral compromise

Karna bore a major share of the consequences of Duryodhana’s pride, jealousy, and greed. His story combines loyalty, honour, pride, duty, rejection, and tragedy.

  • Karna knew that Duryodhana’s actions and ego were leading them toward the wrong path.
  • He nevertheless remained loyal because of deep friendship and because Duryodhana had given him respect and recognition when society had rejected him.
  • His strong sense of honour toward his benefactor overrode his inner moral compass.
  • Karna and Duryodhana mirrored one another: Duryodhana felt entitled to the throne; Karna felt entitled to recognition as a great warrior.
  • Both allowed pride and a misguided sense of righteousness to justify harmful decisions.

Karna’s death is linked to this alliance. During the war his chariot wheel became stuck in mud, leaving him vulnerable; Arjuna, guided by Krishna, defeated him. The lecture’s interpretation is that Duryodhana’s cause placed Karna in a position where he constantly had to prove his worth while fighting for the wrong side. His loyalty, though personally understandable, became ethically destructive.

Exam distinction: Loyalty is not automatically virtuous. Loyalty detached from moral judgment can sustain injustice. Ethical loyalty includes the courage to challenge or leave a wrongful cause.

8. War, losses, and final defiance

As the war progressed, the damage caused by Duryodhana’s choices became unmistakable:

  • jealousy and refusal to share power produced the war;
  • his army weakened day by day;
  • trusted allies died;
  • his family suffered devastating losses;
  • relationships and support structures collapsed;
  • he still would not step back or rethink his approach.

The Pandavas, by contrast, remained focused on a sense of justice rather than personal acquisition, which gave them clarity and strength.

After Bhima struck Duryodhana down, Duryodhana was badly wounded. This could have been a final moment for reflection, responsibility, peace, or amends. Instead, he remained defiant and believed he had not truly lost. Jealousy and greed had shaped his thinking so deeply that even total collapse left little room for healing.

9. Modern consequences of jealousy and greed

9.1 Relationships

Jealousy generates unnecessary conflict in friendships and romantic relationships. Constant suspicion makes communication harder and trust fragile. Greed makes another person feel neglected or undervalued when one partner always demands more.

These emotions can generate:

  • guilt-tripping;
  • gaslighting;
  • controlling behaviour;
  • emotional manipulation;
  • reduced empathy;
  • emotional neglect;
  • a shift from “us” to “me.”

9.2 Mental health

Jealousy encourages continuous comparison with partners, friends, or colleagues. The comparison produces inferiority and low self-esteem—an enduring sense of never being enough.

Jealousy may become obsessive monitoring of another person. Greed may become obsession with money, power, possessions, or success. Because “more” never becomes enough, the result can be emptiness, chronic dissatisfaction, burnout, anxiety, and depression.

9.3 Society

Greed can intensify inequality as people advance by exploiting others, widening the gap between rich and poor. It can also damage the environment through overconsumption, pollution, and exploitation of finite resources.

Jealousy at a collective level converts community into rivalry. Instead of collaboration, groups resent one another’s success. Both jealousy and greed can encourage corruption: breaking laws, manipulating systems, and ignoring ethical standards in politics, business, and daily life.

9.4 Personal growth

Attention directed toward another’s wealth or status is attention not spent on learning, emotional intelligence, skills, or one’s own development. Jealousy and greed also prevent gratitude for existing achievements, blessings, relationships, and opportunities.

9.5 Family dynamics

Inheritance, possessions, or competition for attention can create permanent rifts. When wealth becomes more important than love and emotional connection, a toxic intergenerational cycle develops. Younger members may learn that material acquisition is the highest value and reproduce the same pattern later.

9.6 Corporate greed

Profit, market share, and growth are legitimate business measures, but harm begins when they override ethics and human values.

Forms discussed include:

  • Financial deceit: manipulating data to impress investors or hide losses. The Satyam scandal was cited as a case in which a false image of success collapsed, producing job losses and loss of employee/investor savings.
  • Hustle culture: expecting constant availability, late nights, missed weekends, and unending targets. The assumption “more hours = more output” can cause burnout, anxiety, and poor quality of life. Investment banking and technology start-ups were mentioned as sectors where well-being may be sacrificed.
  • Unfair labour practices: reducing costs through global supply chains in places with weak labour laws, low wages, long hours, and unsafe conditions. Cheap clothing or electronics may carry a hidden human price.

The ethical question is: Is a cheap product worth it if another person pays through unsafe or unfair working conditions?

10. How to overcome jealousy and greed

10.1 Focus on self-improvement

Replace constant comparison with investment in one’s own journey. Celebrate achievements, large or small, and aim to become a better version of oneself. Others then become less threatening.

10.2 Cultivate gratitude

Appreciating what is already present shifts attention away from lack. Happiness does not arise from endlessly wanting more, but from recognizing what is valuable now.

10.3 Make ethical choices

Greed directs action toward self-interest at others’ expense. Integrity, sharing resources, and considering the effects of one’s choices make behaviour more mindful and less selfish.

10.4 Detach from materialism

Possessions and status cannot by themselves provide durable happiness. Relationships, well-being, and meaningful experiences support a more contented life.

11. Video 2 key takeaways

  1. Duryodhana’s talents were undermined by jealousy, greed, entitlement, and fear of losing control.
  2. The dice game institutionalized unfairness: it was designed to dispossess, not compete.
  3. Draupadi’s humiliation marked a decisive moral boundary and gave the conflict a deeply personal and ethical dimension.
  4. Karna shows that gratitude and loyalty can become destructive when they silence conscience.
  5. Duryodhana rejected wise counsel and remained defiant even after total loss.
  6. Jealousy harms trust and self-worth; greed creates endless dissatisfaction.
  7. Their effects spread across relationships, mental health, families, business, society, and the environment.
  8. Self-improvement, gratitude, ethics, and reduced materialism are central correctives.

Video 3 — Shakuni: Master of Deceit and Strategy

1. Learning objectives

The session aims to:

  1. analyze Shakuni’s character;
  2. examine his motives and tactics in manipulating the Kaurava–Pandava conflict; and
  3. draw modern lessons concerning revenge, deceit, manipulation, and ethical behaviour.

2. Identity and role in the conflict

Shakuni, Duryodhana’s maternal uncle, is portrayed as a tactical mastermind behind the Kaurava–Pandava conflict. The lecture suggests that without his schemes the Kurukshetra war might not have occurred. His tactical brilliance served wrongful ends because revenge was his governing purpose.

He was a prince of Gandhara, identified in the lecture with modern Kandahar. His sister Gandhari married Dhritarashtra, blind king of Hastinapura, linking the two families and drawing Shakuni into Kuru politics.

3. Important source caution: folklore versus the original epic

The lecture explicitly identifies the imprisonment-and-starvation narrative as modern folklore, not originally part of the Mahabharata. According to this story, Shakuni’s family was imprisoned and starved by the Kuru family during internal power struggles. Witnessing his relatives die filled him with lasting anger and a determination to destroy the Kuru dynasty.

This distinction should be preserved in an exam answer: the story explains the lecture’s psychological reading of Shakuni, but it should not be presented uncritically as part of the epic’s earliest textual core.

4. Trauma converted into revenge

In the folklore account, starvation and mistreatment scarred Shakuni. Revenge became his sole purpose. He moved from injured family member to master manipulator, willing to shape situations regardless of cost.

The psychological chain is:

suffering and perceived injustice → fixation on retaliation → identity organized around revenge → manipulation of others → destruction far beyond the original target.

His pain may explain his motive, but it does not ethically justify the devastation he caused.

5. The cursed dice as story and symbol

Another tale says that Shakuni made magical dice from his father’s bones. The dice obeyed him, allowing him to control outcomes and win.

Symbolically, the dice represent his entire approach:

  • he did not want a fair game;
  • he wanted control over the game itself;
  • he wanted odds permanently arranged in his favour;
  • intelligence became a tool for trickery rather than truth.

The dice became central to the rigged match that cost the Pandavas their kingdom, freedom, dignity, and exile.

6. Manipulating Duryodhana

After his release, Shakuni moved to Hastinapura with a mission. Duryodhana became the instrument through which he could destabilize and destroy the Kuru family.

Shakuni exploited Duryodhana’s existing weaknesses:

  • jealousy of the Pandavas;
  • desire for status and power;
  • insecurity;
  • entitlement;
  • hostility to compromise.

Effective manipulation often does not create a desire from nothing; it identifies an existing insecurity and repeatedly validates, intensifies, and directs it.

7. The dice game as calculated psychological warfare

The Pandavas staked and lost wealth, kingdom, freedom, and Draupadi. Draupadi’s public humiliation was a blow not only to her but also to the Pandavas and all who witnessed it.

Shakuni’s aim extended beyond winning property. He wanted to break the Pandavas’ spirit and make recovery difficult. The result was thirteen years of exile and loss of power and dignity. Thus the game was a long-term strategic setup: apparent short-term play created future vulnerability and conflict.

8. Preventing peace and pulling strings during war

As war approached, Shakuni continued urging Duryodhana to reject peace offers. He was not interested in resolution because conflict served his revenge. Although not primarily the great battlefield warrior in this account, he remained influential in Kaurava strategy and “pulled the strings” behind decisions that led the family toward disaster.

He was eventually killed by Sahadeva in the final days of the war. The narrative presents his end as the consequence of deceit and manipulation catching up with their author.

9. Revenge plus pride in intellect

Revenge was not Shakuni’s only motivation. He also took pride in his cleverness and believed he could outsmart everyone. His ambition became proof that the Kuru dynasty could be brought down by his wits alone.

This produced two interlocking forms of pride:

  • moralized revenge: “I was wronged, so whatever I do is justified”; and
  • intellectual superiority: “I can control all outcomes because I am cleverer than everyone.”

The plan did hurt his targets, but at enormous cost to everyone, including his own side. This is why revenge is strategically self-defeating even when it appears temporarily successful.

10. Modern deceit and manipulation

Short-term deception may achieve an immediate objective, but commonly produces broken trust, ruined reputation, and institutional decline.

10.1 Corporate deceit and the Satyam example

Deceit occurs when a company intentionally misleads people through financial statements, hidden problems, or false facts for unfair advantage.

The lecture cites the 2009 Satyam Computers scandal: founder Ramalinga Raju falsely inflated financial reports by more than one billion US dollars, making the company appear far more profitable than it was. Disclosure destroyed trust; employees lost jobs and investors lost large sums. The effect extended beyond the company into the wider industry.

Trust is essential to business. Once broken, it may take years to rebuild, and some firms never fully recover.

10.2 Manipulative leadership

A manipulative leader uses psychological tactics to control a team unethically, for example:

  • withholding important information;
  • making promises with no intention to keep them;
  • emotionally pressuring employees;
  • taking credit for an employee’s work;
  • creating fear so people will not question or disagree.

Consequences include a toxic culture, disempowerment, disengagement, unhappiness, low morale, high turnover, and reduced innovation. Without trust, sustainable organizational growth becomes difficult.

10.3 Fraud and deceptive business practice

Fraud is intentional deception to obtain money or benefit. Examples include fake products, inflated profits, and false reports to stakeholders.

Fraud is not only a financial issue; it is a trust issue. It reduces faith in markets, makes investors hesitant, leads to stricter regulation, and increases skepticism toward future business claims.

10.4 Deceit in media

Media can manipulate opinion through biased reporting, selective event coverage, or fake news. The lecture’s consumer example is beauty advertising: models’ skin may be heavily edited and retouched, creating unrealistic standards and suggesting that a product can produce an unattainable flawless appearance. This affects self-esteem, confuses consumers, and weakens public trust.

11. Revenge, personal vendetta, and temporary relief

The desire to retaliate after being wronged may occur in personal, academic, or professional life. Revenge can create momentary victory, but satisfaction fades. The person may then feel hollow and regret:

  • the action taken;
  • the damage caused; and
  • the person they became while pursuing revenge.

A personal vendetta is a deep desire for revenge triggered by perceived injustice and emotions such as anger, humiliation, or betrayal. The person tries to “restore balance” through retaliation. In practice, this usually prolongs conflict and produces emotional exhaustion rather than resolution.

12. Ethics over vendetta

Emotion-driven decisions can lose logic and fairness. For example, an employee who feels undermined may retaliate through gossip or passive resistance. That harms the colleague, team morale, the retaliator’s reputation, and personal integrity. Temporary satisfaction gives way to long-term loss of trust.

Ethics is the practice of deciding through principles such as honesty, respect, fairness, and responsibility. Ethical behaviour requires stepping back, assessing objectively, and choosing integrity rather than revenge.

Practical ethical alternatives include:

  • speaking directly with the person concerned;
  • using appropriate grievance or escalation channels;
  • investigating before assigning blame;
  • accepting appropriate responsibility;
  • seeking accountable resolution rather than humiliation.

Long-term benefits of ethics:

  • credibility and trust;
  • respect and dependable leadership;
  • peaceful and accountable culture;
  • fewer monitoring and defensive behaviours;
  • emotional maturity;
  • stronger relationships;
  • attention redirected toward meaningful goals.

Illustrative case from the lecture: a team leader unfairly blamed for failure can avoid counter-blame, accept responsibility for investigating, determine causes fairly, and resolve the problem constructively. This increases credibility and willingness of others to follow.

Rising above pettiness is not weakness; it is self-respect and emotional discipline.

13. Video 3 key takeaways

  1. Shakuni combined tactical intelligence with revenge and unethical purpose.
  2. The family-starvation story is identified as later folklore, not original Mahabharata material.
  3. The cursed dice symbolize the desire to control rules and outcomes rather than compete fairly.
  4. Shakuni amplified Duryodhana’s existing jealousy and insecurity.
  5. The dice game was psychological and long-term warfare, not a simple contest.
  6. Revenge and pride in cleverness expanded destruction beyond the intended target.
  7. Corporate deceit, manipulative leadership, fraud, and media distortion all trade long-term trust for short-term gain.
  8. Ethical process creates credibility, peace, accountability, and personal growth.
  9. Choose principles over impulses and long-term peace over short-term revenge.

Week 4 Live Session — Behavioural Pitfalls in Management

1. Why this belongs in an MBA programme

Organizations are built on people. Managerial decisions, conversations, negotiations, conflict, teamwork, and leadership all revolve around human behaviour. Managers must understand both other people and themselves.

The aim is not to eradicate emotion in one day. It is to become aware of behavioural pitfalls so that they do not unconsciously control professional and personal conduct. Awareness and gradual improvement support long-term success.

Students were also reminded to watch LMS recordings regularly, use the Q&A platform for questions, and avoid allowing material to pile up at the end of the semester.

2. Ego and self: definitions and relationship

Ego

In ordinary human behaviour, ego is the part of the mind that says, “This is me; this is who I am.” It protects identity, shapes how a person sees themselves, supports interaction with the external world, and reacts when identity feels threatened.

Ego is not automatically negative. People need an ability to protect identity and self-respect. It becomes problematic when the protective function grows controlling or “too loud.” Examples include becoming defensive before considering criticism, treating challenge as an attack, or giving excessive importance to oneself.

Self

The self is the deeper, relatively stable inner identity beyond surface thoughts, roles, physical appearance, and achievements. It includes:

  • awareness;
  • emotions;
  • values;
  • culture;
  • personality;
  • capacity to connect with others;
  • the inner essence that remains while life circumstances change.

Cover-and-content analogy

  • Ego = the cover of a book: the external identity presented and protected in interaction.
  • Self = the content/story inside: the deeper inner truth.

Both matter. They are connected rather than enemies. The goal is balance: use ego when protection and self-respect are needed, but do not allow it to control behaviour. Balance supports an authentic, peaceful, fulfilled life.

3. Common ego triggers

Ego can become activated when:

  • someone criticizes one’s work;
  • someone challenges an idea;
  • a junior or less-experienced employee points out a senior’s mistake;
  • another person praises us, causing pride and exaggerated uniqueness;
  • someone compares us unfavourably with a colleague or new employee;
  • conflict occurs with coworkers, spouses, children, or parents.

Praise as well as criticism can inflate ego. The boundary between necessary self-protection and uncontrolled ego is thin, so trigger awareness is essential.

4. Healthy ego versus inflated ego

4.1 Healthy ego

A healthy ego is a balanced sense of self. One knows strengths and limitations, remains grounded, is not constantly trying to impress others, and does not feel threatened by another person’s success.

Its features are:

  1. Confidence without arrogance: achievements need not be repeatedly announced.
  2. Emotional resilience: criticism does not automatically break the person or trigger defense. Irrelevant malicious criticism can be ignored; useful criticism can be learned from.
  3. Self-respect: the person recognizes their worth.
  4. Growth orientation: the person remains open to improvement.
  5. Gratitude and shared credit: success is acknowledged without denying others’ contribution.

Promotion example: a person with healthy ego can say, “I am proud that my work paid off, but I still have much to learn, I did not reach here alone, and I appreciate those who supported me.” Pride remains grounded because achievement and interdependence are both recognized.

4.2 Inflated ego

An inflated ego exaggerates self-importance and frequently appears in organizations as:

  • arrogance: assuming one is right and dismissing other viewpoints;
  • poor listening: interrupting meetings to force one’s own point;
  • overconfidence: accepting every responsibility to impress a manager and assuming one can do everything;
  • defensiveness: treating negative feedback as an attack and struggling to admit mistakes;
  • superiority masking insecurity: refusing to acknowledge contributors because their success feels threatening.

Promotion example: an inflated response is “I deserved it more; I did better,” with no acknowledgment of support. Outward superiority often conceals inward insecurity.

5. Visual exercise: six negative tendencies

Students interpreted six pictures representing:

  1. deception;
  2. envy;
  3. jealousy;
  4. greed;
  5. manipulation; and
  6. ego.

The activity also demonstrated that perception can differ. The purpose was not merely labeling pictures, but recognizing that these tendencies are observable and relatable in personal and professional life.

6. Psychology of envy

Envy occurs when another person receives something one wanted—recognition, promotion, appreciation, or opportunity. Instead of happiness for the colleague, a person feels an internal “pinch” and asks, “Why did I not get it?”

Consequences include:

  • self-doubt and wondering whether one is good enough;
  • reduced collaboration with the successful person;
  • repeated silent comparison;
  • difficulty working together;
  • a comparison spiral.

The upward comparison trap is repeated comparison with someone perceived as doing better. It can persist across situations and consume attention that could be used for improvement.

7. Psychology of greed

Greed differs from envy:

  • Envy looks outward: someone else has what I want.
  • Greed looks inward: what I have is never enough; I need more.

Greed converts achievement into an immediate question: “What next? What more can I get?” It must be distinguished from healthy ambition or a wish to improve. In the lecture, greed is associated with insecurity, scarcity thinking, the need to prove oneself, and dependence on external validation.

Professional consequences include:

  • permanent dissatisfaction;
  • unethical shortcuts to success or profit;
  • growing performance pressure;
  • personal gain replacing teamwork;
  • strained personal and professional relationships.

Envy and greed share a root: “Who I am now is not enough unless I have more or outperform someone.” Both reflect dissatisfaction with oneself and create emotional imbalance.

Two-manager example

Two equally competent managers join together. At appraisal, one becomes senior manager and the other does not.

  • The unpromoted manager thinks, “Why them before me? What do they have that I do not?” and compares instead of seeking feedback: envy.
  • The promoted manager enjoys salary and recognition but immediately wants a bigger title, cabin, team, and bonus, perhaps taking unnecessary risks or compromising standards: greed.

8. Deception at work

Deception is not limited to an explicit lie. Withholding important information can also deceive because it prevents others from making sound decisions.

Project-status example

A team reports “green, no issues, no risks” every Monday. Two days before the client presentation, someone admits a problem has existed for two weeks. The manager asks, “If you knew earlier, why am I only hearing now?”

The team member may have feared embarrassment or criticism and told themselves they were buying time. In reality, they reduced the opportunity for help and lost credibility. The delayed disclosure affected the manager, client, team, and project.

Key idea: In organizations, truth is an asset. Once information is doubted, decisions slow down because people demand more reports, approvals, and evidence. Productivity falls not through incompetence, but because trust has disappeared.

9. Manipulation at work

Manipulation influences another person’s decision while avoiding responsibility for that influence.

Example: two managers seek the same promotion. One focuses on results. The other shapes perceptions through remarks such as, “I am only concerned whether that team can handle the project,” or “I do not know all the facts, but that is what I heard.” The speaker may not make a directly provable false statement, but plants doubt.

Manipulation is difficult to prove because it may occur through:

  • casual conversations;
  • selective communication;
  • informal influence;
  • corridor discussions after the official meeting;
  • unsupported suggestions rather than written claims.

Pressure to meet targets, please clients, perform, or explain delays can tempt people to hide bad news or present only the positive side. This may solve today’s problem while creating a larger one tomorrow.

10. Ripple effect: individual → team → organization

One person’s behaviour does not stay with that person. Positive and negative patterns spread and, when repeated, become culture.

Level 1: Individual

Unchecked envy, greed, fear, and insecurity first damage the person through:

  • emotional exhaustion;
  • chronic stress;
  • poor decisions under pressure;
  • ethical compromise to protect oneself or achieve a short-term goal;
  • eventual loss of credibility.

Credibility, once lost, is difficult and slow to rebuild.

Level 2: Team

Individual behaviour becomes a management problem when it affects colleagues:

  • people stop sharing information openly;
  • self-protection replaces collaboration;
  • “This is not my responsibility” replaces mutual support;
  • meetings become arenas for defense instead of problem-solving;
  • aggression, tension, and visible unhappiness increase;
  • teamwork declines.

Level 3: Organization

When the same patterns repeat across teams, they become organizational culture:

  • employees and departments stop trusting each other;
  • talented performers may leave despite liking the job itself;
  • the working environment becomes a major reason for resignation;
  • innovation slows because negativity suppresses contribution of ideas;
  • customers detect disengagement and frustration;
  • financial performance and reputation suffer.

Customer experience example

The instructor described visiting a restaurant where no one promptly took the order and the eventual waiter appeared disinterested and unsmiling. Rather than assuming only an individual attitude problem, she considered whether a wider organizational issue was making everyone unhappy. Similar patterns may be visible in customer-care interactions, banks, government offices, or any service where many employees show the same rudeness or indifference. Customers can “feel” culture through employee behaviour.

11. Five evidence-based management strategies

Negative emotions cannot be completely eliminated because organizations contain human beings, not robots. Envy, frustration, and insecurity are part of human experience. The objective is to prevent them from controlling decisions, relationships, and leadership.

11.1 Emotional awareness and reflection

Recognize and name the emotion before reacting.

Example: another employee is chosen to lead a high-visibility project. The mind quickly invents a story: “Has my manager lost confidence? Is my work unnoticed?” Instead of spiraling, acknowledge: “I feel disappointed and insecure.” The decision has already occurred, so constructively respond to the email or speak with the manager to understand the criteria. Emotional awareness replaces imaginary scenarios with reflection and inquiry.

11.2 Ethical decision-making lens

Many unethical decisions begin not with openly evil intentions but with pressure—deadlines, targets, client expectations, and performance demands. Before speaking, deciding, or concluding, check the choice against personal and organizational ethical frameworks. Pressure explains temptation but does not remove responsibility.

11.3 Cognitive reframing

Reframe before reacting. Instead of “They are valued more than I am,” ask, “What did they do differently that I can learn from?” This does not require assuming management is always correct; it requires considering another perspective realistically.

Instructor’s example: a former colleague repeatedly received promotions. Her initial story was “pure luck,” and she said this to herself and family. Later she recognized he probably possessed relevant qualities—communication, delivery, ideas, or execution. She reframed the promotion as potentially deserved and wished him well. That change of interpretation is cognitive reframing.

11.4 Transparent communication

Do not build decisions on assumptions. If struggling with a deadline or negative thoughts, clarify with the appropriate person. Transparency does not mean telling everything to everyone; it means communicating relevant truth to the right person at the right time rather than remaining silent.

11.5 Intrinsic goal orientation

Shift from comparison with others to comparison with one’s earlier self:

  • How can I improve?
  • What can I change?
  • What actions can I take?
  • Am I better than last year, or unchanged?

Intrinsic growth gives a more stable basis for motivation than external ranking and validation.

12. Nuance from the closing discussion: is all deception bad?

A student observed that deception is not always bad, giving the familiar example of parents using harmless stories to persuade a child to eat. The instructor agreed that context and harm matter: deception that harms others is wrong, while personal-life examples may be more nuanced. However, in professional settings deception is especially consequential because it affects trust, responsibility, and other stakeholders.

This should not be read as permission to mislead at work; it is a reminder to evaluate intention, harm, consent, role responsibility, and consequences.

13. Live-session key takeaways

  1. Ego protects external identity; the self is the deeper inner truth.
  2. Ego is useful in balance but damaging when it becomes controlling.
  3. Healthy ego combines confidence, resilience, self-respect, learning, and shared credit.
  4. Inflated ego appears as arrogance, poor listening, overconfidence, defensiveness, and superiority masking insecurity.
  5. Envy concerns another’s advantage; greed concerns one’s inability to feel that anything is enough.
  6. Both arise from insecurity and dissatisfaction with self.
  7. Withholding critical information is a form of workplace deception.
  8. Manipulation plants or shapes perceptions while hiding responsibility for influence.
  9. Negative behaviour spreads from individuals to teams and then becomes organizational culture visible even to customers.
  10. The five correctives are emotional awareness, ethical decision-making, cognitive reframing, transparent communication, and intrinsic goal orientation.

2. Three distinct but connected pitfalls

  • Ego: protects identity but becomes destructive when being right or superior matters more than truth.
  • Jealousy/envy: discomfort at another person’s advantage or success.
  • Greed: inability to experience enoughness; continuous desire for more.
  • Revenge: desire to retaliate for perceived injustice.
  • Deceit: intentional misleading, including material concealment.
  • Manipulation: shaping another’s perception or decision without owning the influence.

They reinforce one another. Insecurity may produce envy; envy may produce greed or retaliation; ego prevents admission of error; deceit and manipulation become tools; rejection of feedback blocks recovery.

3. Leadership-failure cycle

  1. Trigger: criticism, comparison, perceived insult, exclusion, or injustice.
  2. Inner emotion: insecurity, humiliation, envy, fear, anger.
  3. Distorted story: “I alone am right,” “They do not deserve it,” “I must prove myself,” or “Retaliation is justice.”
  4. Unethical tactic: domination, rigging, concealment, manipulation, revenge.
  5. Rejection of feedback: advisers are called weak, disloyal, or irrelevant.
  6. Isolation and distrust: truth stops reaching the leader.
  7. Ripple effect: individual judgment damages teams, institutions, families, and society.
  8. Downfall: loss of trust, relationships, legitimacy, reputation, and peace.

4. Recovery cycle

  1. Pause and name the emotion.
  2. Separate identity from the criticism or outcome.
  3. Ask for facts and feedback.
  4. Reframe the interpretation.
  5. Apply an ethical lens.
  6. Communicate transparently with the right stakeholder.
  7. Choose purpose and long-term consequences over status and impulse.
  8. Apologize, correct, and learn where necessary.
  9. Measure growth intrinsically rather than through constant comparison.

Practical Use Cases

Use case 1: A junior challenges a senior manager

An inflated ego interrupts or dismisses the junior to preserve status. A healthy ego asks for evidence, thanks the junior, and corrects the plan if necessary. The immediate issue may be technical, but the longer-term effect is cultural: how the senior responds determines whether future risks will be reported.

Use case 2: A colleague receives a promotion

  • Envious response: reduce cooperation, attribute success to luck, and spread doubt.
  • Greedy promoted response: instantly demand a larger role, title, and bonus without consolidating learning.
  • Constructive response: acknowledge disappointment, request feedback, identify learnable behaviour, congratulate the colleague, and set intrinsic development goals.

Use case 3: Project risk emerges before a client deadline

Concealing the risk to avoid embarrassment resembles workplace deception. Early transparent escalation allows corrective action. The ethical manager builds a no-surprise culture where bad news can travel upward without humiliation, while deliberate concealment remains accountable.

Use case 4: Competition for a leadership role

One candidate delivers results; another informally plants uncertainty about the rival. The second behaviour resembles Shakuni’s manipulation: it changes perceptions without taking responsibility for a factual claim. A fair process should use evidence, documented criteria, multiple evaluators, and an opportunity to respond.

Use case 5: Profits versus employee well-being

Constant availability may improve short-term output but produce burnout, turnover, errors, and reputational harm. Duryodhana’s greed lesson applies: refusing to recognize “enough” can destroy the very system from which value is extracted.

Use case 6: A leader ignores risk counsel

A product or compliance leader warns of safety or ethical concerns. Calling the adviser “too cautious” parallels Ravana and Duryodhana dismissing wise counsel. Good governance protects dissent through independent review, escalation channels, and recorded risk decisions.

Use case 7: Workplace retaliation

An employee unfairly blamed for a failure can gossip and obstruct, obtaining short-term emotional relief, or gather evidence, speak directly, and use the formal grievance process. The latter represents ethics over vendetta and protects credibility.

Final synthesis

Week 4’s deepest lesson is that downfall rarely begins with the final visible crisis. It begins when a person allows an emotional wound, insecurity, or inflated identity to overrule reflection and ethics. Ravana, Duryodhana, and Shakuni all had intelligence or capability, but none used inner awareness to restrain the flaw directing that capability. In modern management, the equivalent failures are ignored feedback, concealed information, manipulative influence, comparison, greed, and cultures of distrust. Sustainable leadership therefore requires humility, truthful communication, ethical decision-making, emotional awareness, and a willingness to correct oneself before the private flaw becomes a collective disaster.
