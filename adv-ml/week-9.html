1. Natural language processing and text in ML

Definition and scope

Natural language processing is an intersection/subfield of computer science and artificial intelligence concerned with processing human language and making it machine-readable. Human natural language can include:

  • written text;
  • spoken language/speech;
  • sign language and gestures;
  • facial expressions used communicatively.

Computers do not directly understand English, Hindi, Tamil, Malayalam, or another human language as humans do. ML algorithms require numbers, so NLP acts as a bridge:

\[ \text{unstructured human language} \longrightarrow \text{structured, computable numerical features} \]

Only after conversion can a model detect patterns, train, classify, retrieve, compare, or predict.

Forms of text

Text occurs as paragraphs, tables, web pages/HTML, emails, social-media posts, documents, and many other sources.

Form Description Example
Structured Consistent schema/fields Clean database table with aligned features
Semi-structured Some organization but not a rigid relational schema HTML/web page
Unstructured/raw No fixed feature layout Email body, social post, free-form feedback

All may require preprocessing. Preprocessing brings usable structure to raw/unstructured language and improves the efficiency/quality of later representations.

Applications mentioned

  • Speech/voice recognition: access control and many industrial systems.
  • Document summarization: reduce pages of material to a few sentences containing the main essence; useful in search and information review.
  • Plagiarism/similarity detection: compare submitted and source documents.
  • Automated question answering.
  • Search engines/information retrieval: rank documents relevant to a query.
  • Text classification: e.g., student-feedback categories in the EduLearn case.

2. End-to-end text-mining pipeline

A typical classical pipeline is:

  1. Collect text.
  2. Clean noise/markup.
  3. Normalize case and punctuation according to the task.
  4. Tokenize.
  5. Optionally remove stop words.
  6. Optionally stem or lemmatize.
  7. Build a training vocabulary.
  8. Convert documents to BoW/count vectors.
  9. Weight features with TF–IDF if needed.
  10. Train a classifier or place documents in a vector-space retrieval model.
  11. Apply the same fitted preprocessing and vocabulary to validation/test text or queries.
  12. Evaluate accuracy/retrieval quality, memory, computation, and business usefulness.

Preprocessing is not universally beneficial. Removing or merging a token can destroy a signal—e.g., removing “not” can reverse sentiment. Choose steps for the task and validate them empirically.

3. Text preprocessing techniques

3.1 Text cleansing and normalization

Common operations include:

  • remove HTML tags, special characters, unwanted markup, and noise;
  • standardize case (often lowercase);
  • handle punctuation;
  • normalize whitespace;
  • decide how to treat numbers, URLs, usernames, emojis, etc.

Without lowercase normalization, Learning and learning become two vocabulary features. This inflates dimensionality even when the distinction is irrelevant.

3.2 Tokenization

Tokenization splits a text/string into units called tokens, commonly words or subwords. Example:

artificial neural network
→ [artificial, neural, network]

Tokens are the units counted by BoW and TF–IDF. A tokenizer defines what happens to punctuation, contractions, hyphens, and special symbols, so tokenization decisions affect the vocabulary.

3.3 Stop-word removal

Stop words are very frequent function words that often add little discriminative information in a particular retrieval/classification task, such as a, an, the, to, and is. Removing them:

  • shrinks vocabulary and vectors;
  • reduces noise and computation;
  • prevents frequent generic terms dominating counts.

However, stop-word lists are task-specific. Negations and certain grammatical words can be meaningful. In the recorded example, to was removed as unhelpful for that BoW task.

3.4 Stemming

Stemming uses heuristic truncation rules to map variants to an approximate root/stem:

running, runner, runs → run (intended family)
changing, changed → chang/change depending on stemmer

Advantages:

  • fast and computationally inexpensive;
  • aggressive vocabulary reduction;
  • less memory and potentially faster training.

Disadvantages:

  • output may not be a valid dictionary word;
  • over-stemming may merge distinct meanings;
  • under-stemming may fail to merge true variants;
  • semantic distinctions can be lost.

3.5 Lemmatization

Lemmatization uses vocabulary and morphological/linguistic analysis to map a word to its valid dictionary base form (lemma):

changing, changed → change
better → good  (with appropriate linguistic analysis)

Advantages:

  • valid dictionary forms;
  • usually better linguistic/semantic consistency;
  • less destructive than crude truncation.

Disadvantages:

  • slower and more computationally expensive;
  • may need part-of-speech/context information;
  • often reduces vocabulary less aggressively than stemming.

Stemming versus lemmatization

Aspect Stemming Lemmatization
Method Heuristic cutting Linguistic/morphological analysis
Output May be invalid stem Valid dictionary lemma
Speed Faster Slower
Vocabulary reduction Usually more aggressive Usually more conservative
Meaning preservation Greater risk Better on average

Neither technique solves BoW’s inability to model word order, synonyms, antonyms, or context. They only consolidate morphological forms.

4. Bag of Words

Definition

Feature extraction transforms data into a form suitable for ML. Bag of Words is a foundational feature-extraction technique that represents a document by the presence or count of vocabulary terms, ignoring:

  • word order/position;
  • grammar and sentence structure;
  • context and compositional meaning.

It is called a “bag” because the tokens are treated as an unordered collection.

Vocabulary and vector construction

Given a corpus:

  1. preprocess/tokenize documents;
  2. list each distinct retained token once to form vocabulary \(V=[t_1,\dots,t_{|V|}]\);
  3. assign each term a fixed coordinate;
  4. for document \(d\), store count \(c(t_i,d)\) at coordinate \(i\).

\[ \mathbf d=[c(t_1,d),c(t_2,d),\ldots,c(t_{|V|},d)] \]

Presence-only/binary BoW uses 1 if the term occurs and 0 otherwise; count BoW preserves frequency. The recordings discuss both occurrence and count, with the worked vectors using counts.

Recorded two-sentence example

The sentences are approximately:

S1: Welcome to Great Learning. Now start learning.
S2: Learning is a good practice.

Without preprocessing: punctuation/case are retained, so capitalized Learning and lowercase learning can become separate terms; common words and punctuation variants enlarge the vocabulary. A document vector position exists for every vocabulary term even if its count is zero.

With preprocessing:

  • lowercase text;
  • remove punctuation;
  • remove stop words such as to and is;
  • combine Learning and learning under one token.

Then learning occurs twice in \(S1\) and once in \(S2\). The lecture states the corrected \(S1\) vector as 1121100 for its displayed vocabulary and explicitly notes a correction to the displayed \(S2\) vector. The transcript garbles that second sequence (000010011) relative to the apparent vocabulary size, so reconstruct vectors from the exact vocabulary/count table rather than memorizing the corrupted digit string.

Key characteristics

Advantages

  • simple and intuitive;
  • easy to implement, debug, and interpret;
  • useful baseline for many classical NLP tasks;
  • often effective for topic/content signals where exact word order is less critical.

Sparsity

Vocabulary can contain thousands or millions of terms, while any one document uses only a small subset. Most vector entries are zero, so BoW vectors are sparse. Sparsity permits specialized storage, but a very large vocabulary is still memory- and computation-intensive at scale.

Equal treatment and semantic limitations

Plain BoW gives no inherent understanding of relations among terms. It does not know that two words are synonyms or antonyms. Every vocabulary coordinate is independent unless later modeling adds structure.

Order insensitivity

John is quicker than Mary.
Mary is quicker than John.

These sentences contain the same terms/counts, so they receive identical BoW vectors despite opposite relational meaning.

Other examples:

  • dog bites man vs man bites dog;
  • good vs not good if not is removed;
  • excellent and great remain separate despite similar meaning;
  • hot and cold remain separate without knowing they contrast.

BoW can support small/basic summarization or classification baselines, but it cannot truly understand a paragraph’s contextual meaning. More advanced embeddings/contextual language models address some of these issues, beyond this week’s scope.

5. Term Frequency (TF)

TF measures how often term \(t\) occurs in document \(d\).

Raw TF

\[ \operatorname{TF}_{raw}(t,d)=c(t,d) \]

Length-normalized TF

Because longer documents naturally contain more words, the lecture normalizes by total terms:

\[ \operatorname{TF}(t,d)= \frac{c(t,d)}{\sum_{w}c(w,d)} =\frac{\text{count of }t\text{ in }d} {\text{total terms in }d} \]

Example: cat appears 3 times in a 100-word document:

\[ \operatorname{TF}(cat,d)=\frac3{100}=0.03 \]

TF captures within-document prominence, but alone overvalues common corpus-wide words.

Log-scaled TF

For large counts, a common weighting is:

\[ \operatorname{TF}_{log}(t,d)= \begin{cases} 1+\log(c(t,d)),&c(t,d)>0\\ 0,&c(t,d)=0 \end{cases} \]

The piecewise zero case matters: \(\log0\) is undefined. One should not blindly compute \(1+\log0\). Log scaling reflects diminishing returns: the difference between 1 and 10 occurrences matters more than between 1,001 and 1,010.

6. Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

Definition

Let:

  • \(N\): total number of documents in the corpus;
  • \(df(t)\): number of documents containing term \(t\) at least once.

The standard course formula is:

\[ \operatorname{IDF}(t)=\log\left(\frac{N}{df(t)}\right) \]

IDF is defined corpus-wide for a term, whereas TF is for a term–document pair.

Interpretation:

  • term in nearly every document: low IDF and low discriminative value;
  • rare term: high IDF and high discriminative value;
  • term in all \(N\) documents: \(\log(N/N)=0\).

IDF downweights common words and upweights rarer, potentially meaningful words. Document frequency counts documents containing a term, not the total number of occurrences across all documents.

Cat example

If the corpus contains \(10{,}000{,}000\) documents and cat occurs in \(1{,}000\) documents:

\[ \operatorname{IDF}(cat)= \log_{10}\left(\frac{10{,}000{,}000}{1{,}000}\right) =\log_{10}(10{,}000)=4 \]

The raw ratio is huge, so the logarithm compresses the scale. Base 10, base 2, or natural log changes numerical scale but normally not rank if used consistently. The lecture often uses base 10; the numerical retrieval example uses base 2.

Common formula error

Do not invert the ratio as \(\log(df/N)\). That would make rare terms negative and reverse the intended weighting. Use \(N/df(t)\), or a documented smoothed implementation.

7. TF–IDF

TF–IDF quantifies how important term \(t\) is to document \(d\) relative to a corpus:

\[ w_{t,d}=\operatorname{TF}(t,d)\operatorname{IDF}(t) \]

For the cat example:

\[ TF\text{–}IDF=0.03\times4=0.12 \]

One weighted variant presented is:

\[ w_{t,d}=left(1+\log TF_{t,d}\right) \log\left(\frac N{df(t)}\right) \]

with zero handling as described above. Different software may use raw/normalized/sublinear TF, smoothed IDF, or add constants. State the chosen convention.

Live practical analogy

In an AI textbook, backpropagation may occur frequently within the deep-learning chapter (high TF there) but infrequently across all chapters (high IDF relative to the full book/corpus). It therefore receives a high TF–IDF weight in the deep-learning chapter. A generic term repeated throughout the book would receive lower IDF.

What the score means

  • high TF + high IDF → characteristic/discriminative for this document;
  • high TF + low IDF → frequent but generic across corpus;
  • zero TF → zero TF–IDF in that document;
  • unseen query term outside the fitted vocabulary has no coordinate/weight in the current vector space.

\[ [157,4,232,0,57,2,2] \]

\[ [0,0,1,0,0,5,1] \]

The exact term spellings/order should be taken from the question table; the conceptual point is that each document becomes a fixed-length count/weight vector.

Geometry

If the vocabulary contains \(|V|\) terms, the vector space has \(|V|\) dimensions. Each axis corresponds to one term, and each document is a point/vector from the origin whose coordinates are counts or TF–IDF weights.

For a three-term vocabulary—e.g., Amrita, Vidyapeetham, University—a document with coordinates such as \([1,5,6]\) is a point in 3-D; the line from the origin to it is its document vector. Real corpora can contain lakhs of documents and hundreds of thousands/millions of terms, creating high-dimensional sparse spaces.

9. Query representation and retrieval

Query as a document

To retrieve documents for query \(q\):

  1. preprocess the query exactly like corpus documents;
  2. treat it as another short document;
  3. use the corpus vocabulary and global corpus IDF values;
  4. compute its TF/TF–IDF coordinates;
  5. assign zero to vocabulary terms absent from the query;
  6. compare the query vector to each document vector;
  7. rank documents from highest relevance score to lowest.

A query and documents must live in the same coordinate space. Do not build an unrelated query vocabulary or recompute IDF from the query alone.

Weighted and unweighted queries

The query may use binary presence, raw/normalized TF, or TF–IDF. Repeated query terms can receive higher TF depending on the adopted convention. Terms not shared with a document contribute zero to their dot product.

10. Why Euclidean distance is problematic for document length

Suppose:

D  = “hello how are you”
D' = “hello how are you hello how are you”

If \(\mathbf d\) is the count vector for \(D\), then \(\mathbf d'=2\mathbf d\). They have the same term proportions/topic direction but different magnitudes.

Euclidean distance is:

\[ \|\mathbf d'-\mathbf d\|_2=\|2\mathbf d-\mathbf d\|_2=\|\mathbf d\|_2>0 \]

It treats repeated/longer text as distant even when content proportions are identical. Cosine similarity compares direction:

\[ \cos(\mathbf d,2\mathbf d)=1 \]

Thus cosine is generally preferred for length-insensitive directional similarity in BoW/TF–IDF retrieval.

Caveat: Euclidean distance is not universally invalid. On L2-normalized vectors,

\[ \|\hat a-\hat b\|_2^2=2-2\cos(a,b) \]

so Euclidean distance and cosine ranking are monotonically related. The lecture’s objection applies mainly to unnormalized text vectors.

11. L2 normalization and cosine similarity

L2 length and unit vector

\[ \|x\|_2=\sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2},qquad \hat x=\frac{x}{\|x\|_2} \]

L2 normalization changes length to 1 while retaining direction.

Cosine similarity

\[ \operatorname{cosim}(q,d)= \frac{q\cdot d}{\|q\|_2\|d\|_2} =\frac{\sum_iq_i d_i} {\sqrt{\sum_iq_i^2}\sqrt{\sum_i d_i^2}} \]

Equivalently:

\[ \operatorname{cosim}(q,d)=\hat q\cdot\hat d \]

For nonnegative count/TF–IDF vectors:

  • near 1: highly similar direction/content distribution;
  • near 0: little/no vocabulary overlap;
  • rank highest cosine first.

The transcript once says “rank in ascending order” but immediately states the highest similarity/most relevant document ranks first. The correct ranking is descending cosine similarity.

12. Numerical retrieval example

Documents and vocabulary

\[ D_1=\text{“New York Times”} \]

\[ D_2=\text{“New York Post”} \]

\[ D_3=\text{“Los Angeles Times”} \]

The alphabetized vocabulary is:

\[ [\text{angeles},\text{los},\text{new},\text{post},\text{times},\text{york}] \]

Raw count vectors are:

\[ TF(D_1)=[0,0,1,0,1,1] \]

\[ TF(D_2)=[0,0,1,1,0,1] \]

\[ TF(D_3)=[1,1,0,0,1,0] \]

Counts are only 0/1, so the instructor omits TF length normalization/log weighting in documents. IDF uses base 2 for illustration, with \(N=3\):

\[ idf(angeles)=idf(los)=idf(post)=\log_2(3/1)\approx1.584 \]

\[ idf(new)=idf(times)=idf(york)=\log_2(3/2)\approx0.584 \]

Therefore:

\[ v(D_1)=[0,0,0.584,0,0.584,0.584] \]

\[ v(D_2)=[0,0,0.584,1.584,0,0.584] \]

\[ v(D_3)=[1.584,1.584,0,0,0.584,0] \]

The corresponding correct L2 lengths from these displayed vectors are approximately:

\[ \|D_1\|=\sqrt{3(0.584)^2}\approx1.011 \]

\[ \|D_2\|=\sqrt{2(0.584)^2+(1.584)^2}\approx1.786 \]

\[ \|D_3\|=\sqrt{2(1.584)^2+(0.584)^2}\approx2.316 \]

Query and course result

The transcript names the query “new times” but gives a query vector resembling:

\[ q=[0,0,0.584,0,0.292,0] \]

and reports lengths/similarities including:

  • course-stated \(\cos(D_1,q)=0.776\);
  • course-stated \(\cos(D_2,q)=0.292\);
  • final ranking \(D_1,D_2,D_3\).

The intended conclusion is sound: New York Times shares both query terms, New York Post shares only new, and Los Angeles Times shares only times; \(D_1\) ranks first.

Accuracy/caveat: internally inconsistent arithmetic

The transcribed calculation mixes conventions and contains conflicting numbers:

  • a two-token query new times normally has normalized TF \(1/2\) for both terms, giving weights \(0.292,0.292\), not \(0.584,0.292\);
  • if raw TF is used, both weights are \(0.584\);
  • the query norm stated as 1.011 does not match either of those query vectors;
  • one denominator reference gives \(D_1\)’s length as 0.652 even though the earlier correct value is 1.011;
  • the stated 0.776 and 0.292 do not consistently follow one displayed vector convention.

For an exam, follow the formula/convention explicitly specified in the question and recompute rather than memorizing these inconsistent intermediate values. Under a consistent normalized-query convention \(q=[0,0,0.292,0,0.292,0]\):

\[ \cos(D_1,q)=\frac{0.584(0.292)+0.584(0.292)}{1.011\sqrt{2(0.292)^2}}\approx0.816 \]

\[ \cos(D_2,q)\approx0.231,qquad \cos(D_3,q)\approx0.178 \]

The ranking remains:

\[ D_1>D_2>D_3 \]

13. EduLearn case study: preprocessing student feedback

Business problem

EduLearn receives thousands of open-ended student comments and wants to classify them automatically. Miguel, a data analyst, proposes comparing preprocessing pipelines on a representative subset before committing to one.

Options

Option Pipeline Strength Risk/cost
A Tokenization only Simplest and fastest preprocessing Largest vocabulary, redundant variants, more memory/features
B Tokenization + stemming Usually smallest vocabulary, lower memory and computation Invalid/ambiguous roots; possible accuracy loss
C Tokenization + lemmatization Valid canonical forms and better semantic consistency More preprocessing time/resources; vocabulary often larger than stemming

Miguel’s experiment

Test all pipelines on a small but representative subset and compare:

  • classification accuracy (and suitable precision, recall, F1 where classes are imbalanced);
  • vocabulary size/vector dimensionality;
  • sparsity and memory use;
  • preprocessing and model-training time;
  • inference latency;
  • interpretability and error patterns.

Do not select a pipeline from theory alone. A small accuracy decrease—illustratively 2%—may be acceptable if memory/computation improves substantially and overall performance remains comparable, depending on business priorities. Conversely, a high-stakes classification task may justify lemmatization’s added cost.

Core strategic lesson

The stemming-versus-lemmatization debate improves morphological consolidation, memory, and feature quality; it does not repair BoW’s deeper lack of order/context/semantics. Evaluate the complete downstream task.

14. Live case: plagiarism detection / Turnitin illustration

The live session used Turnitin as a conceptual large-scale document-similarity case:

  1. tokenize and clean a submitted document;
  2. convert it and source documents to TF–IDF vectors;
  3. compute cosine similarity against many source vectors;
  4. identify/flag highly similar passages/sources;
  5. generate a report containing similarity information for an instructor/checker.

The class scenario used cosine similarity \(\ge0.7\) as an illustrative flag threshold and contrasted cosine’s angle-based comparison with raw Euclidean distance. It also discussed reports for plagiarism and AI-generated content.

Important caveats

  • The exact proprietary Turnitin production architecture, training corpus, thresholds, and AI-detection methods were not established from a primary technical source in the transcript. Treat TF–IDF + cosine and the 0.7 threshold as the instructor’s explanatory model/case, not a guaranteed description of every product component.
  • A high text similarity is evidence for review, not automatic proof of plagiarism. Quotations, references, standard phrasing, templates, and legitimate overlap require human interpretation.
  • AI-authorship detection is a different problem from source matching; cosine overlap alone cannot reliably prove that text was generated by an LLM.
  • At scale, systems commonly operate at passage/segment level and use indexing to avoid comparing every document pair naively.

Practical use case: a university can use similarity scores to prioritize submissions for manual review while providing matched sources and highlighted passages, preserving a human decision stage.

16. Exam-focused traps and recall

Common traps

  • TF counts occurrences within one document; DF counts documents containing the term.
  • IDF is \(\log(N/df)\), not \(\log(df/N)\).
  • A term occurring in all documents has standard unsmoothed IDF 0.
  • BoW ignores order; TF–IDF changes weights but still ignores order/context.
  • Sparse does not mean “small vocabulary”; it means most coordinates in each vector are zero.
  • Stemming is not always better or more accurate than lemmatization; it is usually faster/more aggressive.
  • Lemmatization does not always shrink vocabulary more than stemming.
  • Query IDF comes from the corpus, not the query itself.
  • Query and documents must share vocabulary coordinates and preprocessing.
  • Normalize both vectors before interpreting their dot product as cosine.
  • Rank retrieval results by descending cosine.
  • Raw Euclidean distance is sensitive to document length; after L2 normalization it is closely related to cosine.
  • Similarity does not itself prove plagiarism or causality/authorship.

Short-answer prompts

  1. Why must NLP vectorize text? ML algorithms require numerical, structured inputs.
  2. Why is BoW called a bag? It retains terms/counts but discards order, grammar, and context.
  3. Why are BoW vectors sparse? Each document uses only a small subset of the corpus vocabulary.
  4. What does TF measure? Local prominence of a term in one document.
  5. What does IDF measure? Corpus rarity/discriminative power.
  6. Why combine TF and IDF? Reward terms frequent locally but uncommon globally.
  7. Why use a logarithm? Compress very large counts/ratios and model diminishing returns.
  8. Why treat a query as a document? It allows the same vocabulary, weighting, and vector comparison.
  9. Why use cosine for text? It compares direction/content proportions with less sensitivity to length.
  10. What remains unsolved after stemming/lemmatization? Word order and contextual/semantic relations.
  11. Why does Miguel test several pipelines? The best accuracy–memory–compute trade-off is data/task dependent.
  12. What is the correct conclusion of the New York example? New York Times ranks first for new times, followed by New York Post, then Los Angeles Times under the course setup.

Final one-minute revision

  • NLP bridges unstructured human language and numerical ML features.
  • Clean → tokenize → optionally remove stop words → stem/lemmatize → build vocabulary → vectorize.
  • BoW is simple/interpretable but sparse, order-insensitive, and semantically weak.
  • \(TF=c/|d|\); \(IDF=\log(N/df)\); TF–IDF is their product.
  • Vocabulary terms are vector-space axes; documents and queries are vectors in the same space.
  • L2-normalize and use cosine; highest cosine ranks first.
  • The “cat” example gives TF 0.03, base-10 IDF 4, TF–IDF 0.12.
  • Shakespeare and literary examples show count vectors and feature-dependent similarity.
  • New York retrieval: intended order is D1, D2, D3, but recompute rather than memorize inconsistent transcript decimals.
  • EduLearn: tokenization is cheapest, stemming is compact but risky, lemmatization is cleaner but costlier; choose through validation.
  • The live plagiarism case illustrates TF–IDF/cosine screening, but high similarity still requires contextual human review.
