Exam scope and learning outcomes

Week 13 represents social relationships as graphs and uses Laplacian eigenstructure to measure connectivity and discover communities. You should be able to:

  • define social networks, sites, actors, ties, affiliations, and content/network layers;
  • explain behavioral user types, strong ties, Ramsey’s theorem, Dunbar-style relationship limits, and Metcalfe’s law;
  • calculate degree, betweenness, and closeness centrality;
  • construct adjacency, degree, and combinatorial Laplacian matrices;
  • state Laplacian spectral properties and infer connected components;
  • derive/interprete the Rayleigh quotient, Fiedler value, and Fiedler vector;
  • carry out two-way and multiway spectral clustering;
  • reproduce the NetworkX/NumPy/scikit-learn workflow;
  • apply the methods critically to the VertexTech collaboration case.

1. Social networks

1.1 Definition

A social network describes a social structure whose actors may be individuals, households, teams, organizations, states, or other entities. Actors are connected through one or more forms of interdependence/familiarity:

  • friendship or kinship;
  • professional relationships or collaboration;
  • financial exchange;
  • likes/dislikes, beliefs/non-beliefs;
  • knowledge, prestige, trust, or support;
  • organizational/team affiliation.

In graph language:

  • actor/entity → node/vertex;
  • relationship/tie → edge/link;
  • attributes may describe nodes or edges;
  • the analytical goal is to study patterns of relationships, influence, flow, communities, and vulnerabilities.

An affiliation connects different entity types, e.g. a person works for IBM, belongs to ACM, or plays on a football team. Such data can be modeled directly as bipartite networks or projected into person–person connections.

1.2 Social-network sites (SNS)

SNS let users present profiles, articulate relationships, and establish/maintain/traverse connections. Platforms are oriented toward different goals:

  • Facebook: personal/symmetric friendship and originally student networking;
  • LinkedIn: professional connection/following, including organization pages;
  • X/Twitter: directed follow network plus a separate content layer of posts/tweets;
  • ResearchGate: research interests and professional exchange;
  • Friendster: originally romantic connections;
  • Myspace, Orkut, Cyworld: personal networking;
  • Last.fm: music; Flixster: movies; Flickr: photos; X/Twitter: microblogging.

Network layer vs content layer: “who follows/connects to whom” is network structure; posts, messages, images, comments, and deletions are content. In the quiz’s protected-account example, approving a follow request changes the network layer; deleting an old post changes content.

1.3 Relationship direction

  • Facebook friendship is generally symmetric/undirected: if A is B’s friend, B is A’s friend.
  • X/Instagram/LinkedIn following can be directed/asymmetric: A may follow B without B following A.
  • Mutual friends/follow suggestions exploit triadic likelihood: A–B and B–C make A–C more plausible, but do not guarantee it.

1.4 Strong and weak ties

Strong ties involve frequent interaction, trust, emotional closeness, and support—e.g. close friends/family. Weak ties are acquaintances; though less intimate, they can bridge communities and expose a person to novel information/opportunities.

2. Social-network intuitions and user behavior

2.1 Ramsey theorem

The classical result (R(3,3)=6): among any six people there must be either:

  • three pairwise mutual acquaintances, or
  • three pairwise mutual strangers.

Graph proof intuition: color every edge of (K_6) according to acquaintance/stranger. From any person, at least three of the five incident edges share a color; the triangle among those vertices forces a monochromatic triangle of one type.

2.2 Dunbar-style relationship limitation

The lectures emphasize that people may have thousands of acquaintances but actively maintain only roughly 10–20 close relationships. The live session calls this Dunbar’s number/intuition and describes strong versus loose connections. Treat 10–20 as the course’s close-tie illustration; broader Dunbar-number literature often uses other tier sizes.

2.3 Metcalfe’s law

The value of a network grows approximately with the square of participants:

\[ V\propto n^2, \]

because potential pairwise connections grow as \(\binom n2\). The transcript says “Medcore/Medca”; the standard name is Metcalfe’s law. It is a heuristic, not a guarantee that every added user contributes equal value.

2.4 Behavioral user classification

Type Characteristic behavior Practical example
Alpha socializers Short bursts of activity to flirt, meet people, get help, or be entertained Briefly contacts an expert for help, then tie weakens
Attention seekers Seek comments/likes/visibility; post photos/videos/messages Frequent Instagram posting for reactions
Followers Mainly observe what others do; little original contribution Follows experts to acquire information
Faithfuls Rekindle/maintain longstanding school/university/family relationships Keeps an old friendship despite infrequent contact
Functionals Use a site single-mindedly for a practical purpose LinkedIn networking or Facebook business promotion

Individuals can display several types depending on the relationship/platform.

The lecture cited an Ofcom-style age comparison: Instagram/Snapchat/TikTok skewed younger; Facebook much older; X/Twitter more broadly distributed. Specific percentages (e.g. Instagram 30% age 16–24 and 4% age 65+, Facebook 12% young and 83% older) were presented as illustrative/older survey values and should not be treated as current timeless facts.

3. Centrality measures

3.1 Degree centrality

Counts direct neighbors:

\[ C_D(v)=\deg(v), \qquad C_D^{norm}(v)=\frac{\deg(v)}{n-1}. \]

High degree means locally well-connected/popular, but not necessarily strategically positioned across communities.

3.2 Betweenness centrality (flow centrality)

\[ C_B(v)=\sum_{\substack{s\ne v\ne t}} \frac{\sigma_{st}(v)}{\sigma_{st}}, \]

where \(\sigma_{st}\) is the number of shortest paths from (s) to (t), and \(\sigma_{st}(v)\) those passing through (v).

A bridge node between communities may have low degree but high betweenness, acting as broker, gatekeeper, bottleneck, or single point of failure. It is important in transportation/information-flow networks.

3.3 Closeness centrality

\[ C_C(v)=\frac{n-1}{\sum_{u\ne v}d(v,u)} \]

for a connected graph. It measures how quickly a node can reach all others via shortest paths. A node may have few direct connections yet high closeness if strategically near the whole network.

Distinction: degree = local reach; betweenness = lies between others’ routes; closeness = globally short average distance. Eigenvector centrality, mentioned in the quiz comparison, rewards links to influential nodes but was not a main lecture topic.

4. Graph matrices

4.1 Adjacency matrix

For an unweighted undirected graph with (n) nodes:

\[ A_{ij}=\begin{cases} 1,&\{i,j\}\in E,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

Properties for a simple undirected graph:

  • (A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n});
  • symmetric: (A=A^T);
  • diagonal zero if there are no self-loops;
  • each undirected edge creates two symmetric ones.

Weighted graphs place connection weights rather than 1.

4.2 Degree matrix

\[ d_i=\sum_j A_{ij}, \qquad D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n). \]

Only diagonal entries are nonzero.

4.3 Combinatorial graph Laplacian

\[ \boxed{L=D-A.} \]

Thus:

\[ L_{ij}=\begin{cases} d_i,&i=j,\\ -1,&i\ne j\text{ and }\{i,j\}\in E,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

for an unweighted graph. Each row sums to zero.

Three-node path (1-2-3):

\[ A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&1\\0&1&0\end{bmatrix}, D=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}, L=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&1\end{bmatrix}. \]

4.4 Block structure and unions

For disjoint components (G,H), after ordering vertices by component:

\[ L_{G\cup H}= \begin{bmatrix}L_G&0\\0&L_H\end{bmatrix}. \]

An isolated vertex yields an all-zero row and column. Adding a bridge between components creates corresponding symmetric (-1) entries and increases the two incident degrees. For graphs on the same vertex set, Laplacians of weighted edge unions add when edge weights add.

5. Laplacian spectrum

5.1 Terminology

The spectrum is the multiset of eigenvalues (often studied together with their eigenvectors), ordered conventionally:

\[ 0=\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\cdots\le\lambda_n. \]

The transcript loosely defines spectrum as eigenvectors and sometimes prints descending software output. For spectral clustering, explicitly sort eigenvalues ascending and retain aligned eigenvectors.

5.2 Fundamental properties

For an undirected combinatorial Laplacian:

  • (L) is real symmetric and positive semidefinite;
  • all eigenvalues are real and nonnegative;
  • eigenvectors can be chosen real and orthonormal;
  • (L\mathbf1=0), so 0 is always an eigenvalue and the constant vector is a trivial eigenvector;
  • the multiplicity of eigenvalue 0 equals the number of connected components.

For two isomorphic disconnected components with component spectrum \(\{0,a,b\}\), the whole spectrum is \(\{0,0,a,a,b,b\}\). Repetition results from block structure/isomorphism; regularity is not what merges or determines zero multiplicity.

5.3 Examples

For the three-node path above:

\[ \lambda=\{0,1,3\}. \]

Possible eigenvectors:

\[ v_1\propto(1,1,1)^T, \quad v_2\propto(-1,0,1)^T, \quad v_3\propto(1,-2,1)^T. \]

For complete graph (K_4), the Laplacian has spectrum \(\{0,4,4,4\}\). The nonzero eigenvalue repeats three times. Numerical output such as \(-3\times10^{-17}\) should be treated as zero floating-point error.

Case inconsistency: the VertexTech text supplies \([0,0.5858,2.4142]\) for the displayed three-node path matrix. Direct calculation gives \([0,1,3]\). If asked “according to the supplied case list,” 0.5858 is its second value; if asked to compute from the matrix, use 1.

6. Rayleigh quotient and algebraic connectivity

6.1 Quadratic form

For undirected weighted graph:

\[ x^TLx=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}(x_i-x_j)^2\ge0. \]

It is small when connected nodes receive similar coordinates.

The Rayleigh quotient is:

\[ R(x)=\frac{x^TLx}{x^Tx}. \]

Unconstrained minimization returns 0 and a constant vector. To expose the next direction, exclude the nullspace:

\[ \boxed{\lambda_2=\min_{x\ne0,\ x^T\mathbf1=0}\frac{x^TLx}{x^Tx}.} \]

Equivalently impose (x^T\mathbf1=0) and (x^Tx=1). Orthogonality means \(\sum_i x_i=0\).

6.2 Fiedler value/vector

For a connected graph:

  • \(\lambda_2>0\) is the Fiedler value/algebraic connectivity;
  • an associated eigenvector (v_2) is a Fiedler vector.

Small positive \(\lambda_2\) suggests a weak bridge/bottleneck and natural division. Larger \(\lambda_2\) generally indicates stronger connectivity/resistance to fragmentation. Adding bridge edges tends to increase \(\lambda_2\); if two communities remain internally much denser, the Fiedler sign pattern may still separate them.

Caveats: the transcript says Fiedler-value multiplicity is always one; this is false for symmetric/highly structured graphs—\(\lambda_2\) can repeat. Also, on a disconnected graph several zeros exist; the “first nonzero eigenvalue” is not necessarily \(\lambda_2\). Algebraic connectivity is specifically the second-smallest eigenvalue, which is 0 when disconnected.

7. Spectral graph clustering

7.1 Two-way clustering

  1. Construct (L=D-A).
  2. Compute eigenpairs and sort eigenvalues ascending.
  3. Select (v_2), the eigenvector for \(\lambda_2\).
  4. Partition by sign/threshold:

\[ C_-=\{i:v_{2i}

Often (t=0), though a sweep over thresholds can better optimize cut weight, ratio cut, normalized cut, or conductance.

Eigenvector sign is arbitrary: (v_2) and (-v_2) give the same partition with labels swapped. A zero/near-zero coordinate needs a stated tie-break (lower cut, smaller cluster, workload, sweep threshold, or k-means).

7.2 Eight-node lecture examples

Two dense four-node groups are joined through edge (1-8). The Laplacian has two near-blocks with symmetric bridge (-1) entries. Reported:

\[ \lambda_2\approx0.354. \]

The Fiedler vector has negative components for nodes 1–4 and positive for nodes 5–8 (or globally reversed), with transition across bridge (1-8); cut there to produce two clusters.

Adding a second bridge (4-5) increases reported \(\lambda_2\) to about 0.764, reflecting stronger inter-group connectivity. The block tendency remains, but the cut is less pronounced.

7.3 Multiway spectral clustering

For (k>2), do not merely threshold “the next eigenvalue”—eigenvalues are scalars. Use several eigenvectors to embed node (i), e.g.:

\[ z_i=(v_{2i},v_{3i},\ldots,v_{(k+1)i}), \]

then run k-means or another clustering algorithm on \(z_i\). Some formulations use the first (k) eigenvectors including the trivial one; conventions depend on normalized/unnormalized Laplacian and implementation.

K-means minimizes within-cluster Euclidean variation in spectral space:

\[ \sum_{r=1}^{k}\sum_{z_i\in C_r}\|z_i-\mu_r\|^2, \]

not the original Rayleigh quotient directly. The eigenvectors first create a graph-aware geometry.

7.4 Normalized alternatives

For strongly unequal degrees, normalized Laplacians can be more meaningful:

\[ L_{sym}=I-D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}, \qquad L_{rw}=I-D^{-1}A. \]

Choice changes eigenvectors and clusters; document which formulation is used.

8. Python/NetworkX/scikit-learn demonstration

8.1 Core libraries and matrix workflow

  • networkx: create graphs, laplacian_matrix, laplacian_spectrum, fiedler_vector, algebraic_connectivity, karate-club graph;
  • NumPy linalg: eigenvalues/eigenvectors;
  • Matplotlib: plotting;
  • scikit-learn SpectralClustering: packaged multiway method.

For symmetric (L), prefer numpy.linalg.eigh (guarantees real ordered-compatible eigenpairs more naturally) over general eig.

G = nx.Graph([(1, 2), (2, 3)])
L = nx.laplacian_matrix(G).toarray()
evals, evecs = np.linalg.eigh(L)      # ascending for symmetric matrix
v2 = evecs[:, 1]
labels = (v2 >= 0).astype(int)

The path output is approximately eigenvalues 0, 1, 3; tiny values such as \(-5.1\times10^{-17}\) are numerical zero.

8.2 Direct Fiedler method

Create an 11×11 zero adjacency matrix, set symmetric edges within two groups and a thin bridge, convert via nx.from_numpy_array, obtain nx.fiedler_vector(G), and label 0 if component <0 else 1. The demonstration colors the two graph halves.

The same result can be obtained manually by sorting Laplacian eigenpairs and selecting the vector for the second-smallest eigenvalue. If manual and NetworkX signs are opposite, only cluster label names differ.

8.3 Karate Club demo

  1. Load nx.karate_club_graph().
  2. Extract ground-truth club membership (Mr. Hi versus the other club).
  3. Obtain adjacency matrix as NumPy array.
  4. Run SpectralClustering(n_clusters=2, affinity='precomputed', ...).
  5. Fit the adjacency affinity, inspect labels, and align/invert labels only for comparison because cluster names are arbitrary.
  6. Plot nodes colored by predicted labels.

The lecture reported roughly 88% agreement with ground truth. This is label agreement for one run/setting, not a universal accuracy guarantee.

9. VertexTech collaboration-network case

9.1 Scenario

VertexTech models developers as nodes and collaboration as edges (possibly weighted by review/message/commit frequency). Alice wants algebraic connectivity; Bruno focuses on Rayleigh minimization; Carla needs workable team load and module cohesion.

9.2 Options

  • Two-way Fiedler cut: most direct relaxed \(\lambda_2\) partition; interpretable and suitable if one dominant bottleneck exists, but does not guarantee balanced team size/workload.
  • Three-way spectral clustering: use several smallest relevant eigenvectors, embed developers, run (k=3) k-means; can isolate a graphics subgroup but may create small/uneven teams and depends on (k)/initialization.
  • Connectivity diagnosis only: use \(\lambda_2\) and (v_2) to locate weak interfaces and improve collaboration rather than force reorganization.

9.3 Recommended goal-aware pipeline

  1. Build a reliable weighted, possibly time-aware graph.
  2. Check directionality, connected components, degree variation, and data quality.
  3. Choose combinatorial or normalized Laplacian.
  4. Inspect smallest eigenvalues/eigengaps and partition stability.
  5. Generate two-cluster Fiedler and three-cluster spectral-embedding candidates.
  6. Evaluate cut weight/conductance, internal density, team size, workload variance, skill coverage, module ownership, and cross-team dependencies.
  7. Use a workload-aware threshold/constrained cut if two balanced review teams are required.
  8. Review results with developers; do not treat an eigenvector as an automatic personnel decision.

Conceptually combine objectives:

\[ \text{cut cost}+\alpha(\text{workload imbalance})+\beta(\text{skill-coverage penalty}). \]

Spectral evidence reveals network structure; organizational judgment decides the team design.

10. Exam-focused revision

Essential formulas

\[ A_{ij}=1\text{ if edge};\quad D_{ii}=\sum_jA_{ij};\quad L=D-A \]

\[ x^TLx=\sum_{\{i,j\}\in E}w_{ij}(x_i-x_j)^2 \]

\[ \lambda_2=\min_{x\ne0,\ x^T\mathbf1=0}\frac{x^TLx}{x^Tx} \]

\[ \#\{\lambda_i=0\}=\#\text{ connected components} \]

\[ C_B(v)=\sum_{s\ne v\ne t}\frac{\sigma_{st}(v)}{\sigma_{st}}, \qquad C_C(v)=\frac{n-1}{\sum_{u\ne v}d(v,u)} \]

One-line recall

  • Nodes are actors; edges are social ties; posts are content rather than network structure.
  • (R(3,3)=6): three mutual acquaintances or three mutual strangers.
  • Metcalfe’s heuristic says network value scales approximately as (n^2).
  • Degree counts neighbors; betweenness finds bridges; closeness measures rapid reach.
  • Laplacian diagonal = degree; edge off-diagonal = (-1); nonedge = 0.
  • A connected graph has one zero eigenvalue; (c) components give (c) zeros.
  • \(\lambda_2\) is algebraic connectivity; (v_2) is a Fiedler vector.
  • More bridges generally increase \(\lambda_2\).
  • Sign of an eigenvector can reverse globally without changing clusters.
  • Use multiple eigenvectors plus k-means for (k>2).

Likely exam prompts

  1. Build (A,D,L) from a small graph and state symmetry/row-sum properties.
  2. Infer components from zero-eigenvalue multiplicity/block-diagonal Laplacian.
  3. Derive \(\lambda_2\) using Rayleigh quotient and explain the orthogonality constraint.
  4. Partition an eight-node graph by sorting (v_2) components and finding a sign/threshold cut.
  5. Explain effect of another bridge: stronger connectivity, generally larger \(\lambda_2\), perhaps same sign groups if community structure remains.
  6. Match manual and NetworkX results: choose eigenvector for second-smallest eigenvalue; ignore global sign swap.
  7. Advise VertexTech: compare two-way cut and multi-eigenvector k-means, then add workload/skill constraints.

Common traps

  • Spectrum means eigenvalues, not merely eigenvectors.
  • Do not select the constant \(\lambda_1=0\) eigenvector for clustering.
  • “First nonzero eigenvalue” is safe only after handling disconnected graphs; Fiedler value is \(\lambda_2\).
  • Fiedler eigenvalue need not be simple.
  • A sign cut does not guarantee balanced clusters or globally optimal discrete cut.
  • Zero Fiedler components need an assignment rule.
  • Do not interpret (-1) in (L) as hostility; it encodes an edge.
  • The displayed path matrix has eigenvalues 0,1,3—not the inconsistent case list.
