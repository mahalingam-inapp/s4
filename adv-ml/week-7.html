Exam scope and learning outcomes

This week develops association analysis from definitions through scalable frequent-itemset algorithms. You should be able to:

  • distinguish association analysis from association-rule mining;
  • calculate support count, support, and confidence;
  • separate frequent-itemset generation from rule generation;
  • explain why brute force is expensive;
  • state and apply the Apriori/anti-monotone principle;
  • perform candidate join, prune, database-scan, and termination steps;
  • generate rules from a frequent itemset and test minimum confidence;
  • explain how PCY uses hashing and a bitmap to reduce second-pass candidate pairs;
  • translate statistically strong rules into practical cross-selling decisions.

1. Association analysis and association-rule mining

1.1 Definitions

Association analysis is the general task of finding frequent patterns among objects/items in transaction databases, relational databases, or other information repositories. The discovered co-occurrence patterns are associations.

Association-rule mining (ARM) is the predictive rule-discovery task: given transactions, find rules that predict the occurrence of one item/itemset based on other items in the same transaction.

Common applications:

  • market-basket/basket-data analysis;
  • cross-marketing and product bundling;
  • catalogue design and shelf/layout planning;
  • loss-leader analysis;
  • web/clickstream or service-usage patterns;
  • preprocessing or pattern discovery for clustering and classification.

Lecture illustration: customers buying automotive accessories/tyres may also be likely to purchase vehicle service. A retailer could offer a service coupon with the product.

1.2 Market-basket data

A transaction is the collection of items purchased by a customer in one visit/order. Market-basket analysis discovers relationships between sets of items across many transactions—not merely within one basket.

Let the item universe be (I={i_1,i_2,\ldots,i_d}) and the transaction database (D={T_1,\ldots,T_N}), with each (T_t\subseteq I).

1.3 Itemsets and support

  • Itemset: collection of one or more items, e.g. \(\{milk,bread,diaper\}\).
  • k-itemset: itemset containing exactly (k) items; \(\{bread,milk\}\) is a 2-itemset.
  • Support count/frequency:

\[ \sigma(X)=|\{T\in D:X\subseteq T\}|. \]

  • Support: fraction of all transactions containing (X):

\[ s(X)=\frac{\sigma(X)}{N}. \]

  • Frequent itemset: (X) such that (s(X)\ge\text{minsup}), equivalently \(\sigma(X)\ge\text{minimum support count}\).

If \(\{milk,bread,diaper\}\) occurs in 2 of 5 transactions, its support count is 2 and support is (2/5=0.40).

1.4 Association rules

An association rule is an implication

\[ X\rightarrow Y, \]

where (X,Y\subseteq I), (X\cap Y=\varnothing), and both are nonempty. (X) is the antecedent and (Y) the consequent. The arrow means association/predictive implication, not logical certainty or causation.

For rule (X\rightarrow Y):

\[ \operatorname{support}(X\rightarrow Y)=s(X\cup Y)=\frac{\sigma(X\cup Y)}{N} \]

\[ \operatorname{confidence}(X\rightarrow Y) =P(Y\mid X) =\frac{s(X\cup Y)}{s(X)} =\frac{\sigma(X\cup Y)}{\sigma(X)}. \]

Support measures how widespread the complete combination is. Confidence measures how often (Y) occurs among transactions already containing (X).

Milk–diaper–beer example (5 transactions): \(\{milk,diaper,beer\}\) occurs twice, while \(\{milk,diaper\}\) occurs three times.

\[ s(\{milk,diaper\}\rightarrow\{beer\})=2/5=0.40 \]

\[ c(\{milk,diaper\}\rightarrow\{beer\})=2/3\approx0.67. \]

Changing the direction changes confidence but not support of the union:

  • \(\{milk,beer\}\rightarrow\{diaper\}\): confidence (2/2=1.00);
  • \(\{diaper\}\rightarrow\{milk,beer\}\): confidence (2/4=0.50).

All partitions of the same union \(\{milk,diaper,beer\}\) have support 0.40, but different antecedent frequencies produce different confidence.

Caveat: high confidence does not prove that (X) causes (Y), and it may reflect a generally popular consequent. This week’s formal thresholds are support and confidence; in practice lift and business context are often added.

2. The association-mining task

Given minimum support and confidence thresholds, find every rule satisfying:

\[ s(X\cup Y)\ge\text{minsup} \quad\text{and}\quad c(X\rightarrow Y)\ge\text{minconf}. \]

The efficient workflow has two phases:

  1. Frequent-itemset generation: use minimum support to find all frequent itemsets.
  2. Rule generation: partition each frequent itemset into antecedent/consequent and retain rules meeting minimum confidence.

This division matters: support is a property of an itemset/union; confidence is directional and belongs to a rule. The transcript occasionally says frequent-itemset generation uses both support and confidence, but the later algorithm and quiz correctly separate responsibilities: support first, confidence second.

3. Brute-force frequent-itemset mining

3.1 Exhaustive method

  1. Enumerate every possible itemset/rule.
  2. Scan transactions and calculate support.
  3. Retain frequent itemsets.
  4. Generate every valid rule partition.
  5. Calculate confidence and prune weak rules.

For (d) unique items there are (2^d) subsets including the empty set. With five items (A,B,C,D,E), the lattice contains:

  • 1 empty set;
  • 5 singleton itemsets;
  • \(\binom52=10\) pairs;
  • \(\binom53=10\) triples;
  • \(\binom54=5\) four-itemsets;
  • 1 five-itemset;
  • total (1+5+10+10+5+1=32=2^5).

3.2 Number of possible rules

For each item, its status can be antecedent, consequent, or absent: (3^d) assignments. Remove assignments with empty antecedent or empty consequent, correcting for the both-empty overlap:

\[ \boxed{3^d-2^{d+1}+1} \]

possible nontrivial association rules. For (d=6):

\[ 3^6-2^7+1=729-128+1=602. \]

The lecture’s spoken formula omitted one factor of 2 but its stated answer 602 corresponds to the correct expression above.

3.3 Cost

Let:

  • (N): number of transactions;
  • (M): number of candidate itemsets (up to approximately (2^d));
  • (W): average/maximum transaction width or cost of matching a candidate to a transaction.

Matching every transaction against every candidate costs approximately:

\[ O(NMW). \]

The exponential candidate count makes brute force impractical. Three improvement directions introduced were:

  1. reduce candidates through pruning;
  2. reduce transactions considered as itemset size grows/remove irrelevant transactions;
  3. reduce comparisons using efficient data structures.

Apriori primarily reduces candidates; PCY further reduces pair candidates using hashing.

4. Apriori principle

4.1 Anti-monotone property of support

If (X\subseteq Y), every transaction containing (Y) also contains (X), so:

\[ \sigma(X)\ge\sigma(Y),\qquad s(X)\ge s(Y). \]

Therefore:

  • if an itemset is frequent, all its subsets are frequent;
  • contrapositively, if an itemset is infrequent, all its supersets are infrequent.

This is the Apriori principle or anti-monotone/downward-closure property of support.

If \(\{A,B\}\) is infrequent, immediately prune \(\{A,B,C\},\{A,B,D\},\{A,B,E\},\ldots\). No larger set containing (AB) can recover support.

Important correction: frequent subsets are necessary but not sufficient for a superset to be frequent. The transcript sometimes loosely suggests a frequent set’s supersets are likely/frequent. They are only candidates and must still be support-counted.

4.2 Example of pruning

For singleton counts bread 4, pork 2, milk 4, beer 3, diaper 4, eggs 1 with minimum support count 3:

  • retain bread, milk, beer, diaper;
  • prune pork and eggs and all supersets containing them.

At the pair level, prune pairs such as bread–beer or milk–beer if their support count is 2 (<3), and never build triples containing those infrequent pairs. The surviving triple bread–milk–diaper has support count 3. The lecture reported that support-based pruning reduced the candidate workload substantially (by 13 in the illustrated lattice).

5. Apriori algorithm

5.1 Notation and key operations

  • (C_k): candidate (k)-itemsets.
  • (L_k): frequent (k)-itemsets satisfying minimum support.
  • Join: generate (C_k) by self-joining compatible members of (L_{k-1}), written (L_{k-1}\Join L_{k-1}).
  • Prune: remove candidate (c\in C_k) if any ((k-1))-subset of (c) is not in (L_{k-1}).

5.2 Iterative procedure

  1. Scan the database to produce frequent singletons (L_1).
  2. Set (k=2).
  3. Join (L_{k-1}) with itself to form (C_k).
  4. Apply subset pruning before support counting.
  5. Scan the database and count support for surviving candidates.
  6. Form (L_k={c\in C_k:\sigma(c)\ge\text{minsup count}}).
  7. If (L_k\ne\varnothing), increment (k) and repeat.
  8. Stop when no candidates/frequent sets remain.
  9. Generate high-confidence rules from all frequent itemsets.

Apriori requires multiple database passes but compares far fewer candidates than exhaustive search.

6. Complete Apriori numerical example

The lecture uses 9 transactions (T_{100})–(T_{900}) (spoken as 101–109 in places), five items (I1\ldots I5), minimum support count 2 (support (2/9\approx22.2\%)), and minimum confidence 70%.

6.1 First pass: (C_1\rightarrow L_1)

Item Support count
I1 6
I2 7
I3 6
I4 2
I5 2

All meet count 2:

\[ L_1=\{I1,I2,I3,I4,I5\}. \]

6.2 Pair generation: (C_2\rightarrow L_2)

Self-join (L_1) to generate all pairs, scan, and remove pairs with count below 2. The frequent pairs are:

\[ L_2=\{I1I2,I1I3,I1I5,I2I3,I2I4,I2I5\}. \]

Illustrative counts include (I1I2=4), (I1I3=4), (I1I4=1), and (I1I5=2). Zero/one-count pairs such as (I3I4,I3I5,I4I5) are excluded.

6.3 Triple generation: (C_3\rightarrow L_3)

Join compatible pairs and apply Apriori pruning:

  • Keep (I1I2I3) because its pair subsets (I1I2,I1I3,I2I3\in L_2).
  • Keep (I1I2I5) because (I1I2,I1I5,I2I5\in L_2).
  • Prune (I2I3I5) because subset (I3I5\notin L_2).

After counting:

Triple Count
\(I1I2I3\) 2
\(I1I2I5\) 2

Thus:

\[ L_3=\{I1I2I3,I1I2I5\}. \]

6.4 Four-item attempt and termination

Joining the two triples yields candidate (I1I2I3I5), but its subset (I2I3I5\notin L_3). Prune it without a database count. Therefore (C_4=\varnothing), and Apriori stops: no larger frequent itemset can exist.

6.5 Rule generation from \(L=\{I1,I2,I5\}\)

For every nonempty proper subset (S\subset L), generate:

\[ S\rightarrow L\setminus S, \qquad c=\frac{\sigma(L)}{\sigma(S)}. \]

Here \(\sigma(L)=2\) and minconf = 70%.

Rule Confidence Decision
\(I1,I2\rightarrow I5\) (2/4=50\%) Reject
\(I1,I5\rightarrow I2\) (2/2=100\%) Accept
\(I2,I5\rightarrow I1\) (2/2=100\%) Accept
\(I1\rightarrow I2,I5\) (2/6\approx33\%) Reject
\(I2\rightarrow I1,I5\) (2/7\approx29\%) Reject
\(I5\rightarrow I1,I2\) (2/2=100\%) Accept

All six partitions have the same union support (2/9), but only three have confidence at least 70%.

7. PCY algorithm

7.1 Motivation

PCY, named for Park, Chen, and Yu, is an Apriori variant for large transaction datasets. Apriori’s second pass can be memory-intensive because (n) frequent singletons can produce up to:

\[ \binom n2=\frac{n(n-1)}2 \]

candidate pairs. PCY uses otherwise spare memory in pass 1 to hash all encountered pairs into buckets, allowing many impossible pairs to be eliminated before explicit counting in pass 2.

7.2 Standard PCY workflow

Pass 1

  1. Count every individual item.
  2. For each basket, generate its pairs.
  3. Hash each pair to a bucket and increment that bucket count.

After pass 1:

  1. Mark frequent singleton items.
  2. Convert bucket counts to a bitmap: bucket bit = 1 if count \(\ge\) minimum support; else 0.

Pass 2

  1. A pair becomes a candidate only if: - both individual items are frequent; and - its hash bucket bit is 1.
  2. Count only these candidate pairs exactly.
  3. Retain pairs whose actual support meets the threshold.

The bitmap is compact (one bit per bucket) and filters candidate pairs. A frequent bucket is necessary, not sufficient: collisions mean several infrequent pairs may collectively make a bucket frequent. Exact pair counts are still required.

7.3 Twelve-transaction example

The lecture dataset has 12 baskets (T_1\ldots T_{12}) over items 1–6 (e.g. (T_1={1,2,3}), (T_2={2,3,4}), (T_{12}={3,4,6})). Singleton counts were:

Item Count
1 4
2 6
3 8
4 8
5 6
6 4

All survive the lecture’s initial low singleton cutoff. Generate unique pairs from baskets; example counts mentioned include (12=2), (13=3), (23=3), (24=3), and (34=4).

The demonstration uses:

\[ h(i,j)=(i\times j)\bmod 10. \]

Mod is the remainder operation: (7\bmod5=2), (2\bmod4=2), (4\bmod2=0). Examples:

  • (h(1,3)=3);
  • (h(2,3)=6);
  • (h(2,4)=8);
  • (h(4,5)=20\bmod10=0);
  • (h(4,6)=24\bmod10=4).

Pairs are grouped by bucket (e.g. bucket 0 includes 45; bucket 2 includes 34; bucket 3 includes 13; bucket 4 includes 46; bucket 8 includes 24 in the displayed mapping). With threshold 4, the lecture concludes that pairs 34 and 35 are potential/frequent candidates.

PCY caveat: the narration describes assigning a bucket the “highest support count” of a pair in it. Standard PCY instead increments the bucket once for every pair occurrence, so a bucket count is the sum of all pair occurrences hashing there. The bitmap then filters; an exact second pass decides which individual pairs are truly frequent.

7.4 Brute force vs Apriori vs PCY

Method Core strategy Strength Weakness
Brute force Enumerate/test everything Simple and complete Exponential candidates, high comparisons
Apriori Downward closure; join and subset-prune Eliminates whole branches Multiple scans; second-pass pairs can still be numerous
PCY Apriori + pass-1 hashing/bitmap Reduces pair candidates and memory Hash collisions; needs exact second-pass counting

8. GreenMart case study and managerial use

8.1 Scenario

GreenMart, a regional supermarket chain, wants data-driven cross-promotions among five abstract products (I1\ldots I5).

  • Emily, data scientist: runs Apriori, calculates support/confidence, ensures statistical reliability.
  • Raj, store manager: assesses inventory, feasibility, shortage risk, and operational cost.
  • Sofia, marketing specialist: designs campaigns, increases cross-selling, engagement, and promotional impact.

The case reproduces the Apriori results:

\[ L_1=\{I1,I2,I3,I4,I5\} \]

\[ L_2=\{I1I2,I1I3,I1I5,I2I3,I2I4,I2I5\} \]

\[ L_3=\{I1I2I3,I1I2I5\},\qquad C_4=\varnothing. \]

From \(I1I2I5\), three rules have 100% confidence:

  • \(I1,I5\rightarrow I2\);
  • \(I2,I5\rightarrow I1\);
  • \(I5\rightarrow I1,I2\).

The reverse-style rule \(I1,I2\rightarrow I5\) has only 50% and is rejected at minconf 70%.

8.2 Choosing a promotion

Confidence alone cannot pick among three 100% rules. Evaluate:

  • antecedent support/customer reach;
  • inventory and risk of stock-outs;
  • gross margin and expected sales lift;
  • campaign and operational complexity;
  • customer clarity/convenience;
  • cost of giving a reward versus incremental revenue.

The quiz favors \(I5\rightarrow I1,I2\) as a potentially attractive campaign: one trigger item is easy to explain and may create two-product cross-selling. However, it is appropriate only if (I1/I2) stock, margins, and fulfilment can support the demand. If (I1) inventory is constrained, a statistically perfect rule pointing toward (I1) may be operationally poor.

Lesson: mining identifies statistically dependable opportunities; managers decide which is profitable, practical, scalable, and customer-friendly.

8.3 End-to-end business workflow

  1. Convert transaction logs to baskets.
  2. Choose minsup to avoid rare/unreliable combinations.
  3. Generate (L_1,L_2,\ldots) through join, prune, and scans.
  4. Stop when no candidate remains.
  5. Generate rules from frequent itemsets.
  6. Apply minconf.
  7. Translate rules into cross-sell/bundle/layout candidates.
  8. Check inventory, margin, reach, and campaign simplicity.
  9. Pilot the promotion and measure incremental lift, not just historical confidence.

9. Practical use cases

  • Retail: bread + diaper → beer; co-locate items, issue coupons, or bundle complementary products.
  • Automotive: tyre/accessory purchase → service booking; trigger maintenance offers.
  • E-commerce catalogue: “frequently bought together” and bundle design.
  • Telecom: data-pack + roaming feature → international calling add-on.
  • Healthcare operations: co-occurring services/tests can inform bundled scheduling; associations must not be interpreted as medical causality.
  • Web behavior: page A + page B → page C; improve navigation or content placement.
  • Fraud/security: repeated combinations of event types can flag patterns for review; association does not itself prove fraud.

10. Exam-focused revision

Essential formulas

\[ \sigma(X)=\#\{T:X\subseteq T\},\qquad s(X)=\frac{\sigma(X)}N \]

\[ s(X\rightarrow Y)=\frac{\sigma(X\cup Y)}N \]

\[ c(X\rightarrow Y)=\frac{\sigma(X\cup Y)}{\sigma(X)}=P(Y\mid X) \]

\[ X\subseteq Y\Rightarrow s(X)\ge s(Y) \]

\[ \#\text{itemsets}=2^d,\qquad \#\text{rules}=3^d-2^{d+1}+1 \]

\[ \text{Brute-force cost}\approx O(NMW),\qquad \#\text{pairs}=\binom n2 \]

One-line recall

  • Association analysis finds patterns; ARM generates directional implication rules.
  • Support decides frequent itemsets; confidence decides strong rules.
  • Same union means same support, but rule direction changes confidence.
  • Apriori: every subset of a frequent itemset is frequent; any superset of an infrequent itemset is infrequent.
  • A candidate survives subset pruning but must still pass exact support counting.
  • (C_k) means candidates; (L_k) means frequent itemsets.
  • Apriori stops when the next candidate/frequent level is empty.
  • PCY hashes pairs in pass 1 and uses a bucket bitmap to filter pass-2 candidates.
  • A frequent PCY bucket does not prove every pair in it is frequent because of collisions.
  • Strong statistical rules still require business feasibility analysis.

Likely exam prompts

  1. Calculate support and confidence. Count union transactions for numerator; use (N) for support denominator and antecedent count for confidence denominator.
  2. Explain two-phase ARM. Frequent itemsets by minsup, then rule partitions by minconf.
  3. Derive candidate counts. Explain (2^d) itemsets and (3^d-2^{d+1}+1) rules.
  4. State/prove Apriori principle. If a transaction contains a superset, it necessarily contains every subset, so support cannot increase with added items.
  5. Run Apriori. Write (C_1\to L_1\to C_2\to L_2\ldots), show join, check every subset, count support, and state termination.
  6. Generate rules from (I1I2I5). Enumerate six nonempty partitions and calculate all confidence values.
  7. Explain PCY. Item counts and bucket counts in pass 1, bitmap, candidate conditions, exact pass-2 counts, collision caveat.
  8. Recommend a GreenMart promotion. Compare confidence plus inventory, reach, simplicity, margin, and expected lift.

Common traps

  • Do not divide confidence by total transactions.
  • Do not treat (X\rightarrow Y) and (Y\rightarrow X) as equally confident.
  • Do not infer causality from co-occurrence.
  • Do not say frequent subsets guarantee a frequent superset.
  • Do not support-count a candidate already invalidated by an infrequent subset.
  • Do not confuse a PCY frequent bucket with a confirmed frequent pair.
  • Do not choose a promotion solely because confidence is 100%.

Appendix — Content of the appended live transcript

The file’s live-session recording introduces itself as a Week 6/review session, not Week 7 association-rule mining. It revises material belonging with the bias–variance/cross-validation notes. It is summarized here solely to preserve full transcript coverage.

A. Bias, variance, and fit

  • Bias is described as systematic difference between predicted/expected and true value; variance as sensitivity to different training samples.
  • Total expected error was reviewed as:

\[ \text{Bias}^2+\text{Variance}+\text{irreducible noise}. \]

  • High bias/low variance → underfitting; low bias/high variance → overfitting; low bias/low variance is the desired but never perfectly zero ideal; high bias/high variance is worst.
  • Bullseye/scatter intuition: bias is displacement from the true center, variance is dispersion; the true function is the underlying natural pattern in the population/data, while a model function is an estimate fitted to samples.
  • A straight line fitted to curved data is too simple/high-bias. A highly complex curve may memorize noise/high variance.
  • Underfitting remedies: suitable richer model, relevant features, more representative training data, less excessive regularization.
  • Overfitting remedies: simplify/remove irrelevant features, L1/Lasso or L2/Ridge regularization, and more representative data.
  • The live examples contrast a too-simple model for house-price relations with an unnecessarily complex model for a simple yes/no rainfall task. The algorithm labels used in the spoken examples are imperfect; the core principle is to match capacity and target type to the problem.

B. Training, validation, test, and k-fold CV

  • Training data fit parameters and produce training error; validation tunes hyperparameters/weights/model choices; test data estimate final generalization error.
  • Most data normally go to training because learning patterns needs sufficient examples.
  • In k-fold CV, split the dataset into (k) approximately equal folds. Repeat (k) iterations, each time validating on a different fold and training on the remaining (k-1). Aggregate validation results.
  • The session’s three-fold illustration should have three total fits, not “3 × 3” fits as accidentally spoken. Each fold serves once as validation.
