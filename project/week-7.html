1. Overview

This quiz focuses on advanced research analysis concepts, especially how researchers move from collected data to meaningful conclusions.

The major themes are:

  1. Understanding relationships between variables.
  2. Distinguishing association from causation.
  3. Measuring effects in path models and mediation.
  4. Selecting appropriate sampling methods.
  5. Understanding confidence intervals.
  6. Evaluating regression results.
  7. Determining appropriate sample size.
  8. Interpreting correlations.

2. Association vs Causation

One of the most important principles in research:

A relationship between two variables does not automatically mean one causes the other.

Example

Observation:

Advertising spending increases.

Sales increase.

This shows:

Association

but not necessarily:

Causation

Possible alternative explanations:

  • Seasonal demand.
  • Economic conditions.
  • Promotions.
  • Competitor activity.
  • Brand growth.

3. Establishing Causality

To claim that X causes Y, researchers need stronger evidence.

A causal argument generally requires:

1. Association

X and Y must be related.

Example:

Advertising and sales move together.

2. Temporal Order

Cause must happen before effect.

Example:

Advertising campaign occurs before sales increase.

3. Elimination of Alternative Explanations

Other causes should be controlled.

4. Experimental and Controlled Designs

The strongest method for causal inference is controlled research.

Example:

Compare two similar groups:

Group A:

Receives increased advertising.

Group B:

Receives normal advertising.

If sales increase only in Group A, stronger evidence exists for causality.

5. Correlation

Definition

Correlation measures:

  • Strength.
  • Direction.

of a linear relationship between two variables.

Example:

Service quality and satisfaction.

Question:

“Do customers with higher service quality ratings also report higher satisfaction?”

6. Pearson Correlation Coefficient (r)

Used when:

  • Variables are continuous.
  • Relationship is linear.

Range:

\[ \-1 \\leq r \\leq \+1 \]

Interpretation:

Positive correlation

As X increases, Y increases.

Example:

More service quality → higher satisfaction.

Negative correlation

As X increases, Y decreases.

Example:

More waiting time → lower satisfaction.

Zero correlation

No linear relationship.

7. Correlation vs Regression

These are related but serve different purposes.

Correlation

Question:

Are two variables related?

Example:

“Is service quality associated with satisfaction?”

Output:

Correlation coefficient (r)

Regression

Question:

Can X predict Y?

Example:

“Does service quality predict satisfaction?”

Output:

  • Regression coefficient.
  • Explained variance.

8. Correlation Does Not Prove Causation

Example:

Ice cream sales and drowning incidents increase together.

Does ice cream cause drowning?

No.

Third variable:

Temperature.

Hot weather increases both.

9. Regression Analysis

Regression estimates relationships between variables.

Basic form:

\[ Y \= a \+ bX \]

where:

  • Y \= dependent variable.
  • X \= predictor.
  • b \= effect size.

10. Evaluating Regression Results

A meaningful relationship requires checking:

1. Statistical significance

Measured by:

  • p-value.

Question:

“Is this relationship unlikely to have occurred by chance?”

2. Practical strength

Measured by:

  • Beta coefficient.

Question:

“How large is the relationship?”

11. p-value vs Beta Coefficient

Measure Purpose
p-value Statistical reliability
Beta coefficient Relationship strength

Example:

Advertising → Sales

p-value:

Shows whether the relationship is statistically significant.

Beta:

Shows how strongly advertising predicts sales.

A good finding should ideally be:

  • Statistically significant.
  • Practically meaningful.

12. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) and Mediation

SEM models relationships among latent variables.

Example:

Employee motivation → Job attitude → Performance.

13. Mediation Model

A mediator explains:

“How does X affect Y?”

Structure:

X → Mediator → Y

Example:

Training quality

↓

Perceived usefulness

↓

Satisfaction

14. Direct, Indirect, and Total Effects

Direct Effect

The immediate relationship:

X → Y

Example:

Advertising → Sales

without considering other variables.

Indirect Effect

Effect through mediator(s).

Example:

Advertising

↓

Brand awareness

↓

Sales

Total Effect

Overall influence:

\[ Total Effect \= Direct Effect \+ Indirect Effect \]

15. Reporting Overall Influence

If the direct effect is near zero but mediation paths are significant:

The researcher should report:

Total Effect

because it captures the complete influence of X on Y.

16. Bootstrap Mediation

Bootstrap methods estimate indirect effects by repeatedly resampling data.

Purpose:

  • Improve confidence in mediation estimates.
  • Avoid relying only on normality assumptions.

If the indirect effect is significant:

Evidence exists that mediation occurs.

17. Sampling and Generalisation

Research results are useful only if they can be generalized appropriately.

18. Probability Sampling

Probability sampling gives known selection probabilities.

Examples:

  • Random sampling.
  • Systematic sampling.
  • Stratified sampling.

Advantages:

  • Better population inference.
  • Reduced selection bias.

19. Random Sampling

Definition

Every population member has a known chance of selection.

Example:

Randomly selecting customers from a complete customer list.

Benefit:

Reduces dependence on researcher choices.

20. Systematic Sampling

Definition

Selecting every kth person from a list.

Example:

Every 50th customer.

Important consideration:

The starting point should usually be random.

21. Systematic Sampling Risk

If the list has patterns, systematic sampling can introduce bias.

Example:

Customer list ordered by:

  • Branch.
  • Account type.

If account type relates to spending or credit score:

Choosing every kth customer may repeatedly capture certain patterns.

22. Best Approach for Ordered Lists

If the roster has possible hidden patterns:

Use:

Simple random sampling

or:

Random-start systematic sampling

Random selection prevents list order from influencing the sample.

23. Sample Unit

Definition

The individual entity being measured.

Example:

Research:

“Customers rate bank service quality.”

Sample unit:

Individual customer.

Not:

  • Branch.
  • Bank policy.
  • Competitor.

24. Confidence Intervals

A confidence interval estimates an unknown population parameter.

Example:

“We are 95% confident average satisfaction is between 7.1 and 7.8.”

25. Meaning of 95% Confidence Level

Correct interpretation:

If the study were repeated many times:

Approximately 95% of constructed intervals would contain the true population mean.

Incorrect interpretation:

“There is a 95% probability that this specific interval contains the true mean.”

The true value is fixed; the interval is the uncertain quantity.

26. Confidence Interval Components

Affected by:

Sample size

Larger sample:

  • Smaller uncertainty.
  • Narrower interval.

Confidence level

Higher confidence:

  • Wider interval.

Example:

99% confidence interval is wider than 95%.

Variability

More variation:

  • Wider interval.

27. SEM Sample Size Planning

Structural equation models require careful sample size planning.

SEM includes:

  • Latent variables.
  • Multiple indicators.
  • Multiple relationships.

28. Power Analysis

Recommended approach.

Considers:

  • Expected effect size.
  • Number of variables.
  • Number of indicators.
  • Desired statistical power.

29. Why SEM Needs Larger Samples

More complex models require more data.

Reasons:

  • More parameters to estimate.
  • More relationships.
  • More indicators.

30. R² vs Effect Measures

R²

Measures:

How much variance in Y is explained by the model.

Example:

Advertising and price explain 60% of sales variation.

However:

R² does not replace effect analysis.

31. Purposive Sampling and Generalisation

Purposive Sampling

Researchers intentionally select participants.

Example:

Selecting expert users.

Advantage:

Useful for specific insights.

Limitation:

Selection probabilities are unknown.

32. Statistical Significance vs Population Generalisation

A p-value answers:

“Is this relationship present in this sample?”

It does not automatically answer:

“Does this relationship exist in the entire population?”

For population claims:

Probability sampling is important.

33. Mediation and Sampling Connection

A mediation analysis can show:

X affects Y through M.

But if sampling is weak:

The mediation relationship may not generalize.

Example:

Purposive sample:

Strong mediation found.

Cannot confidently claim:

All customers behave this way.

34. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1:

A strong correlation proves causation.

Reality:

Correlation only shows association.

Misconception 2:

A significant p-value means the effect is important.

Reality:

Need effect size.

Misconception 3:

A large R² proves causation.

Reality:

Explained variance does not establish causal direction.

Misconception 4:

A confidence interval gives the probability of the parameter being inside the interval.

Reality:

It describes the long-run success rate of the method.

Misconception 5:

Any random-looking selection is random sampling.

Reality:

Selection must follow a defined probability mechanism.

35. Final Revision Framework

To evaluate relationships:

Check:

  1. Correlation.
  2. Regression coefficient.
  3. p-value.
  4. Effect size.

To claim causation:

Need:

  1. Association.
  2. Temporal order.
  3. Control of alternatives.

To evaluate mediation:

Check:

  1. Direct effect.
  2. Indirect effect.
  3. Total effect.

To generalize findings:

Need:

  1. Appropriate sampling.
  2. Known selection probabilities.
  3. Representative sample.

To design SEM studies:

Consider:

  • Model complexity.
  • Indicators.
  • Effect sizes.
  • Statistical power.

Core Principle

Good research conclusions require three layers:

Measurement quality → Statistical evidence → Appropriate generalisation

A statistically significant result from a weak sample or poor design may describe only the studied participants, not the wider population.

At LearnPro, an educational technology startup, Research Director Maria Nguyen and Data Analyst Jamal Ortiz must design a study to evaluate a new peer mentoring feature aimed at increasing course completion rates. Marketing Lead Priya Desai wants evidence to decide if the feature should be expanded. Choosing stratified sampling ensures each course category has enough data, while cluster sampling reduces travel costs. They finalize the stratified approach, drawing a proportional sample of learners from three main course strata.With data collected, Jamal runs a path analysis testing mentoring (X), engagement (M), completion rate (Y), and prior experience (W). 1. Case Overview

LearnPro is an educational technology startup evaluating a new peer mentoring feature designed to increase course completion rates.

The research objective is to determine:

  1. Whether peer mentoring improves course completion.
  2. Whether mentoring works by increasing learner engagement.
  3. Whether the effect differs depending on learners’ prior experience.

The study involves two major components:

  1. Sampling design — selecting a representative group of learners.
  2. Path analysis — testing relationships between mentoring, engagement, completion, and prior experience.

2. Research Problem Structure

The central research question:

Does peer mentoring improve course completion, and for whom does it work best?

The model contains:

  • Independent Variable (X): Peer mentoring feature.
  • Mediator (M): Learner engagement.
  • Dependent Variable (Y): Course completion rate.
  • Moderator (W): Prior experience.

The basic model:

This represents mediation.

4. Sampling Design

Before collecting data, researchers must define the sampling design.

A sampling design includes five major elements:

  1. Sample unit.
  2. Target population.
  3. Sampling method.
  4. Sample size.
  5. Sampling frame.

5. Sample Unit

Definition

The sample unit is the individual entity from which data is collected.

LearnPro Example

Sample unit:

Each learner who has enrolled in a course.

This means each learner provides information about:

  • Mentoring exposure.
  • Engagement.
  • Completion behaviour.
  • Experience level.

Importance

Clearly defining the sample unit avoids ambiguity.

Incorrect:

“Courses are sampled.”

Correct:

“Learners enrolled in courses are sampled.”

6. Target Population

Definition

The entire group the researchers want to understand.

LearnPro Example

Target population:

All active LearnPro platform users.

The sample represents this population.

Example:

Population:

100,000 active users.

Sample:

600 selected learners.

Research goal:

Understand mentoring effects among all users.

The actual list or database from which the sample is selected.

LearnPro Example

Sampling frame:

Enrollment database.

  • Learner identities.

The team compares:

  1. Stratified sampling.
  2. Cluster sampling.

9. Stratified Sampling

Definition

A probability sampling method where the population is divided into meaningful subgroups called strata.

Samples are taken from each stratum.

LearnPro Example

Learners divided by:

  • Course category.

Examples:

  • Programming.
  • Business.
  • Design.

Then learners are selected from each category.

10. Purpose of Stratified Sampling

The main goal:

Ensure important subgroups are adequately represented.

Without stratification:

Popular course categories may dominate.

Example:

Programming courses:

70% of sample.

Other categories:

Underrepresented.

With stratification:

Each category receives appropriate representation.

11. Advantages of Stratified Sampling

1. Better Representation

Ensures all important groups appear in the sample.

2. Better Subgroup Analysis

Allows comparison:

Example:

Does mentoring work differently for:

  • Programming learners?
  • Business learners?

3. Improved Statistical Power

Enough observations exist within each subgroup.

12. Limitations of Stratified Sampling

Requires:

  • Knowledge of population groups.
  • More planning.
  • More complex sampling process.

13. Cluster Sampling

Definition

A sampling method where natural groups are selected instead of individuals.

LearnPro Example

Clusters:

Geographic regions.

Example:

  • North region.
  • South region.
  • East region.

Researchers select regions and collect data within them.

14. Purpose of Cluster Sampling

Main purpose:

Reduce cost and logistical effort.

Example:

Instead of contacting learners worldwide:

Study selected regions.

15. Advantages of Cluster Sampling

1. Lower Cost

Reduces:

  • Travel.
  • Administration.
  • Data collection complexity.

2. Easier Field Operations

Useful when population is geographically dispersed.

16. Limitations of Cluster Sampling

Reduced Representation

Selected clusters may not reflect the entire population.

Example:

One region may contain unusually motivated learners.

Lower Statistical Efficiency

Learners within the same cluster may be similar.

17. Stratified vs Cluster Sampling Comparison

Feature Stratified Sampling Cluster Sampling

The research requires:

23. Mediation Analysis

Purpose

Explains:

How does X influence Y?

Model:

Mentoring
↓
Engagement
↓
Completion

Interpretation:

Mentoring improves engagement, which improves completion.

24. Indirect Effect

The indirect effect measures:

X → M → Y

Example:

Mentoring increases engagement.

Engagement increases completion.

The combined pathway is the indirect effect.

25. Direct Effect

The direct effect measures:

X → Y

Example:

Mentoring directly increases completion even without considering engagement.

26. Total Effect

Formula:

Total Effect \= Direct Effect + Indirect Effect

It represents the overall influence of mentoring on completion.

Definition

A moderator changes the strength or direction of a relationship.

Question:

↓

29. Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

The team uses bootstrap methods.

Purpose

To test indirect effects without relying heavily on normality assumptions.

Process:

  1. Repeatedly resample data.
  2. Estimate indirect effect.
  3. Build confidence interval.

Interpretation:

If confidence interval does not include zero:

Indirect effect is significant.

30. Conditional Indirect Effects

The team examines:

Does mediation occur at different experience levels?

Example:

Novices:

Strong mentoring → engagement → completion.

Experts:

Weak or no effect.

31. Research Finding Interpretation

Finding:

Mentoring-to-engagement-to-completion chain is significant for novices but not experts.

Meaning:

Beginners benefit from mentoring.

Why?

They may need:

  • Guidance.
  • Support.
  • Learning strategies.

Experts may benefit less.

Why?

They already possess:

  • Experience.
  • Knowledge.
  • Independent learning ability.

32. Beta Coefficients

Definition

Standardized coefficients showing:

  • Direction.
  • Strength.

of relationships.

Example:

Mentoring → Engagement:

β \= 0.50

Engagement → Completion:

β \= 0.30

Larger absolute beta:

Stronger relationship.

33. Why Beta Coefficients Matter

They allow comparison.

Example:

Which factor matters more?

  • Mentoring effect.
  • Prior experience effect.

Beta provides objective comparison.

34. Statistical Significance vs Effect Strength

Researchers need both:

Statistical significance

Question:

“Is this relationship reliable?”

Measured by:

  • p-value.
  • Confidence intervals.

Effect strength

Question:

“How large is the relationship?”

Measured by:

  • Beta coefficient.

A relationship can be:

Significant but weak.

Example:

Large sample detects tiny effect.

38. Strategic Trade-Off

The decision depends on research priority.

If priority is:

Understanding differences between learners:

Choose stratified sampling.

If priority is:

Reducing operational cost:

Choose cluster sampling.

For LearnPro:

Because the goal is to understand mentoring effectiveness across learner groups, stratified sampling provides stronger research value.

39. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1:

Cluster sampling is always worse.

Reality:

Cluster sampling is valuable when cost and geography matter.

Misconception 2:

A significant mediation means mentoring works for everyone.

Reality:

Moderation shows effects may differ across groups.

Misconception 3:

A mediator is just another predictor.

Reality:

A mediator explains the mechanism.

Misconception 4:

A moderator explains why something happens.

Reality:

A moderator explains when or for whom an effect occurs.

Misconception 5:

Large samples automatically solve bias.

Reality:

A large biased sample can still produce incorrect conclusions.

Sampling strategy + statistical model + research objective

  1. Whether mentoring improves completion.
  2. How mentoring creates improvement.
  3. Which learners benefit most from the feature.

The study combined:

  • Sampling design principles.
  • Path analysis.
  • Mediation analysis.
  • Moderation analysis.
  • Bootstrap confidence intervals.
  • Strategic interpretation of results.

2. Research Model Structure

The LearnPro model contains four important variables:

Role Variable Meaning
Independent Variable (X) Peer Mentoring The intervention being tested
Mediator (M) Engagement The mechanism explaining the effect
Dependent Variable (Y) Course Completion Rate The outcome being improved
Moderator (W) Prior Experience The condition that changes the effect

3. Basic Research Question

The main question:

Does peer mentoring improve course completion, and does this improvement happen differently for learners with different levels of prior experience?

4. Sampling Design

Before analyzing data, researchers must decide:

  • Who will be studied.
  • How participants will be selected.
  • Whether the sample represents the population.

A sampling design has five key elements:

  1. Sample unit.
  2. Target population.
  3. Sampling method.
  4. Sample size.
  5. Sampling frame.

5. Sample Unit

Definition

The sample unit is the individual element selected for the study.

LearnPro Example

Sample unit:

Each learner who enrolled in a course.

This means:

Each observation corresponds to one learner.

Data collected:

  • Mentoring exposure.
  • Engagement.
  • Completion.
  • Prior experience.

Importance

Correctly identifying the sample unit prevents confusion.

Example:

Incorrect:

“The course is the sample unit.”

Correct:

“The learner is the sample unit.”

8. Sampling Methods Compared

The team considered:

  1. Stratified sampling.
  2. Cluster sampling.

These methods solve different problems.

9. Stratified Sampling

Definition

A method where the population is divided into meaningful subgroups called strata, and samples are selected from each subgroup.

LearnPro Example

Strata:

  • Programming courses.
  • Business courses.
  • Design courses.

Participants are selected proportionally from each group.

10. Purpose of Stratified Sampling

Primary objective:

Ensure important subgroups are adequately represented.

Why important?

Because the team wants to understand:

  • Does mentoring work across different course categories?
  • Are some learner groups more affected?

11. Advantages of Stratified Sampling

1. Better Representation

Prevents large groups from dominating the sample.

Example:

Without stratification:

Programming learners may dominate.

With stratification:

All course categories are represented.

2. Better Subgroup Analysis

Allows comparison between groups.

Example:

Mentoring impact:

Programming learners vs business learners.

3. Higher Statistical Reliability

Each subgroup has enough observations.

12. Limitations of Stratified Sampling

Requires:

  • Knowledge of population structure.
  • More planning.
  • More administration.

13. Cluster Sampling

Definition

A method where naturally occurring groups are selected instead of individuals.

LearnPro Example

Clusters:

Geographic regions.

Example:

  • North India.
  • South India.
  • Europe.

Researchers select clusters and collect data within them.

14. Purpose of Cluster Sampling

Primary objective:

Reduce operational cost and improve feasibility.

Useful when:

  • Population is geographically distributed.
  • Data collection is expensive.

15. Advantages of Cluster Sampling

Lower Cost

Reduces:

  • Travel.
  • Coordination.
  • Fieldwork.

Easier Scaling

Useful for large studies.

16. Limitations of Cluster Sampling

Lower Precision

People within clusters may be similar.

Example:

Learners from one region may have similar behaviours.

Higher Sampling Error

Selected clusters may not fully represent the population.

Purpose

31. Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

Purpose

Used to test indirect effects.

Why bootstrap?

Indirect effects often do not follow normal distributions.

Bootstrap provides more reliable estimates.

Process:

  1. Resample data many times.
  2. Calculate indirect effects.
  3. Create confidence intervals.

32. Interpreting Bootstrap Results

If confidence interval excludes zero:

The indirect effect is significant.

Example:

Novices:

Indirect effect significant.

Experts:

Indirect effect not significant.

Effect size

Mediators and moderators are similar.

Reality:

Explains mechanism.

Changes strength.

Cluster sampling is inferior.

Cluster sampling is valuable when cost efficiency is important.

Week 7 Exam Appendix

Rapid-recall map

Define population and sampling unit → obtain a sampling frame → select a probability/non-probability method → justify sample size → collect representative data → describe and validate measurement/data assumptions → estimate population parameters and relationships → report uncertainty, effect strength, and significance → generalize only as far as the design permits

High-value distinctions

Distinction Exam-ready answer
Population vs. sample Population is the entire group about which conclusions are desired; a sample is the smaller studied subset intended to represent it.
Sampling unit vs. sampling frame Unit is the eligible entity providing data (person, couple, country, machine); frame is the operational list/source containing the population’s units.
Sample statistic vs. population parameter A statistic describes a sample (e.g., sample mean); a parameter describes the population and is usually unknown. Estimation uses statistics to infer parameters.
Sampling method vs. sample size Method answers how units are selected; size answers how many are selected. Together they form sampling design. A large biased sample does not repair poor selection.
Probability vs. non-probability sampling Probability selection uses a known random selection mechanism and supports statistical generalization; non-probability selection does not ensure known/equal selection chances and limits generalization.
Stratified vs. cluster sampling Stratified: divide into internally homogeneous strata and sample from every relevant stratum to improve representation/comparison. Cluster: divide into naturally occurring, internally heterogeneous clusters, select some clusters, then survey all/some units within them, mainly to lower cost.
Systematic vs. simple random sampling Simple random uses independent random selection; systematic chooses every kth unit after a random start. Systematic sampling is efficient but vulnerable to periodic ordering in the frame.
Confidence interval vs. confidence level Interval is the estimated range for a population parameter; confidence level is the long-run coverage rate of the procedure (e.g., 95%). Higher confidence usually requires a wider interval, other things equal.
Statistical significance vs. practical strength A p-value/significance test addresses compatibility with a null hypothesis; a beta/effect size indicates magnitude and often direction. A tiny effect can be significant in a large sample.
Correlation vs. regression Correlation summarizes the strength/direction of symmetric association; regression specifies an outcome and predictor(s), estimates their effects, supports prediction, and can adjust for other variables.
Association vs. causation Association alone is insufficient. A causal claim additionally needs temporal precedence and credible elimination of rival explanations, ideally through randomization/control or a strong causal design.
Direct vs. indirect vs. total effect Direct: X→Y after accounting for M. Indirect: X→M multiplied by M→Y. Total: direct + indirect effect.
Mediation vs. moderation Mediation explains how/why X affects Y (X→M→Y); moderation asks when/for whom an effect varies (X×W→Y). Moderated mediation means an indirect effect differs by W.
R² vs. beta/effect size R² is the proportion of outcome variance jointly explained by the model; a beta is a particular path/predictor’s estimated effect. High R² does not prove causality.
Significance vs. generalizability A statistically significant coefficient within a purposive sample does not make that sample representative of the population. Inference depends on both analysis and sampling design.

Sampling-design decision guide

Four elements

  1. Sampling unit: who/what is qualified to supply the data? - Banking service quality → a bank customer. - Divorce intention → the couple may be the unit. - Export competitiveness → a country. - Plant machine performance → an individual machine.
  2. Target population: the complete group to which the researcher wishes to infer.
  3. Sampling frame: the list/device used to identify units—school attendance register, voter list, bank customer database.
  4. Method and size: how the subset is selected and how many observations are needed for valid/reliable analysis.

Probability methods

  • Simple random: every member has a known/equal chance; lottery or random-number generator. Example: assign employee IDs and randomly select 100.
  • Systematic: calculate an interval k, choose a random start, then every kth entry. Example: from 100 names, select 10 using every 10th entry after a random start. Check that list order has no pattern aligned with k.
  • Stratified: form mutually exclusive homogeneous subgroups, then randomly sample from each, often proportionally. Example: sample students from every class/year so all levels are represented.
  • Cluster: form natural heterogeneous groups, randomly select a subset of groups, then study all/some units within them. Example: randomly choose five schools and survey students within those schools.

Non-probability methods

  • Convenience: select those easiest to reach; fast and inexpensive but bias-prone.
  • Purposive/judgmental: researcher deliberately selects information-rich/qualified units. Useful for experts or narrowly defined cases, but does not justify broad statistical generalization.
  • Quota: fill specified subgroup numbers non-randomly; resembles stratification in subgroup targets, but not in random selection.
  • Snowball: one participant refers the next; useful for hidden or rare populations, such as people with a rare disease. Network dependence can create selection bias.

Sample-size logic

Sample size should be calculated/justified, not guessed. It depends on:

  • population size (especially for finite/small populations);
  • desired confidence level;
  • desired margin of error/interval precision;
  • expected variability/effect size;
  • number of indicators/parameters and model complexity;
  • statistical power and significance criterion;
  • expected missing data/non-response.

Simple estimation, ANOVA, or regression can use an appropriate sample-size/power calculator. Multiple regression, path analysis, and SEM require planning based on model parameters/indicators and power; they usually need larger samples because multiple relationships and latent constructs are estimated simultaneously.

What statistics contributes to research

Statistics helps researchers:

  • estimate population characteristics from sample characteristics;
  • quantify precision/uncertainty through standard errors and confidence intervals;
  • verify analysis assumptions;
  • assess measurement quality of latent constructs;
  • describe data;
  • test relationship and group-difference hypotheses;
  • forecast outcomes, such as future sales from historical data;
  • reduce dimensions and discover patterns/clusters among related items; and
  • support evidence-based inference and decisions.

Height-estimation example

A school has 1,000 students. A random sample of 100 has a mean height of 150 cm. The 150-cm value is a sample statistic used to estimate the unknown population mean (parameter). Report an interval and confidence level rather than pretending the estimate is exact.

Precise interpretation: a 95% confidence procedure is one that, over repeated comparable samples, would produce intervals containing the true parameter about 95% of the time. Avoid saying there is a 95% probability that a fixed parameter lies in this already calculated frequentist interval; the lecture uses that intuitive shorthand, but the long-run statement is technically safer in an exam.

Relationship estimation and causal reasoning

Correlation

Pearson’s r ranges from −1 to +1:

  • positive: variables increase together;
  • negative: one tends to decrease as the other increases;
  • near zero: little/no linear association.

Magnitude indicates linear association strength; sign indicates direction. It does not establish cause, handle all nonlinear patterns, or eliminate confounders.

Classic caution: Ice-cream sales and drowning may correlate because hot weather increases both. Temperature is an alternative explanation.

Regression

A simple regression may be expressed as:

Y = a + bX + error

  • a: intercept/expected Y when X=0 (interpret only if meaningful).
  • b or beta: expected change in Y for a one-unit change in X, under the model.
  • p-value/CI: uncertainty about the coefficient.
  • R²: proportion of Y’s observed variation explained by the predictor set.

With multiple regression, each coefficient is interpreted while other included predictors are held constant. Significance answers whether evidence is inconsistent with a null/no-effect model; it does not answer whether the effect is managerially important.

Conditions for a causal claim

  1. X and Y are associated.
  2. X occurs before Y (temporal order).
  3. Plausible alternative explanations are eliminated or credibly controlled.

Randomized controlled experiments offer the strongest routine design for these conditions. Observational regression can support an argument but does not automatically prove causality.

Mediation, moderation, and bootstrap inference

For mediation:

  • path a: X→M;
  • path b: M→Y controlling X;
  • indirect effect: a × b;
  • direct effect: c′ (X→Y controlling M);
  • total effect: c = c′ + ab.

The indirect effect’s sampling distribution is often non-normal. Bootstrapping repeatedly resamples the observed data, estimates ab, and forms a confidence interval. An indirect effect is conventionally supported when the bootstrap CI excludes zero.

For moderation, include an interaction (X×W). Interpret the conditional X effect at meaningful W values/groups. For moderated mediation, estimate the indirect effect at different W values and compare conditional indirect effects; do not infer it merely because one subgroup is significant and another is not.

LearnPro applied case

Model

  • X: peer mentoring.
  • M: learner engagement.
  • Y: course-completion rate.
  • W: prior experience.

The substantive question is whether peer mentoring improves completion through greater engagement and whether this indirect mechanism differs for novice and experienced learners. Findings suggest beginners benefit more from mentoring while experts may benefit less.

Sampling choice

  • Sample unit: an eligible LearnPro learner.
  • Target population: the defined active learner population to which LearnPro wants to apply the results.
  • Frame: the relevant learner/customer database after applying eligibility rules.
  • Stratification: sample from novice/intermediate/expert or other important subgroups to preserve representation and enable subgroup comparisons.
  • Cluster alternative: select whole regions, centers, cohorts, or organizational clients to reduce field cost; operationally easier but usually less precise because people in a cluster resemble one another.

Strategic trade-off

Use/expand stratification when the managerial priority is understanding differences among learner segments and ensuring representation. Use clusters when geographic/operational cost dominates and reduced precision is acceptable. A hybrid/multistage design may select clusters and then stratify/sample within clusters, provided inference and design effects are handled correctly.

Decision use

Do not simply roll out mentoring uniformly. If conditional indirect effects show the mechanism is strong for novices but weak for experts, target intensive mentoring toward novice learners and consider lighter or different support for experts. Combine statistical significance with beta/indirect-effect magnitude, interval precision, cost, and business value.

Frequent mistakes to avoid

  • Treating the sampling frame as identical to the target population despite omissions/duplicates.
  • Claiming probability sampling when selection was convenient or purposive.
  • Assuming a large sample is representative.
  • Using systematic sampling without inspecting periodic ordering.
  • Confusing strata (sample from each) with clusters (select some groups).
  • Choosing sample size by an arbitrary “rule of thumb” without power, precision, or model justification.
  • Treating a 95% confidence level as certainty.
  • Treating p < .05 as proof of a large, useful, or causal effect.
  • Reporting beta without direction, units/standardization, CI, or context.
  • Claiming causality from correlation or cross-sectional regression.
  • Reporting only direct effects when mediation makes total/indirect effects central.
  • Declaring mediation from separate significant paths without testing ab.
  • Declaring moderated mediation without conditional indirect effects/interaction evidence.
  • Generalizing purposive-sample results merely because coefficients are significant.
  • Reading R² as the effect of one predictor or as causal proof.
