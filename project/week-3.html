1. Overview of Literature Review in Research

A literature review is a structured process of examining existing research to understand:

  • What is already known about a topic.
  • Which theories and variables have been studied.
  • What methods previous researchers used.
  • Where gaps or unanswered questions remain.

A strong literature review does not simply summarize previous studies. It builds an argument for why a new study is needed.

The overall purpose is:

Existing knowledge → Identify limitations → Develop research direction

2. Purpose of a Literature Review

A literature review helps researchers:

1. Understand Existing Knowledge

Researchers examine:

  • Previous findings.
  • Established theories.
  • Common research methods.
  • Important concepts.

Example:

A researcher studying hybrid work and team performance reviews previous studies to understand:

  • How remote work affects collaboration.
  • Which performance measures are commonly used.
  • Where findings disagree.

2. Build a Theoretical Foundation

Literature reviews help identify:

  • Relevant theories.
  • Important concepts.
  • Relationships between variables.

Example:

A digital adoption study may use:

  • Technology Acceptance Model.
  • Diffusion of Innovation Theory.

The literature review helps determine:

Which theory explains the research problem?

3. Identify Research Variables

Previous studies help researchers identify:

Independent variables:

  • Possible causes.
  • Influencing factors.

Dependent variables:

  • Outcomes being explained.

Example:

Research topic:

Employee engagement and retention.

Possible variables:

  • Leadership support.
  • Workplace culture.
  • Employee satisfaction.
  • Intention to stay.

4. Identify Research Gaps

A research gap is something that previous studies have not adequately explained.

Examples:

  • Missing populations.
  • Missing geographical contexts.
  • Outdated data.
  • Conflicting findings.
  • Limited theoretical understanding.

3. Types of Literature Reviews

Different review methods serve different purposes.

3.1 Systematic Literature Review (SLR)

Definition

A systematic literature review is a structured and reproducible review method that uses predefined procedures to identify, select, evaluate, and synthesize existing studies.

It aims to reduce:

  • Selection bias.
  • Researcher subjectivity.
  • Inconsistent source selection.

Key Characteristics

A systematic review includes:

1. Defined Research Question

The review begins with a focused question.

Example:

What factors influence digital technology adoption among small businesses?

2. Search Strategy

Researchers define:

  • Databases used.
  • Keywords.
  • Search combinations.
  • Date ranges.

3. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Rules determine:

Which studies are included?

Example inclusion criteria:

  • Peer-reviewed articles.
  • Published after 2015.
  • Studies involving organizational adoption.

Example exclusion criteria:

  • Non-English articles.
  • Studies unrelated to the research question.

4. Quality Assessment

Included studies are evaluated for:

  • Research quality.
  • Methodological strength.
  • Risk of bias.

5. Evidence Synthesis

Researchers combine findings to identify:

  • Common themes.
  • Relationships.
  • Research gaps.

3.2 Narrative (Traditional) Review

Definition

A narrative review summarizes and discusses literature based on the author’s interpretation.

Characteristics

Advantages:

  • Flexible.
  • Useful for understanding broad topics.
  • Allows conceptual discussion.

Limitations:

  • Less reproducible.
  • Higher risk of selection bias.

Comparison with SLR

Feature Systematic Review Narrative Review
Search process Planned and documented Flexible
Selection criteria Explicit Often subjective
Reproducibility High Lower
Bias control Stronger Weaker

3.3 Scoping Review

Definition

A scoping review maps the breadth of research on a topic.

It answers:

What research exists in this area?

rather than:

What is the effect of a specific intervention?

Characteristics

Useful for:

  • Emerging topics.
  • Broad research areas.
  • Identifying available evidence.

Example:

Mapping all studies related to artificial intelligence in healthcare.

3.4 Rapid Review

A rapid review is an accelerated form of evidence review.

It may simplify:

  • Search process.
  • Screening.
  • Quality assessment.

Used when:

  • Decisions are needed quickly.
  • Time is limited.

4. Systematic Literature Review Process

A typical SLR follows a structured sequence.

Step 1: Define Research Question

A clear research question guides:

  • Search strategy.
  • Study selection.
  • Analysis.

A vague question creates irrelevant results.

Step 2: Identify Search Terms

The search strategy should be planned before searching.

Identifying Main Concepts

First identify major concepts.

Example:

Research question:

How does remote work affect employee productivity?

Main concepts:

  1. Remote work
  2. Employee productivity

Finding Synonyms and Related Terms

Each concept may have multiple terms.

Example:

Remote work:

  • Remote work
  • Telework
  • Work from home
  • Hybrid work

Productivity:

  • Performance
  • Efficiency
  • Work output

Combining Terms Using Boolean Operators

Common operators:

AND

Narrows results.

Example:

remote work AND productivity

Returns studies containing both concepts.

OR

Expands results.

Example:

remote work OR telework OR hybrid work

Finds studies using different terms.

Correct Search Planning Sequence

  1. Identify main concepts.
  2. List synonyms and related terms.
  3. Combine using AND/OR.
  4. Document the final search strategy.

5. Search Tools and Databases

Different search tools serve different purposes.

Google Scholar

Purpose

Broad academic search.

Includes:

  • Journal articles.
  • Conference papers.
  • Reports.
  • Theses.
  • Other scholarly documents.

Useful for:

  • Initial broad searches.
  • Finding grey literature.

JSTOR

Purpose

Academic archive.

Strong in:

  • Humanities.
  • Social sciences.
  • Historical research.

Focus:

Mostly journal collections and books.

PubMed

Purpose

Biomedical and health sciences database.

Useful for:

  • Medicine.
  • Healthcare.
  • Clinical studies.

Web of Science

Purpose

Curated scholarly database.

Strengths:

  • Citation tracking.
  • High-quality indexing.
  • Research impact analysis.

Database Comparison

Tool Main Strength
Google Scholar Broad academic coverage
JSTOR Archived academic collections
PubMed Medical and health research
Web of Science Citation analysis and indexed journals

6. Screening and Study Selection in SLR

After searching databases:

Researchers usually obtain many records.

The process involves:

1. Remove Duplicates

The same paper may appear in multiple databases.

Duplicate removal prevents:

  • Double counting.
  • Incorrect evidence estimates.

2. Apply Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies are screened based on predefined rules.

Example:

Include:

  • Studies on employee engagement.
  • Quantitative and qualitative research.

Exclude:

  • Opinion articles.
  • Studies unrelated to workplace settings.

3. Evaluate Eligible Studies

Remaining studies are assessed for:

  • Quality.
  • Reliability.
  • Bias risk.

4. Extract Data

Researchers collect information such as:

  • Author.
  • Year.
  • Methodology.
  • Sample.
  • Key findings.

7. PIECES Framework in Literature Review

PIECES represents stages in systematic review workflow.

The relevant stages include:

Identifying Candidate Studies

Purpose:

Find potential research papers through searching.

Activities:

  • Database searching.
  • Keyword application.
  • Initial collection.

Evaluating Eligible Studies

Purpose:

Determine whether selected studies are suitable.

Includes:

  • Applying inclusion/exclusion criteria.
  • Quality assessment.
  • Risk-of-bias evaluation.

Collecting and Combining Evidence

Purpose:

Extract and synthesize findings.

Activities:

  • Data extraction.
  • Comparison.
  • Evidence integration.

Summarising Review Outcomes

Purpose:

Present final findings.

Includes:

  • Themes.
  • Patterns.
  • Research gaps.
  • Conclusions.

8. Data Extraction and Evidence Synthesis

After selecting studies, researchers extract relevant information.

A structured extraction table may include:

Category Example
Author Smith et al.
Year 2024
Research topic Hybrid work
Method Survey
Sample 500 employees
Findings Remote flexibility improved satisfaction

Why Structured Extraction Matters

It allows researchers to:

  • Compare studies.
  • Identify patterns.
  • Avoid selective interpretation.

9. Thematic Analysis

Definition

Thematic analysis identifies common patterns or themes in qualitative data.

Used with:

  • Interview transcripts.
  • Focus group discussions.
  • Open-ended responses.

Process

Step 1: Familiarization

Read transcripts repeatedly.

Step 2: Coding

Assign labels to meaningful sections.

Example:

Interview statement:

“My manager rarely communicates changes.”

Code:

  • Poor communication.

Step 3: Theme Development

Combine related codes.

Example:

Codes:

  • Poor communication.
  • Lack of updates.
  • Unclear expectations.

Theme:

Leadership communication problems.

Step 4: Interpretation

Explain how themes relate to the research question.

10. Literature Review and Research Gap

A good literature review should not be a list of summaries.

Weak approach:

Study 1 found X.

Study 2 found Y.

Study 3 found Z.

Strong Approach

Organize literature by themes.

Example:

Theme 1:

Benefits of hybrid work.

Theme 2:

Challenges of hybrid collaboration.

Theme 3:

Industry differences.

Then identify:

What remains unresolved?

11. Moving From Literature Review to Research Problem

A literature review should justify the new study.

A strong ending explains:

  1. What is known.
  2. What is uncertain.
  3. Why the uncertainty matters.
  4. How the proposed study addresses it.

Example

Existing research:

  • Hybrid work improves flexibility.
  • Results differ by industry.

Gap:

  • Limited evidence after organizational policy changes.

Research need:

  • Study hybrid work effects in post-policy-change environments.

12. Research Problem Description Structure

A research problem description usually contains:

1. Ideal Situation

What should happen?

Example:

Everyone should have easy access to healthcare services.

2. Current Situation

What is happening now?

Example:

Many people report difficulty accessing services.

3. Consequences

Why does the problem matter?

Examples:

  • Poor health outcomes.
  • Increased healthcare burden.
  • Reduced service effectiveness.

4. Research Direction

What needs to be investigated?

Example:

Identify factors limiting healthcare access.

13. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1:

“A literature review is just summarizing papers.”

Reality:

A good review synthesizes evidence and builds an argument.

Misconception 2:

“More citations automatically mean a better review.”

Reality:

Quality, relevance, and synthesis matter more than citation count.

Misconception 3:

“Systematic reviews only collect journal articles.”

Reality:

Depending on the strategy, they may include:

  • Conference papers.
  • Reports.
  • Grey literature.

Misconception 4:

“Search terms can be created after searching.”

Reality:

A systematic review requires a documented and repeatable search strategy.

Misconception 5:

“The research gap appears automatically.”

Reality:

Researchers must analyze patterns, contradictions, and limitations to identify gaps.

14. Key Revision Summary

Systematic Literature Review Process

Define question → Plan search → Identify studies → Remove duplicates → Apply criteria → Evaluate quality → Extract data → Synthesize evidence → Identify gaps

Literature Review Helps Determine

  • Theory.
  • Variables.
  • Research direction.

Search Strategy

Correct order:

Concepts → Synonyms → Boolean operators → Database search

Research Gap Identification

Look for:

  • Missing contexts.
  • Conflicting findings.
  • Method limitations.
  • Outdated evidence.

Analysis Methods

Data Type Method
Interview text Thematic analysis
Numerical data Statistical analysis
Multiple studies Literature synthesis

Final Principle

A rigorous literature review is not a collection of summaries. It is a structured process that transforms existing knowledge into a foundation for new research.

A strong researcher moves from:

Existing evidence → Critical evaluation → Research gap → Theory and variables → New investigation.

Case Study

At BrightStar Ltd., a research team led by Priya is finalizing their report on remote work productivity. The guidelines require documenting the limitations of the current study – constraints in scope, sample, methodology, or context that may reduce the generalisability or applicability of the findings; outlining a Future Research Agenda – unresolved questions, unexplored variables, or methodological improvements suggested by the current study; compiling References: a complete, consistently formatted list of all sources cited in the report, organised alphabetically; and assembling Appendices: supporting materials not included in the main body – questionnaire instruments, raw data samples, supplementary tables, interview protocols, etc..Having completed the literature review as the process of systematically searching, critically evaluating, and synthesising existing published research on a specific topic, concept, or problem, the team now addresses feedback from stakeholders. Priya wants to highlight the single-department sample limitation to clarify boundary conditions. Marco, the operations manager, believes methodology constraints are secondary to actionable insights. Dr. Liu, the research methods professor, stresses a detailed Future Research Agenda that investigates team diversity and digital collaboration tools.The team faces a decision point. Option A: Emphasize scope and sample limitations to guide generalisability. Option B: Focus on methodological improvements, such as adopting a mixed methods design, to strengthen validity. Option C: Include both in the main report and reserve detailed survey instruments and interview protocols in Appendices. Students should analyze how each choice shapes reader interpretation, balances depth and scope, and supports BrightStar Ltd.’s strategic planning.

Comprehensive Notes: BrightStar Ltd. Research Report Limitations, Future Research Agenda, References, Appendices, and Research Communication

1. Case Overview

BrightStar Ltd. has completed a research study on remote work productivity. The research team, led by Priya, is preparing the final report and must ensure that the report communicates not only findings but also the boundaries, limitations, and future directions of the study.

The case focuses on four essential components of a strong research report:

  1. Limitations of the Current Study
  2. Future Research Agenda
  3. References
  4. Appendices

The team must decide how to present these elements so that readers can correctly interpret the findings and use them for strategic planning.

2. Role of the Final Research Report

A research report is not only a presentation of results. It must communicate:

  • What was studied.
  • How it was studied.
  • What conclusions can reasonably be drawn.
  • Where conclusions should be applied carefully.
  • What future research is needed.

A strong report balances:

Evidence → Interpretation → Boundaries → Future improvement

3. Limitations of the Current Study

Definition

Research limitations are constraints or weaknesses that may affect:

  • Generalisability.
  • Applicability.
  • Interpretation of findings.
  • Strength of conclusions.

Limitations do not mean the research is invalid. They define the conditions under which the findings should be interpreted.

4. Types of Research Limitations

4.1 Scope Limitations

Scope limitations define what the study covers and what it does not cover.

Example:

BrightStar studies remote work productivity but only examines:

  • One department.
  • One business unit.
  • One type of work.

The findings may not apply to:

  • Other departments.
  • Different job roles.
  • Other organizations.

4.2 Sample Limitations

Sample limitations relate to who participated in the study.

Example:

BrightStar used employees from only one department.

Impact:

The sample may not represent:

  • The entire company.
  • Different organizational roles.
  • Other industries.

4.3 Methodological Limitations

Methodological limitations relate to the research design and data collection methods.

Examples:

  • Survey-only approach.
  • Self-reported responses.
  • Lack of qualitative interviews.
  • Limited measurement tools.

4.4 Context Limitations

Context limitations relate to the environment in which the study was conducted.

Examples:

  • One company.
  • One geographic region.
  • Specific remote-work policies.
  • Particular organizational culture.

5. Importance of Reporting Limitations

A limitation section improves research credibility.

It shows that researchers:

  • Understand boundaries.
  • Avoid overclaiming.
  • Recognize uncertainty.

Example

Incorrect conclusion:

Remote work improves productivity for all companies.

Better conclusion:

Within BrightStar’s studied department, remote work was associated with improved productivity. Further research is required to determine whether this applies across other departments and organizations.

6. Priya’s Focus: Single-Department Sample Limitation

Priya wants to highlight:

The study used a single-department sample.

This is an important limitation because it affects generalisability.

Why Sample Boundaries Matter

Employees in different departments may differ in:

  • Work type.
  • Collaboration needs.
  • Technology usage.
  • Management style.

Example:

A software development team may adapt well to remote work.

A customer-support team may face different challenges.

Strategic Value of Mentioning This Limitation

It helps decision-makers understand:

  • Where findings can be applied.
  • Where caution is required.
  • What additional evidence is needed.

7. Future Research Agenda

Definition

A Future Research Agenda identifies unresolved questions and suggests directions for future studies.

It answers:

What should researchers investigate next?

8. Purpose of a Future Research Agenda

A good future research section:

  • Builds on current findings.
  • Identifies unanswered questions.
  • Suggests improvements.
  • Extends knowledge.

It converts limitations into opportunities.

Example

Current limitation:

Only one department was studied.

Future research:

Study multiple departments to compare how remote work affects different job roles.

9. Dr. Liu’s Recommendation: Future Research Directions

Dr. Liu emphasizes investigating:

9.1 Team Diversity

Future research could examine:

  • Does remote work affect diverse teams differently?
  • How do cultural differences influence collaboration?
  • Does team composition influence productivity outcomes?

9.2 Digital Collaboration Tools

Future research could explore:

  • Which tools improve productivity?
  • How does tool adoption affect teamwork?
  • Do communication platforms reduce remote-work challenges?

9.3 Methodological Improvements

Future studies could improve research design through:

  • Larger samples.
  • Multiple organizations.
  • Longitudinal studies.
  • Mixed methods.

10. References Section

Definition

References provide a complete list of all sources cited in the report.

Purpose

References allow readers to:

  • Verify evidence.
  • Locate original sources.
  • Evaluate research foundations.

Requirements

A good reference section should be:

Complete

Every cited source must appear.

Consistent

All sources should follow the same formatting style.

Examples:

  • APA style.
  • Harvard style.
  • IEEE style.

Alphabetical

References are usually organized by:

  • Author surname.

Example:

Anderson, J.

Brown, M.

Smith, R.

11. Appendices

Definition

Appendices contain supporting materials that are useful but too detailed for the main report.

Purpose

Appendices:

  • Maintain report readability.
  • Provide transparency.
  • Allow deeper examination.

Examples of Appendix Materials

1. Questionnaire Instruments

Example:

Survey questions used to measure remote work productivity.

2. Raw Data Samples

Example:

Sample responses or datasets.

3. Supplementary Tables

Example:

Detailed statistical outputs.

4. Interview Protocols

Example:

Questions used during qualitative interviews.

12. Main Report vs Appendix

A key reporting principle:

Main Report

Contains:

  • Important findings.
  • Major explanations.
  • Key limitations.
  • Strategic implications.

Appendix

Contains:

  • Detailed evidence.
  • Supporting documents.
  • Technical materials.

Why Not Include Everything in the Main Report?

Including all materials can:

  • Overload readers.
  • Hide key findings.
  • Reduce readability.

13. Decision Point: Evaluating Report Options

The team considers three approaches.

Option A: Emphasize Scope and Sample Limitations

Focus

Highlight:

  • Single-department sample.
  • Limits of generalisation.

Advantages

Improves Interpretation

Readers understand:

  • Where findings apply.
  • Where caution is needed.

Supports Responsible Decision-Making

Managers avoid applying findings incorrectly.

Limitation

May underemphasize:

  • Research improvement opportunities.
  • Methodological development.

Option B: Focus on Methodological Improvements

Focus

Recommend:

  • Mixed methods.
  • Better research designs.
  • Stronger validity.

Advantages

Improves future research quality.

Examples:

Current study:

Survey only.

Future study:

Survey + interviews.

Limitation

May shift attention away from current study boundaries.

Readers may not clearly understand:

  • Who was studied.
  • Where findings apply.

Option C: Include Both Limitations and Future Improvements, Use Appendices

Approach

Main report includes:

  • Study limitations.
  • Future research agenda.

Appendices contain:

  • Surveys.
  • Interview guides.
  • Supporting data.

Advantages

Balanced Communication

Readers receive:

  • Appropriate interpretation.
  • Future direction.
  • Technical transparency.

Better Strategic Planning

Managers understand:

  • Current evidence.
  • Research boundaries.
  • Next steps.

Most Comprehensive Approach

Option C provides the strongest reporting structure.

It balances:

  • Scope clarity.
  • Methodological improvement.
  • Reader usability.

14. Relationship Between Limitations and Future Research

Limitations and future research are connected.

A limitation identifies:

What the current study cannot answer.

Future research identifies:

How future studies can address it.

Example

Limitation:

Single department sample.

↓

Future research:

Study multiple departments.

Example

Limitation:

Survey-only methodology.

↓

Future research:

Use mixed methods.

15. Mixed Methods as a Future Improvement

Definition

Mixed methods combines:

  • Quantitative research.
  • Qualitative research.

Example

Current study:

Employee survey measuring productivity.

Future study:

Survey + interviews.

Benefits

Provides:

Quantitative Evidence

Answers:

  • How much?
  • How often?
  • What relationships exist?

Qualitative Understanding

Answers:

  • Why?
  • How?
  • What experiences explain the findings?

16. Research Quality and Validity

Generalisability

Question:

Can findings apply to other groups or settings?

Affected by:

  • Sample size.
  • Sample diversity.
  • Study context.

Internal Validity

Question:

Are conclusions supported by the research design?

Affected by:

  • Method quality.
  • Control of alternative explanations.

Construct Validity

Question:

Are concepts measured correctly?

Affected by:

  • Measurement design.
  • Research instruments.

17. Common Misconceptions

Exam Study Guide Appendix

Week 3 — Research Execution, Analysis, Reporting, Literature Review and Theory

How to use these notes

This week moves through the research process from execution to analysis and reporting, then explains literature review, theoretical frameworks, variables, hypotheses, and related project decisions. For exam answers, do three things: define the concept, explain its purpose/process, and add a business example.

1. Position of the execution stage in research

A typical research endeavour has five broad stages:

  1. Planning — define the business problem, translate it into a research problem, identify concepts/variables, propose relationships and hypotheses, identify stakeholders, decide whose views matter, and determine whom and how many to study.
  2. Execution — carry out the plan: meet participants, collect the required data, verify its quality, store it, clean it, and arrange it for analysis.
  3. Analysis — apply methods capable of answering the research questions and establishing the authenticity of findings.
  4. Reporting — communicate methods, evidence, findings, contributions, limitations, and recommendations in a structured form.
  5. Decision-making — use the findings to address the original business problem.

The execution stage is primarily about data collection and data management. Its key elements are:

  • choosing and applying a data-collection method;
  • preventing or minimising bias, prejudice, and error;
  • storing data in a usable format;
  • cleaning and preparing data by resolving errors, inconsistencies, and missing values;
  • documenting precautions so decision-makers can judge the credibility of the results.

2. Primary and secondary data

2.1 Definitions

  • Primary data is collected directly by the researcher from the field or participants, for the first time and for the specific study.
  • Secondary data was collected previously by another researcher, agency, or source—usually for another purpose—and is reused in the current study.

The classification is relative to the study, not inherent in the data. Customer responses collected by Researcher A are primary for A; the same responses are secondary when reused by Researcher B.

2.2 Examples and sources

  • Surveying customers directly about their feelings toward a product produces primary data.
  • Census data on customer age/income, a telephone directory or contact list, economic data, market reports, and third-party consumer-behaviour studies are secondary sources.
  • Businesses also possess extensive internal data: sales reports, customer types, demand fluctuations, service studies, economic information, and market changes.

Internal data may itself have different origins:

  • Data generated through routine organisational operations—such as store-wise sales, visitor types, or seasonal demand—is internally held and originally collected by the organisation. The lecturer described this as internal data of a primary nature.
  • Reports prepared by KPMG, EY, Deloitte, or another consulting agency are stored internally but were not collected by the business; they remain secondary in origin.

Before collecting new data, determine whether adequate secondary data can answer the research question. Primary data is necessary when secondary evidence is absent, insufficient, outdated, contextually unsuitable, or unable to measure the required attitudes, perceptions, feelings, or opinions.

2.3 Three primary-data methods

  1. Observation — observe a phenomenon such as how a customer moves through a store or makes a purchase; record the observations as text and analyse them for behavioural patterns. This corresponds to the “exposure” source of research understanding mentioned by the lecturer.
  2. Interview — use an interview schedule to collect information from experts or other relevant people. Interview extracts are primary, usually textual, data.
  3. Survey — approach a comparatively large number of critical participants with a questionnaire. A questionnaire can collect perceptions and convert them into structured responses.

Practical use case: A retailer investigating falling repeat purchases could combine transaction history (secondary/internal operational data), in-store observation (primary), interviews with store managers (primary qualitative), and a customer satisfaction questionnaire (primary quantitative).

3. Data storage, coding, cleaning, and preparation

3.1 Converting responses into analysable form

Collected data may remain textual or may be transformed into numbers. A statement such as “I like this product” can use a five-point scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree; categories can be assigned numeric codes. Numeric conversion makes storage, comparison, mathematical analysis, and transfer into statistical tools easier and is essential for quantitative analysis.

For spreadsheet storage, think of an m × n matrix:

  • rows represent the m respondents or observations;
  • columns represent the n questions/variables;
  • each cell stores a respondent’s answer to a question.

Long variable names should receive concise, consistent codes. If five statements measure satisfaction, they might be SAT1, SAT2, … SAT5. Qualitative material may instead be retained as transcripts, field notes, images, audio, or video.

3.2 Data-quality problems

  • Pattern/straight-line responses: the respondent gives the same answer, such as “neutral” or “strongly agree,” to all 20 items. This raises doubt about whether the respondent applied their mind.
  • Non-response/missing value: an item is omitted.
  • Invalid response: the permitted scale is 1–5 but another value or text is entered.
  • Inconsistency/error: answers conflict or are entered incorrectly.

Cleaning means identifying these problems, correcting them when a defensible correction is possible, coding consistently, and excluding unusable responses where necessary. Data must finally be stored in a form accepted by the selected analysis tool.

Exam caution: Do not say cleaning merely “deletes bad data.” It includes validation, correction, coding, treatment of missingness, and reasoned exclusion.

4. Bias in data collection and research

Bias can arise from sample selection, measurement, the researcher, participants, or interpretation. Researchers must anticipate it before data collection, minimise it during collection, and explain the precautions in the report.

4.1 Selection and sampling biases

Bias Meaning Transcript example / implication
Sampling bias Sample selection is not sufficiently random or representative of the target population. A landline telephone survey excludes many younger mobile-phone users; sample findings may not represent the customer population.
Non-response bias People who refuse/avoid participation differ systematically, in a research-relevant way, from participants. Unhealthy people may avoid a health-practices survey, leaving an overly healthy respondent group.
Volunteer/self-selection bias People with particular characteristics choose to participate, skewing the respondent pool and crowding out other perspectives. Highly enthusiastic customers may dominate an optional product-feedback survey.
Attrition bias Participants drop out after a longitudinal study or experiment starts, and leavers differ significantly from those who remain. Participants experiencing adverse effects leave a drug-effectiveness study before completion.

4.2 Information and measurement biases

Management research often studies latent variables—intrinsic constructs such as perceptions, feelings, happiness, pleasure, attitudes, and beliefs that cannot be measured directly like height or weight. They require indicators and instruments, so measurement error is especially important.

  • Measurement bias: flaws, ambiguity, inconsistency, or inaccuracy in the tool or collection method prevent the intended phenomenon from being measured correctly. An ambiguous questionnaire confuses respondents and produces erroneous answers.
  • Recall bias: participants cannot accurately or completely remember past events. Asking for details from five years ago invites guessing or reconstructed memories; use appropriate recall periods and avoid unnecessarily remote questions.
  • Observer/researcher bias: a researcher’s expectations or beliefs unconsciously influence collection or later interpretation—for example, by giving clues about a preferred answer.
  • Interviewer bias: the interviewer’s tone, body language, phrasing, recording, or interpretation systematically influences responses. It overlaps with observer bias but is specific to the interview interaction.

4.3 Response biases

  • Social desirability bias: participants report what society approves rather than their true behaviour or belief—for example, expressing strong support for charity regardless of actual conduct.
  • Acquiescence bias: a tendency to agree or answer “yes” regardless of the item’s content.
  • Demand-characteristics bias: once participants infer the purpose or expected result of the study, they alter answers to fit that expectation.

4.4 Analysis and cognitive biases

  • Confirmation bias: the researcher seeks, interprets, or favours evidence that confirms a pre-existing belief or expected hypothesis outcome.
  • Survivorship bias: analysis focuses only on cases that succeeded or survived and ignores failures. Studying only successful firms cannot reveal the complete causes of success and failure.

Practical controls: representative/random sampling where feasible, multiple contact attempts, dropout analysis, validated and pilot-tested instruments, neutral wording, standard interviewer scripts/training, blinding where feasible, triangulation, explicit inclusion/exclusion rules, audit trails, and reporting both supportive and contradictory evidence.

5. Quantitative and qualitative analysis

The analysis choice should be anticipated at planning stage because it depends on the research purpose and the form of data to be collected.

Dimension Quantitative analysis Qualitative analysis
Data Numbers: counts, scores, percentages Non-numeric material: transcripts, open-ended responses, field notes, images, video/audio
Purpose Measure magnitude, test hypotheses, establish associations, compare groups, forecast Explore meanings, experiences, reasons, language, and context
Typical questions What? How much? How many? To what extent does X affect Y? Why? How does an experience or meaning emerge?
Typical outputs Tables, charts, graphs, p-values, correlations, estimates, forecasts Codes, categories, themes, networks, rich description, narratives
Tool style Excel/SPSS/jamovi/Minitab and graphical interfaces; R/RStudio and code-based tools NVivo and other qualitative-data-analysis/text-mining tools

“How” appears in both: quantitative analysis expresses how much one unit of X changes Y, while qualitative analysis narrates the mechanism, meaning, or experience.

The methods below are illustrative, not exhaustive.

5.1 Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics summarise and describe a dataset:

  • central tendency: mean, median, mode;
  • dispersion: range, variance, standard deviation;
  • position: percentiles and quartiles; the median is the 50th percentile, dividing ordered data into two halves;
  • shape/symmetry: skewness and kurtosis.

A distribution describes how data points are scattered around a central value. Data may be symmetrical or non-symmetrical.

5.2 Inferential and comparative methods

  • Independent-samples t-test: tests whether the means of two independent groups differ statistically—for example, satisfaction among male versus female customers, two store regions, or average sales under website layout A versus B (A/B testing).
  • ANOVA (analysis of variance): compares means across three or more groups.
  • Chi-square test: tests association between categorical variables—for example, region (South/East/West) and preferred product (A/B/C). Do not define chi-square simply as a comparison of two numerical means.
  • Correlation: assesses whether variables move together and the strength/direction of association; it does not by itself establish a directional causal model.
  • Regression: estimates the relationship of a dependent variable with one or more independent variables, supports forecasting, supplies direction, and helps identify the strongest predictor. Example: forecast sales using advertising spend, price, and competitor activity.

5.3 Time-series analysis

Analyses observations collected over time to identify trend, cycles, and seasonality and forecast future values from history. Uses include future sales, stock values, monthly electricity consumption, and demand planning.

5.4 Data mining methods

  • Market-basket/association analysis: finds items or sequences frequently purchased together, potentially using the Apriori algorithm. If milk and bread co-occur, a retailer can design bundles, shelf placement, and recommendations.
  • Cluster analysis: an unsupervised method that groups similar customers, products, transactions, or geographic areas based on characteristics. Uses include target-market segmentation and pattern discovery.

5.5 Qualitative methods

  • Content analysis: systematically codes and categorises communication in text, audio, or video to discover patterns and draw inferences. Uses include customer feedback, social-media posts, and competitor advertisements to understand brand perception or sentiment.
  • Thematic analysis: identifies, analyses, and reports recurring themes in interview transcripts and open-ended responses—for example, themes explaining employee reactions to a new policy.
  • Narrative/discourse analysis: studies language, stories, and context.
  • Sentiment analysis, text mining, and topic modelling: useful for voluntarily produced reviews such as Glassdoor data; reviews can also be clustered to classify employee/customer groups.

The lecturer emphasised that unsolicited reviews can be valuable because customers or employees provide them voluntarily, without the direct compulsion of a research interview, though their representativeness must still be assessed.

6. Structure of a research report

A strong report is structured so a decision-maker can understand and trust the work.

6.1 Title

Be precise and clear. Indicate, where useful, the central concepts, context, and stakeholder perspective.

6.2 Abstract

A concise summary of the entire research activity. It should cover:

  • what was studied;
  • why it was needed and justified;
  • how it was conducted;
  • who participated and/or who benefits;
  • one or two overarching key findings;
  • how the findings benefit the business or relevant stakeholders.

6.3 Introduction

Include:

  • central concepts;
  • background/context and why the study is necessary;
  • relevant stakeholder entities and those affected;
  • origin of the research problem in a business or social problem;
  • problem statement: ideal situation, actual situation, the gap, and consequences;
  • research questions;
  • research gap and why previous solutions cannot simply be applied;
  • objectives;
  • anticipated/potential contribution.

Context should clarify geography, time, variables, and stakeholder/entity boundaries. A poor introduction makes later methodological choices difficult to understand.

A gap may be:

  • theoretical: existing explanations omit newly important causal variables or no longer explain the phenomenon adequately;
  • contextual: knowledge is missing for a geography, stakeholder, sector, or period.

6.4 Literature review

Explain the evolution of concepts, relevant theories, constructs/variables, major prior findings, limitations, contradictions, and new phenomena that challenge earlier knowledge. It justifies the new study by showing why existing findings are not wholly transferable to the current context.

The lecturer treated a construct as approximately a variable for introductory understanding, while noting that a methodological distinction exists.

6.5 Theoretical framework

State the adopted theory/model, important variables or constructs, theoretical and operational definitions, proposed relationships, assumptions, the phenomenon explained, and background/control variables needed for generalisation.

6.6 Methodology/research design

Explain:

  • research type: exploratory, descriptive, or causal;
  • whether the design is quantitative, qualitative, mixed, experimental, etc.;
  • population, sampling unit, sampling method, sample size, and scientific basis for that size;
  • data type/source and collection process;
  • collection instrument (e.g., questionnaire);
  • bias/error controls and data-quality standards;
  • selected analytical methods.

6.7 Findings and discussion

Present findings logically, then interpret them. Discussion is essential to credibility, particularly when a finding is new or contradicts prior research. Explain it in light of contextual realities so decision-makers can act with confidence.

6.8 Contributions

  • Theoretical: validates, challenges, augments, or extends an existing theory.
  • Practical/business/social: helps solve the investigated problem or improves stakeholder outcomes.
  • Policy: informs organisational or public policy.
  • Academic/methodological: advances research practice or knowledge.

Separating contribution types makes their value explicit.

6.9 Limitations and future research

Disclose remaining limitations even after bias controls. This defines where findings may not apply, protects responsible decision-making, and helps future researchers replicate the study while correcting weaknesses. Then present unanswered issues and extensions as a future research agenda.

Typical limitations:

  • scope: a narrow phenomenon, function, period, or set of variables;
  • sample: small, non-representative, or drawn from one department/group;
  • method: reliance on self-report, cross-sectional design, one method, or weak instrument;
  • context: one firm, industry, country, or unusual event.

6.10 References and appendices

  • References: complete, consistently formatted, alphabetically organised acknowledgement of every cited source; they provide academic credit, transparency, and traceability.
  • Appendices: detailed supporting material that is important for verification but would interrupt the main narrative—questionnaires, interview protocols, raw-data samples, supplementary tables, coding guides, and other instruments.

The main report should contain evidence needed to understand and decide; appendices hold audit detail. Do not hide a crucial finding in an appendix.

7. Literature review: definition, purposes, and types

7.1 Definition

A literature review systematically or critically searches, evaluates, and combines existing research on a topic, concept, or problem to understand current knowledge and guide the solution to the problem under investigation. It reveals the direction, scope, limitations, and methods of extant research.

It can support every element of the “5W1H” of research:

  • Why: problem, question, and gap;
  • Where/when: context and time;
  • What: concepts/variables;
  • Who: participants and beneficiaries;
  • How: methods.

When clarity is lacking, the researcher can learn through three broad sources stressed in the lectures: experts, exposure/observation, and existing research reports.

Purposes include understanding the current state of research; defining the research problem; identifying gaps; identifying variables and prior relationships; narrating context convincingly; selecting methodology; and building a theoretical foundation.

7.2 Review types

  • Narrative/traditional review: broad overview of knowledge, theories, and common viewpoints; selection and synthesis may be flexible and narrative.
  • Rapid review: abbreviated process used where time/resources are limited; useful for quick decisions but sacrifices comprehensiveness.
  • Scoping review: maps the breadth, concepts, evidence types, and gaps in a wide or emerging field rather than narrowly estimating an effect.
  • Systematic literature review (SLR): answers a specific, well-defined research question through transparent, reproducible identification, quality evaluation, and synthesis of all relevant evidence.
  • Structured literature review: applies an explicit structure to selection and extraction, often recording research question, location, participants, method, and findings.
  • Research synthesis: integrates an existing evidence base.
  • Meta-analysis: statistically combines quantitative results from independent studies to estimate a more precise overall effect—for example, combining treatment-effect estimates across studies. It is not simply a narrative summary.

The continuum runs from few formal rules (traditional/rapid/narrative) to strict, transparent rules (SLR/structured review/meta-analysis). The lecturer placed greatest academic emphasis on SLR.

8. Conducting a systematic literature review

8.1 Start with a review question and protocol

An SLR begins with a clear research question, the need/background for answering it, scope, intended approach, search terms, databases, inclusion/exclusion criteria, quality assessment, and planned synthesis.

Identify the main concepts in the question, then create synonyms and related terms. Combine them with Boolean logic:

  • AND narrows by requiring both concepts: customer satisfaction AND packaged food.
  • OR broadens by accepting alternatives: (Gen Z OR young consumer).

A planned string might be: (customer satisfaction OR consumer satisfaction) AND (packaged food OR FMCG) AND (India OR emerging economy).

8.2 Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Predefine what will count. Criteria may concern date, geography, population/generation, language, publication type, industry, method, or relevance.

Transcript example: for post-COVID satisfaction with packaged foods among Gen Z in India/developing economies, exclude pre-2019 studies and irrelevant developed-country contexts; include studies focused on India, developing economies, and Gen Z. Criteria must follow the question rather than being adjusted merely to produce a preferred result.

8.3 Databases

Possible sources include Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, JSTOR, and PubMed. Select based on disciplinary coverage and review scope. Searches across multiple databases create duplicates, which must be removed.

8.4 PIECES framework

PIECES provides a staged view of SLR:

  1. Planning: define question, rationale, approach, scope, keywords, databases, and eligibility criteria.
  2. Identifying: search and identify studies meeting preliminary criteria.
  3. Evaluating: assess relevance, quality, and risk of bias; eliminate evidence that is not trustworthy under the protocol.
  4. Collecting and combining: extract relevant fields/themes and integrate the shortlisted evidence.
  5. Explaining: interpret results in context and discuss strengths, weaknesses, and important observations.
  6. Summarising: transparently report methods and findings.

8.5 Screening and PRISMA

PRISMA means Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses. Its flow documents:

  1. Identification: records found through databases/registers and other sources; duplicates and clearly unsuitable records are removed.
  2. Screening: titles and abstracts are screened; potentially eligible full texts are sought and assessed.
  3. Included: the final set enters qualitative synthesis and, where appropriate, quantitative meta-analysis.

At each stage, report the number retained/excluded and reasons for full-text exclusion. This makes selection transparent and reproducible.

8.6 Structured extraction and synthesis

For each eligible study, extract comparable fields: citation, question/objective, theory, variables, context, participants/sample, design, data, measures, analysis, results, limitations, and quality/risk-of-bias judgement. A structured table prevents selective memory and supports defensible comparison.

Thematic synthesis process:

  1. familiarise yourself with the evidence;
  2. code relevant passages/findings;
  3. group codes into themes;
  4. interpret patterns, contrasts, and their implications.

8.7 TCCM reporting framework

The transcript sometimes renders the acronym imperfectly; the components described are Theory, Context, Characteristics, and Methodology (TCCM):

  • Theory: dominant models/concepts/frameworks and unexplored theoretical perspectives.
  • Context: country, sector/industry, period, and setting; reveals transferability and contextual gaps.
  • Characteristics: focal constructs, variables, themes/domains, and their mapped relationships.
  • Methodology: design, quantitative/qualitative approach, data collection, sample, and analytical methods; reveals dominant methods and the need for methodological diversity.

PIECES organises the review process; TCCM organises how the body of research is analysed/reported.

9. Finding and articulating a research gap

A literature review should not end as a list of summaries. Compare the literature to identify what remains unknown, under-tested, contradictory, context-bound, or methodologically weak.

A strong problem/gap narrative follows:

  1. Ideal situation: what should happen according to theory, organisational goals, or accepted practice.
  2. Current situation: what actually occurs.
  3. Consequences: why the discrepancy matters.
  4. Research direction: what question/relationship/context must now be investigated.

Possible gaps include missing variables/relationships, an under-studied population or geography, outdated evidence after environmental change, inconsistent findings, dependence on one method, weak samples, or theory not tested in a new context.

Practical example: Technology-acceptance studies may establish perceived usefulness and ease of use in conventional systems, but agentic AI adoption by an agency workforce may be untested. Applying and validating UTAUT/TAM in that new context is a contextual confirmation or extension—not proof that no earlier technology research exists.

10. Theory, theoretical frameworks, and variables

10.1 Theory

A theory is a systematic explanation of a phenomenon through relationships among factors/variables contributing to its occurrence or development. A phenomenon such as buying can be explained as a sequence involving need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives/value, selection, and purchase.

Existing examples mentioned:

  • Technology Acceptance Model (TAM): Davis (1989); technology adoption is driven particularly by perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Later technology-acceptance work includes Venkatesh and UTAUT.
  • Diffusion of Innovation for adoption/spread.
  • Value-Belief-Norm theory and Stages of Change in environmental/behavioural contexts.
  • AIDA: Attention → Interest → Desire → Action. If an expensive advertisement gains exposure but does not generate interest, desire, or purchase, the expected theory/process breaks at an identifiable point.

A researcher may adopt an existing theory, validate it in a new context, extend/challenge it, or develop a new explanation. A model gives direction by identifying what should cause the desired outcome and what must be measured.

10.2 Five variable roles

  1. Dependent variable (DV): the effect/outcome to be explained. Example: sales or satisfaction.
  2. Independent variable (IV): the presumed cause/predictor. Example: advertisement effectiveness or product quality.
  3. Mediator: explains how/why an IV produces a DV. Advertisement → perceived value → purchase/sales; perceived value carries the effect.
  4. Moderator: changes the strength or direction of the IV–DV relationship. Trust in advertising strengthens the effect of ad effectiveness on purchase; distrust weakens it.
  5. Control variable: a third factor whose influence is held constant so the focal relationship can be estimated/generalised. Gender, age, income, or education may affect ad response and therefore be controlled when justified.

Do not confuse mediation and moderation: a mediator is the mechanism; a moderator specifies when/for whom/how strongly the relation occurs.

10.3 Building a model from a business problem

The live-session retail example follows this chain:

  1. Business problem: store footfall is declining.
  2. Desired effect: repeat visits/footfall must increase.
  3. Candidate DV: customer satisfaction and/or loyalty, because satisfied/loyal customers are more likely to return.
  4. Inventory possible causes: established theory, prior studies, experts, and business experience suggest product quality, price, variety, service, store ambience/location, offers, display, payment options, etc.
  5. Classify variables: determine IVs, mediators, moderators, and justified controls such as age.
  6. Propose hypotheses: e.g., perceived product quality positively affects satisfaction.
  7. Collect measurements: assess each construct using suitable questionnaire items or operational data.
  8. Analyse relationships: identify strong and weak predictors; a weak/non-supported link suggests where corrective action is needed.

An illustrated model was: product attributes and store attributes → perceived value → satisfaction; variety-seeking and price sensitivity may moderate relevant paths. Data must measure every construct before estimating the hypothesised relationships.

11. Hypotheses, significance, estimation, and generalisation

A hypothesis is a logical, testable assumption made at the beginning about an expected outcome. Theories comprise multiple hypothesised relationships.

Two broad hypothesis uses were emphasised:

  • Relational hypotheses: whether variables are related—for example, product quality affects satisfaction.
  • Group-difference hypotheses: whether a variable differs across demographic groups such as gender, age, or income.

Classical statistical form:

  • Null hypothesis (H₀): no significant relationship/difference.
  • Alternative hypothesis (H₁): a significant relationship/difference exists.

In business research, the desired practical result is often support for the alternative because the business expects an action such as a better advertisement to raise purchase intention. Contemporary articles may state only the directional/alternative research hypothesis, but students should know how to write both forms.

A sample result must be used to infer a population. A path coefficient/beta expresses estimated relationship strength; the live session used p < .05 as the conventional benchmark for statistical significance/generalisation. Do not equate statistical significance with practical importance, causality, or proof; also inspect effect magnitude, design, assumptions, and context.

Estimation is using sample statistics to infer population parameters, accompanied by concepts such as confidence level and confidence interval. If a true census measures every member, sample-to-population inference is unnecessary.

12. Questionnaires and measurement

Many marketing and HR constructs are latent; directly observable metrics (purchase count, revenue, frequency) can be counted, while beliefs, attitudes, perceived quality, and satisfaction require carefully designed measures.

A typical questionnaire may contain:

  1. Respondent profile: age, gender, income, education, social background, etc.
  2. Exploratory/behaviour section: visit frequency, online/offline preference, decision style, preferred format, and reasons.
  3. Construct measures: multiple statements measuring each model variable.
  4. Optional open-ended questions: recommendations or service expectations.

The number of questions is determined by objectives, constructs, required depth, respondent burden, and validated scale requirements—not a universal fixed number. Reuse/adapt standard questionnaires from literature where appropriate and document the source and adaptation.

13. Project and live-session applications

  • A project proposal is a plan and was described as not itself graded; the final report is evaluated. Absence of a non-acceptance message was treated in the session as acceptance, while revisions can be made as concepts become clearer.
  • Design of Experiments (DOE), including Taguchi designs, is acceptable where suited to the problem. The report must still explain why an experiment is required and connect it to the business problem.
  • Glassdoor/review data can be valid textual secondary data for sentiment analysis, text mining, topic modelling, and clustering, subject to source-quality and sampling limitations.
  • UTAUT for agentic-AI workforce acceptance is a legitimate use of an existing theory in a new context; perceptual data would ordinarily be collected to test it.
  • A topic such as “digital marketing strategies” is too broad unless narrowed to a specific outcome/question—for example, emerging strategies for customer engagement or testing drivers of engagement.
  • For a virtual project, begin with objectives and determine data requirements; then check whether appropriate secondary data exists and whether it is free or paid.
  • FMCG input-cost inflation: possible secondary sources include CPI and government/RBI data; CMIE was mentioned as another potential source, possibly paid.
  • Mutual-fund investment among salaried employees: perception can be studied by surveying salaried employees; precise investment-volume data may require access to fund providers.
  • Institutional funding in PE/VC and firm effects: viable in principle but likely dependent on suitable secondary-data access and a clear analytical design.
  • Multiple controls can be included simultaneously as covariates in SPSS/jamovi or other modelling tools. First justify/test whether a candidate factor materially changes the focal effect; t-tests/ANOVA can inspect group differences, while the final model can control multiple covariates together.

14. Review/case augmentation: limitations, future research, references, and appendices

The source’s BrightStar Ltd. review/case reinforces final-report judgement. A good report should not choose between honesty about limitations and useful future action; it should combine both while preserving readability.

14.1 BrightStar reporting logic

If a study of collaboration/remote work used only one department, disclose the sample and scope limitation. Findings may have internal value but limited generalisability across departments, functions, organisations, or cultures. The limitation does not automatically invalidate the study; it defines the boundary of inference.

Then convert boundaries into a future research agenda:

  • replicate across departments and organisations;
  • include more diverse teams and demographic/professional backgrounds;
  • test digital collaboration tools and remote/hybrid conditions;
  • use longitudinal designs to examine change over time;
  • add qualitative interviews/focus groups to explain quantitative patterns;
  • improve measures and sampling.

The strongest reporting option is therefore:

  1. disclose material limitations in the main report;
  2. explain their likely impact on interpretation;
  3. connect each limitation to a feasible future study;
  4. keep actionable insights visible;
  5. place bulky instruments, raw-data samples, and supplementary tables in appendices.

14.2 Mixed methods

Mixed methods combine:

  • quantitative breadth: magnitude, frequency, group comparison, relationship testing, and potential generalisation;
  • qualitative depth: meaning, mechanism, context, and participant experience.

For remote-work research, a survey might quantify collaboration, engagement, or productivity while interviews explain isolation, communication barriers, tool fatigue, trust, or managerial support. Combining them gives a fuller basis for decisions, provided the integration strategy is explicit.

14.3 Validity concepts

  • Generalisability/external validity: whether findings extend beyond the studied sample/context.
  • Internal validity: whether the observed effect is credibly attributable to the proposed cause rather than confounding factors.
  • Construct validity: whether the instrument truly measures the intended construct.

A single-department sample principally threatens generalisability; ambiguous satisfaction items threaten construct validity; an uncontrolled third variable threatens internal validity.

14.4 Common misconceptions corrected

  • Reporting limitations does not necessarily weaken a report; transparent boundaries strengthen credibility.
  • A future research agenda is not a vague wish list; it should follow directly from gaps and limitations.
  • References are not optional background reading; every cited work must be traceable and consistently listed.
  • Appendices are not a dumping ground; include only relevant verification/support material.
  • Quantitative methods are not inherently superior to qualitative methods; fit depends on question and data.
  • Mixed methods is not merely collecting two kinds of data; findings must be purposefully integrated.
  • A literature review is not an article-by-article summary; it requires evaluation and synthesis.
  • Meta-analysis is not synonymous with SLR; it is a statistical synthesis that may form part of a systematic review when compatible quantitative studies exist.

15. High-yield exam comparisons

Primary vs secondary data

Primary is collected specifically and directly for the present inquiry; secondary pre-exists and is reused. Primary offers fit and control but costs time/resources; secondary is fast and economical but may be outdated, differently defined, or contextually mismatched.

Quantitative vs qualitative analysis

Quantitative measures magnitude and tests numerical relationships; qualitative explains meaning, experience, and mechanism. The research question and data—not researcher preference—determine the choice.

Correlation vs regression

Correlation describes association; regression specifies an outcome and one or more predictors, estimates direction/magnitude, and supports prediction. Neither alone proves causation without an appropriate design.

t-test vs ANOVA vs chi-square

  • t-test: numerical mean across two independent groups;
  • ANOVA: numerical mean across three or more groups;
  • chi-square: association between categorical variables.

Mediator vs moderator vs control

  • mediator = mechanism (“how/why”);
  • moderator = boundary condition (“when/for whom/how strongly”);
  • control = competing influence held constant.

Narrative review vs SLR vs meta-analysis

  • narrative = broad, flexible synthesis;
  • SLR = question-driven, transparent, reproducible evidence selection and synthesis;
  • meta-analysis = statistical pooling of compatible quantitative estimates.

16. Exam-ready integrative example

Problem: A retailer’s footfall is declining.

Planning and literature: Define the ideal and current footfall, consequences, stakeholders, and question. Review retail-satisfaction and loyalty literature using databases, explicit keywords, criteria, PRISMA screening, and TCCM extraction. Identify whether product/store attributes, value, satisfaction, loyalty, variety-seeking, and price sensitivity are supported and whether the local context is under-studied.

Framework: Product attributes and store attributes (IVs) → perceived value (mediator) → satisfaction/loyalty (DVs); price sensitivity or variety-seeking may moderate paths; age/income may be controls.

Execution: Use transaction data plus a representative customer survey, observation, and possibly interviews. Pilot neutral questionnaire items, standardise administration, code Likert items, monitor missing and straight-line responses, and document bias controls.

Analysis: Describe distributions; use t-test/ANOVA for group differences, regression for predictors, time series for footfall trends, basket analysis for co-purchases, and thematic analysis for interview/open-response explanations.

Report/decision: Present findings and contextual discussion, contributions, limitations, actions (assortment, ambience, pricing, offers, payment options), and a future agenda. Put instruments and supplemental detail in appendices and cite all sources consistently.
