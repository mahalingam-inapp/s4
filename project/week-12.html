1. Overview

This quiz focuses on building and interpreting regression models for business research.

The major concepts covered are:

  • Regression with predictors and controls.
  • Theory-based control variables.
  • Moderation analysis using interaction terms.
  • Composite index construction.
  • Standardization and z-scores.
  • Interpreting standardized coefficients.
  • Evaluating robustness of regression effects.

The central principle:

A statistical model should be driven by theory and research objectives, not simply by the availability of variables.

2. Regression as a Predictive Model

Purpose of Regression

Regression estimates how one or more predictors relate to an outcome variable.

General form:

Outcome \= Intercept + Predictor effects + Error

Example:

Predict:

Customer Satisfaction

from:

  • Store Attributes.
  • Product Attributes.

Regression helps answer:

  • Which predictors influence the outcome?
  • How strong are those relationships?
  • Does each predictor contribute after controlling for others?

3. Multiple Linear Regression

When to use

Multiple regression is appropriate when:

  • There is one continuous dependent variable.
  • There are multiple predictors.

Example:

Customer Satisfaction predicted by:

  • Store Attributes.
  • Product Attributes.
  • Age.
  • Gender.
  • Income.

Benefits:

  • Estimates unique contribution of each predictor.
  • Controls for overlap among predictors.
  • Provides a comprehensive explanatory model.

4. Control Variables in Regression

Purpose of Controls

Control variables are included to account for alternative explanations.

They help answer:

“Does the main predictor still matter after accounting for other relevant factors?”

Example:

Research question:

Do Store Attributes affect Satisfaction?

Potential control:

Age.

Reason:

Age may influence satisfaction independently.

Regression model:

Satisfaction \=
Store Attributes +
Age

Interpretation:

The Store Attributes coefficient represents the effect after accounting for age.

5. Theory-Based Controls

A major principle:

Include controls because theory suggests they matter, not simply because data is available.

Example:

Appropriate control:

Age, if previous research suggests age affects satisfaction.

Inappropriate control:

Shop preference, if:

  • It is not a cause of satisfaction.
  • It does not confound the attribute measures.

6. Why Not Add Every Available Variable?

Including unnecessary controls can:

  • Reduce model clarity.
  • Increase complexity.
  • Reduce statistical power.
  • Introduce noise.

A good regression model is:

Not the model with the most variables.

It is the model with the most theoretically justified variables.

8. Composite Scores and Index Construction

Researchers often combine multiple measures into one overall score.

Example:

Creating a:

“Store Quality Index”

from:

  • Waiting time.
  • Product availability.
  • Number of items.
  • Complaints.

Purpose:

Create a single meaningful construct.

9. Problem of Different Measurement Units

Different variables may use different scales.

Example:

Metric Unit
Waiting time Minutes
Distance km
Sales Rupees
Inventory Count

If summed directly:

Variables with larger numerical values dominate.

Example:

₹10,000 sales overwhelms a 5-minute waiting time.

Solution:

Standardization.

10. Standardization Using z-scores

A z-score transforms variables into a common scale.

Formula:

z \= (value - mean) / standard deviation

After standardization:

  • Mean \= 0.
  • Standard deviation \= 1.

Benefits:

  • Removes unit differences.
  • Makes variables comparable.
  • Prevents large-unit variables dominating.

11. Direction Alignment Before Combining Measures

A composite score must have consistent interpretation.

Desired:

Higher score \= Better performance.

Problem:

Some measures may have opposite meanings.

Example:

Higher is better:

  • Products available.
  • Sales.

Higher is worse:

  • Waiting time.
  • Complaints.

Before combining:

Reverse-code negative indicators.

Example:

Waiting time:

Low waiting time \= good.

After reverse coding:

Higher score \= better.

12. Correct Composite Index Process

Steps:

Step 1

Identify indicators.

Example:

Store metrics.

Step 2

Align direction.

Reverse-code lower-is-better measures.

Step 3

Standardize variables.

Convert to z-scores.

Step 4

Combine.

Average or sum standardized values.

13. Moderation Analysis

Purpose

Moderation tests whether the relationship between two variables changes depending on another variable.

Question:

“Does the effect of X on Y depend on Z?”

Example:

Store Attributes → Satisfaction

Moderated by:

Variety Seeking.

14. Testing Moderation

Moderation is tested using an interaction term.

Regression model:

Satisfaction \=
Store Attributes +
Variety Seeking +
(Store Attributes × Variety Seeking)

The key term:

Interaction effect.

If interaction is significant:

The effect of Store Attributes differs depending on Variety Seeking.

15. Interpreting Stable Moderation

Suppose:

Before controls:

Interaction p \< 0.05

After demographic controls:

Interaction p \< 0.05

Conclusion:

The moderation effect is robust.

Meaning:

The effect remains after accounting for:

  • Age.
  • Gender.
  • Education.
  • Income.

16. Adding Demographic Controls to Models

Researchers often compare:

Model 1

Main predictors only.

Example:

Satisfaction \=
Store Attributes

Model 2

Predictors + controls.

Example:

Satisfaction \=
Store Attributes +
Age +
Gender +
Income

Researchers examine:

  1. Change in coefficients.
  2. Change in explained variance.

17. Evaluating Regression Robustness

If a coefficient changes very little after controls:

Example:

Before:

β \= 0.35

After:

β \= 0.345

Interpretation:

The relationship is stable.

Term:

Robust estimate.

Meaning:

The predictor effect is not explained by demographic differences.

18. Understanding ΔR²

Adding controls may increase explained variance.

Example:

Model 1:

R² \= 0.40

Model 2:

R² \= 0.45

ΔR²:

0.05

Meaning:

Controls explain additional variation.

Important:

Controls do not need to be significant individually to improve understanding.

19. Standardized vs Unstandardized Coefficients

Unstandardized coefficients (B)

Represent effects in original units.

Example:

A one-point increase in Store Attributes increases Satisfaction by 0.4 points.

Useful for:

Practical interpretation.

Standardized coefficients (Beta)

Convert variables to standard deviation units.

Useful for:

Comparing relative strength of predictors.

20. Why Use Standardized Beta Coefficients?

Predictors may have different units.

Example:

Predictor 1:

1–5 attitude score.

Predictor 2:

Seconds.

Comparing raw coefficients is misleading.

Standardized coefficients allow comparison because all predictors are placed on the same scale.

21. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1:

Add every available variable as a control.

Reality:

Controls should be theory-driven.

Misconception 2:

Statistical significance alone decides whether to include controls.

Reality:

Theoretical relevance is more important.

Misconception 3:

Standardization fixes direction problems.

Reality:

Standardization changes scale, not meaning.

Lower-is-better variables still need reverse coding.

Misconception 4:

Interaction significance disappears when controls are added.

Reality:

If it remains significant, the moderation is more credible.

Misconception 5:

Unstandardized coefficients can always compare predictor importance.

Reality:

Different units make direct comparison inappropriate.

22. Regression Analysis Decision Framework

Question:

“Which variables predict satisfaction?”

Use:

Multiple regression.

Question:

“Does one variable explain an outcome?”

Use:

Simple regression.

Question:

“Does the relationship depend on another variable?”

Use:

Moderation with interaction.

Question:

“Can several indicators be combined?”

Use:

Composite index construction.

Question:

“Which predictor is strongest?”

Use:

Standardized beta coefficients.

23. Complete Workflow for Building a Strong Regression Model

Step 1:

Define theoretical model.

Identify:

  • Outcome.
  • Predictors.
  • Controls.
  • Moderators.

Step 2:

Prepare variables.

  • Reverse-code where needed.
  • Standardize indicators.
  • Create composite scores.

Step 3:

Run regression.

Interpret:

  • Coefficients.
  • Significance.
  • R².

Step 4:

Add justified controls.

Check:

  • Coefficient changes.
  • ΔR².

Step 5:

Test interactions if theory suggests moderation.

Final Key Takeaways

  1. Regression should answer a clear theoretical question.
  2. Controls improve interpretation only when they are justified.
  3. Categorical controls belong as factors in Jamovi.
  4. Composite indices require:
    * Same direction.
    * Same scale.
  5. Standardization removes unit effects but does not fix direction.
  6. Moderation is tested through interaction terms.
  7. Stable coefficients after controls indicate robustness.
  8. Standardized beta coefficients allow comparison of predictor strength.
  9. The strongest models combine:
    * Theory.
    * Appropriate statistics.
    * Clear interpretation.

Good statistical modelling is not about adding more variables; it is about building the simplest model that accurately answers the research question.

Continuous controls, such as age and number of prior sessions, go into the covariates box. Other factors may influence satisfaction:

  • Age.
  • Gender.
  • Region.
  • Prior experience.

Control variables reduce alternative explanations.

“Does the main predictor still matter when other relevant factors are held constant?”

Example:

Without controls:

Satisfaction \= Interface Update

Possible issue:

Adding age as a control:

Satisfaction \=

  • Improves accuracy.

But excessive controls can create problems:

  • More complex models.
  • Reduced interpretability.
  • Lower statistical power.
  • Possible overfitting.

The goal:

Not the largest model.

The goal:

The most theoretically justified model.

The case asks whether the reference category should shift.

Only if there is a theoretical reason.

  • Model fit.
  • Overall significance.

Example:

Comparing:

16. Hierarchical Regression

Purpose

Hierarchical regression evaluates whether new predictors add explanatory value beyond existing variables.

It answers:

“Does this new variable explain additional variance after accounting for existing predictors?”

17. Building Blocks in Hierarchical Regression

Example:

Model 1: Controls only

  • Age.
  • Gender.
  • Region.

Meaning:

How much variance in satisfaction is explained by controls.

Model 2: Add interface usage

Predictors:

  • Age.
  • Gender.
  • Region.
  • Feature usage.

Measure:

New R².

18. ΔR² (Change in Explained Variance)

Formula:

ΔR² \= R²(Model 2) − R²(Model 1)

Meaning:

How much additional variance is explained by the new predictor block.

Example:

Model 1:

R² \= 0.30

Model 2:

R² \= 0.42

ΔR²:

0.12

Interpretation:

Feature usage explains an additional 12% of satisfaction variance beyond controls.

19. Adding Customer Tenure as Another Block

Emma suggests adding tenure.

20. Impact of Adding Another Block

Adding predictors can:

Increase:

  • R².

Because more variables are included.

However:

The increase may be small.

It improves explanation.

If tenure adds little:

It may not justify additional complexity.

21. R² vs Adjusted R²

R²

Measures explained variance.

Problem:

It always increases when predictors are added.

Even useless predictors can increase R².

Adjusted R²

Penalizes unnecessary predictors.

It considers:

  • Number of predictors.
  • Sample size.

If adjusted R² decreases:

The added variable may not improve the model enough.

22. Model Complexity vs Parsimony

Complex model

Advantages:

  • More variables.
  • More complete explanation.

Disadvantages:

  • Harder interpretation.
  • Higher overfitting risk.

Simple model

Advantages:

  • Easier interpretation.
  • More stable.
  • Better generalization.

Disadvantages:

  • May miss important factors.

Best principle:

Add complexity only when it provides meaningful explanatory improvement.

R²

Measures explained variance.

It always increases when variables are added.

  • Sample size.

Standardization converts variables into a common scale.

Usually:

Adding all variables improves the model.

Exam Study Guide Appendix

Exam-Oriented Appendix

A. Practical examples and use cases

1. Regression with control variables: retail satisfaction

Question: How strongly do store attributes (SA) and product attributes (PA) predict customer satisfaction after accounting for demographic factors?

Base model:

Satisfaction = b₀ + b₁(SA) + b₂(PA) + error

The lecture example reports approximately R² = .299. Standardised effects were about βSA = .350 and βPA = .329, so SA was slightly stronger before demographic controls.

Controlled model: Add theoretically relevant age, gender, education, and income variables. In Jamovi:

  • continuous controls go under Covariates;
  • categorical controls go under Factors and are dummy-coded automatically.

After controls, the SA and PA standardised effects were roughly .34 and .33, indicating that their predictive relationships were stable. Most demographic terms were nonsignificant, though the above-55 versus under-25 age contrast was significant and positive in the transcript example.

Interpretation: “Holding the included demographics constant, higher store and product attribute scores are associated with higher satisfaction.” Do not say controls remove all confounding or prove causality.

2. Dummy coding and reference categories

For a three-category Region variable (North, South, West), regression uses k − 1 = 2 indicators. If North is the reference:

  • South coefficient = expected outcome difference between South and North, holding other predictors fixed;
  • West coefficient = West–North difference.

Changing the reference category changes coefficient comparisons/intercept, not fitted values, R², or the underlying model. Choose a reference that is substantively meaningful or aids interpretation. The lecture’s Jamovi examples used first categories such as Male, age under 25, matric-level education, and income below ₹25,000 as references.

3. Hierarchical regression use case: IntellaTech

Goal: Test whether interface/feature usage explains customer satisfaction beyond background factors.

  • Block/Model 1: theory-based controls—age, region, prior sessions, perhaps tenure.
  • Block/Model 2: add interface/feature usage predictors.
  • Optional Model 3: add customer tenure only if theory requires a separate incremental test.

Compare R², adjusted R², ΔR², the F-change/significance of the added block, coefficient stability, diagnostics, and interpretability.

The retail demonstration’s control-only adjusted R² was approximately .00748, increasing to about .31 after the focal attributes were added. The important conclusion is that the focal predictors contributed substantial incremental explanatory power beyond weak demographic controls—not that high R² proves causal truth.

4. Choosing control variables

Include a control when theory or prior evidence indicates that it affects the outcome and could distort the focal predictor–outcome relationship. For IntellaTech:

  • age may relate to technology familiarity;
  • region may capture service/infrastructure variation;
  • prior sessions represent existing engagement;
  • customer tenure may reflect accumulated experience.

Do not include every available variable. Unnecessary controls reduce parsimony/power, increase collinearity, complicate interpretation, and can introduce overcontrol bias, especially if the variable is a mediator or consequence of exposure.

5. R² and adjusted R² use case

  • R² is the sample proportion of outcome variance explained and never falls when predictors are added.
  • Adjusted R² penalises predictors that do not improve the model enough; it can fall after adding noise variables.
  • ΔR² is the incremental explained variance between nested blocks: R²new − R²previous.

Example: R²control = .08, R²usage = .32; ΔR² = .24. Interface usage adds 24 percentage points of explained sample variance beyond controls. Report whether the change is statistically supported and whether it is practically meaningful.

6. Standardised versus unstandardised effects

  • B (unstandardised): expected change in Y’s original units for a one-unit increase in X, holding others constant. Best for practical prediction: “One additional prior session predicts 0.12 satisfaction points.”
  • β (standardised): expected SD change in Y for a one-SD increase in X. Useful for comparing predictor strength within a model when units differ.

Large β does not automatically mean causal or strategically easiest to change. Compare β only after checking model assumptions, uncertainty, construct quality, and predictor ranges.

7. Mediation with regression paths

Question: Does perceived value (PV) mediate the effect of product attributes (PA) on satisfaction (SAT)?

Estimate:

  1. a: PA → PV;
  2. b: PV → SAT while accounting for PA;
  3. c′: direct PA → SAT while accounting for PV.

Indirect effect = a × b; total effect = c′ + ab. The lecture demonstrates this through multiple regressions and multiplication of coefficients. For formal inference, use a dedicated mediation procedure with bootstrapped confidence intervals; significance of separate paths alone is not a sufficient indirect-effect test.

8. Moderation with an interaction

Question: Does variety-seeking (VS) change the PA → satisfaction relationship?

SAT = b₀ + b₁PA + b₂VS + b₃(PA×VS) + controls + error

The interaction coefficient b₃ tests moderation. The lecture example reports a positive, significant interaction (unstandardised estimate around .0551; standardised value around .3427 after controls), interpreted as product attributes generating greater satisfaction among stronger variety seekers.

Keep both lower-order terms (PA, VS) in a model containing PA×VS. Mean-centering can improve interpretation and reduce nonessential collinearity with the product term, but does not change substantive model fit or cure true predictor collinearity. Probe a significant interaction using simple slopes or predicted-value plots rather than interpreting only its sign.

9. Practical business conclusions from the retail case

Descriptive means in the lecture were approximately PA 4.10/5, satisfaction 3.64/5, and perceived value 3.62/5. Store attributes were a stronger direct satisfaction predictor, while product attributes created value and had stronger effects for variety seekers.

Possible decisions drawn from the combined evidence:

  • expand assortment breadth/depth and product quality screening;
  • create “What’s New” displays/demos for variety seekers;
  • improve store ambience, signage, movement, queues, service encounters, employee training/motivation;
  • maintain courteous/helpful staff as a current strength;
  • display savings and price comparisons to strengthen cognitive value;
  • launch loyalty mechanisms where repeat/loyalty intent is weak;
  • periodically monitor online-shopping preference rather than assuming it is static.

Low item means/loading patterns can suggest improvement opportunities but should be interpreted against item wording, construct validity, importance, and feasibility—not treated mechanically as strategy.

10. Metric-based measurement model

The same conceptual variables can be measured using operational metrics rather than perceptions:

Construct Example objective metrics Desired direction
Store attributes distance to store, checkout wait, available SKUs distance/wait lower is better; availability higher is better
Product attributes price deviation from market, defects per 1,000, stock-out frequency, product life/warranty price/defects/stock-outs lower; life higher
Perceived/realised value proxy usage duration ÷ price paid, savings, benefit per cost higher is better
Variety-seeking brands switched, unique SKUs bought, first-time product trials, categories explored higher indicates more variety-seeking
Satisfaction outcomes complaints, repeat visits, retention/loyalty, recommendation complaints lower; repeat/retention higher

Metrics may be more objective than self-reports but are still operational proxies; validity and data quality remain necessary.

11. Building a composite index from mixed-unit metrics

Never directly average kilometres, minutes, rupees, counts, and percentages. Use:

  1. Validate and clean every indicator.
  2. Standardise each: z = (x − mean)/SD.
  3. Align direction: if high values mean worse performance (distance, wait, defects, complaints), reverse the standardised score using −z.
  4. Aggregate aligned z-scores by justified mean/sum (or theoretically supported weights).
  5. Document and validate the resulting index before regression.

Example: Store Index = mean(−zDistance, −zWait, +zAvailability). A high index consistently represents better store attributes.

Standardisation removes unit/scale differences; it does not correct invalid data, make a nonnormal variable normal, establish equal importance, or prove the indicators belong in one construct.

12. Selecting methods for metric business data

  • Descriptive: mean, median, SD, distribution, trends, frequency.
  • Two independent groups: t-test; nonparametric analogue often Mann–Whitney U.
  • 3+ groups: ANOVA; appropriate rank-based alternative when assumptions/design require.
  • Categorical association: chi-square.
  • Multiple outcomes: MANOVA where justified.
  • Association/prediction: correlation and regression.
  • Binary outcome: logistic regression.
  • Time-ordered outcomes: moving averages, exponential smoothing/Holt-type methods, ARIMA/SARIMA as appropriate.
  • Advanced prediction: trees/random forests, Bayesian models, neural networks—chosen for the problem and validation needs, not sophistication alone.

B. High-yield distinctions

Predictor vs control vs mediator vs moderator

  • Focal predictor: variable whose direct contribution is a research interest.
  • Control: competing/background variable held statistically constant.
  • Mediator: mechanism carrying an effect (X → M → Y).
  • Moderator: boundary condition changing effect strength/direction (X×W → Y).

Placing a mediator in the control block can remove part of the very effect being studied.

Covariate vs factor in Jamovi

  • Covariate: numeric/continuous predictor entered directly.
  • Factor: categorical predictor represented through contrasts/dummy variables.
  • “Control” describes analytical purpose, not a distinct software data type.

Dummy coding vs reverse coding

  • Dummy coding turns a categorical variable into k−1 indicators relative to a reference.
  • Reverse coding changes direction so high values have consistent meaning; for a 1–5 item, common reversal is 6 − x; for a z-score, multiply by −1.

Standardisation vs mean-centering

  • Standardising subtracts the mean and divides by SD; mean 0, SD 1.
  • Centering subtracts the mean only; units/SD remain unchanged.
  • Standardisation supports mixed-unit comparison/indexing; centering supports interpretation of interaction main effects.

Composite scale vs composite index

  • Scale items commonly reflect one latent construct and should covary.
  • Index indicators may form an overall condition and need not be interchangeable; weighting/direction requires theory.

Simultaneous vs hierarchical regression

  • Simultaneous: all predictors enter in one block; estimates their joint/unique effects.
  • Hierarchical: researcher specifies theoretically ordered blocks and tests incremental contribution (ΔR²).
  • Hierarchical regression is not stepwise selection; order comes from theory, not an automated algorithm.

R² vs adjusted R² vs ΔR²

  • R²: total sample variance explained.
  • Adjusted R²: complexity-penalised estimate for comparing models with different predictor counts.
  • ΔR²: incremental variance explained by a new block.

B vs β

  • B retains original units and is used in prediction/economic interpretation.
  • β uses SD units and helps compare relative association strength.

Mediation vs moderation

  • Mediation asks how/why X affects Y.
  • Moderation asks when/for whom/how strongly X affects Y.
  • Indirect effect is a product of paths; moderation is tested by an interaction.

Perceptual vs metric data

  • Perceptual: respondents’ beliefs/attitudes on scales; necessary for internal experiences such as felt value.
  • Metric: observed operational quantities; less dependent on self-report but may be incomplete proxies and measured in mixed units.
  • They can be combined/triangulated rather than treated as mutually exclusive.

Parametric vs nonparametric methods

Parametric methods estimate parameters under distributional/model assumptions and often use means; nonparametric/rank methods make different/weaker assumptions and can suit ordinal or strongly nonnormal data. Nonparametric does not mean “assumption free.”

D. Rapid revision traps

  • A control variable is justified by theory/design, not simply because it is available.
  • Nonsignificant controls do not automatically make the study unbiased.
  • Statistical control cannot fix omitted/unmeasured confounding or establish randomisation.
  • Categorical variables belong as factors/dummies, not as one continuous numeric code.
  • Changing the dummy reference does not change model fit.
  • More predictors always maintain/increase raw R², so raw R² alone rewards complexity.
  • Adjusted R² can fall when a weak predictor is added.
  • ΔR² is incremental explained variance, not automatically a causal contribution.
  • Hierarchical regression is theory-ordered, not the same as automated stepwise regression.
  • A significant interaction must be probed; main effects become conditional in its presence.
  • Centering does not remove genuine multicollinearity or change the interaction’s fit.
  • Mediation is not shown merely because X, M, and Y are pairwise significant.
  • Multiplying path estimates gives an indirect estimate, but infer it with an appropriate CI/bootstrap.
  • Standardised β is unitless but depends on sample variability; it is not an immutable importance score.
  • Directly averaging kilometres, minutes, defects, and rupees is meaningless.
  • Reverse before aggregating so every high score represents the same direction.
  • A high metric index is useful only if its indicators and weights are defensible.
  • Objective metrics are not automatically valid measures of subjective value/satisfaction.
  • Item loading and item mean answer different questions: measurement contribution versus performance level.
  • Low loading does not by itself prove an operational weakness in the business; it may indicate a poor item.
  • p-values, R², and β must be translated into contextual/practical significance.
