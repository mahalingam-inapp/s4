2. Time-Series Data

A time series consists of observations recorded sequentially over time for a particular entity or process.

Examples:

  • Monthly sales for one store
  • Daily stock prices
  • Annual GDP
  • Weekly website traffic
  • Hourly electricity demand

The ordering of observations matters because earlier observations may influence later observations.

Key characteristics

Time-series data often contain:

  • Trend
  • Seasonality
  • Cyclicity
  • Irregular/random variation
  • Autocorrelation
  • Lagged relationships

3. Lagged Effects

A lagged effect occurs when an event or variable affects an outcome after a delay.

For example:

A holiday occurs today, but sales increase several days later.

The holiday’s effect is therefore lagged.

Example

Suppose:

  • Holiday occurs on December 10
  • Sales response occurs on December 13

The effect has a time delay.

Conceptually:

Event at time t → Effect at time t + k

where k represents the lag.

Lead vs lag

A useful distinction:

Lag effect:
The cause/event occurs first, and its effect occurs later.

Lead effect:
A variable or event provides information about something that occurs later; in time-series terminology, the predictor is effectively ahead of the outcome.

For the quiz example, because the holiday affects sales a few days later, the appropriate concept is:

Lagged effect

4. Time-Series Components

A time series can be viewed as containing several underlying components.

The major components are:

  1. Trend
  2. Seasonality
  3. Cyclicity
  4. Irregularity/noise

Understanding the distinction among these is essential.

5. Trend

A trend represents the general long-term direction of a series.

It can be:

  • Increasing
  • Decreasing
  • Relatively stable

Example:

A company’s sales increase steadily over ten years.

That long-term movement represents a trend.

Trend does not require the pattern to repeat at regular intervals.

6. Seasonality

Seasonality refers to a pattern that repeats at a regular and predictable frequency.

Examples:

  • Retail sales increasing every December
  • Ice-cream sales increasing every summer
  • Electricity demand following a daily pattern
  • Hotel bookings increasing every holiday season

The defining characteristic is regular repetition.

Important distinction

A pattern occurring every year in the same months is seasonal.

A movement caused by a recession that does not repeat at the same time each year is not seasonality.

7. Cyclicity

Cyclicity refers to longer-term fluctuations associated with broader economic or business conditions.

Examples:

  • Economic expansion
  • Recession
  • Recovery
  • Business-cycle fluctuations

A cycle does not necessarily repeat at a fixed calendar interval.

Quiz example

A company’s monthly sales:

Rise for approximately 18 months → fall during a recession

Because the movement:

  • Is related to economic conditions
  • Does not repeat in the same months every year

it represents cyclicity.

8. Cyclicity vs Seasonality

This is one of the most important distinctions.

Feature Seasonality Cyclicity
Repetition Regular Not necessarily regular
Timing Predictable Variable
Typical duration Often fixed Often longer and variable
Example December sales spike Recession-related sales decline
Calendar relationship Strong Usually weak

Memory aid

Seasonality \= regular repetition

Cyclicity \= economic/business cycles

9. Irregularity or Noise

Irregularity, also called random or residual variation, represents unpredictable movements that are not explained by the systematic components.

Examples may include:

  • Unexpected disruptions
  • Sudden shocks
  • Random fluctuations
  • Measurement noise
  • Unusual one-off events

Noise is generally difficult to predict systematically.

10. Time-Series Decomposition

A time series can conceptually be decomposed into:

Observed Series \= Trend + Seasonality + Cyclicity + Irregularity

Depending on the model, decomposition may be additive or multiplicative.

Additive decomposition

Conceptually:

Y \= T + S + C + I

where:

  • Y \= observed series
  • T \= trend
  • S \= seasonal component
  • C \= cyclical component
  • I \= irregular component

The purpose of decomposition is to understand what is driving movements in the series.

11. Forecast Uncertainty

Forecasts become increasingly uncertain as we look farther into the future.

A point forecast gives one expected value:

Forecast sales \= ₹10 million

But the actual future value may differ.

A prediction interval expresses uncertainty around the forecast.

For example:

  • 50% prediction interval
  • 80% prediction interval
  • 95% prediction interval

The wider the desired coverage, the wider the interval generally becomes.

12. Fan Charts

A fan chart is a visualization designed to show how forecast uncertainty changes over the forecast horizon.

It typically contains:

  • A central forecast path
  • Nested shaded prediction intervals
  • Increasing width as the forecast horizon extends

For example:

Central forecast
± 50% interval
± 80% interval
± 95% interval

The visual appearance resembles a fan because uncertainty spreads outward into the future.

Why it is appropriate

If the objective is:

“Show one graphic where uncertainty visibly widens as the horizon extends,”

a fan chart is particularly appropriate.

13. Forecasting Workflow

Suppose a manager has:

36 monthly sales observations from one store

and wants forecasts for future months.

A sensible workflow is:

Step 1: Verify the time index

Ensure that:

  • Dates are correctly formatted
  • Observations are in chronological order
  • There are no unexpected duplicate periods
  • Missing observations are understood

Step 2: Inspect the series

Look for:

  • Trend
  • Seasonality
  • Outliers
  • Structural changes
  • Variability
  • Autocorrelation

Step 3: Select an appropriate forecasting model

Depending on the characteristics of the series, possible models include:

  • ARIMA
  • Exponential smoothing
  • Seasonal models
  • Other time-series forecasting methods

Step 4: Validate the model

Use appropriate validation or out-of-sample testing.

Step 5: Produce forecasts

Generate future predictions and, where appropriate, prediction intervals.

14. Why “Model First, Inspect Later” Is Poor Practice

One option in the quiz suggested:

Fit an ARIMA-style model immediately and only afterward inspect trend and seasonality.

This reverses an important part of the workflow.

The researcher should first understand the data before selecting a model.

Model selection should be informed by characteristics such as:

  • Trend
  • Seasonality
  • Autocorrelation
  • Stationarity
  • Forecast horizon

Therefore:

Inspect → model → validate → forecast

is preferable to:

Model blindly → inspect afterward

15. Why Pooling 36 Monthly Observations as Independent Data Is Problematic

Time-series observations are generally not independent.

For example:

January sales may be related to February sales.

Treating the 36 observations simply as independent observations and using their overall mean ignores:

  • Trend
  • Seasonality
  • Autocorrelation
  • Time dependence
  • Recent changes

A time-series forecasting model is therefore more appropriate when the goal is to forecast future monthly sales.

16. Panel Data

Panel data combines two dimensions:

  1. Entity dimension
  2. Time dimension

Examples:

  • Companies observed over several years
  • Countries observed annually
  • Individuals observed monthly
  • Stores observed over multiple periods

A typical panel dataset might look like:

Company Year Sales Advertising
A 2022 … …
A 2023 … …
A 2024 … …
B 2022 … …
B 2024 … …

The company identifier tells us that multiple observations belong to the same entity.

17. Missing Years Do Not Automatically Destroy Panel Structure

An important point from the final quiz question:

A dataset can still be panel data even if some entity-year combinations are missing.

For example:

Company Year
A 2021
A 2022
A 2024
B 2021
B 2022
B 2023

Company A has no observation for 2023, but the data remain panel data because:

  • There is a company dimension.
  • There is a time dimension.
  • The company identifier allows repeated observations to be tracked.

This is called an unbalanced panel.

Balanced vs unbalanced panel

Balanced panel:
Every entity has observations for every relevant time period.

Unbalanced panel:
Some entity-time combinations are missing.

Both are panel data.

18. Time Series vs Cross-Sectional vs Panel Data

Data type Entity dimension Time dimension Example
Time series Usually one entity/process Yes Monthly sales of one store
Cross-sectional Many entities One point/period Sales of 500 firms in 2025
Panel Many entities Multiple periods Sales of 500 firms from 2020–2025

Key diagnostic question

Ask:

Do I have repeated observations of multiple entities over time?

If yes, the data are generally panel data.

19. Pooled OLS

Pooled OLS combines all entity-time observations and estimates one common regression while ignoring entity-specific effects.

For example:

Sales \= β₀ + β₁ Advertising + error

The model does not explicitly control for permanent differences between firms.

This can be problematic when firms differ systematically in characteristics such as:

  • Brand strength
  • Management quality
  • Corporate culture
  • Long-standing reputation

20. Fixed Effects Model

The Fixed Effects (FE) model controls for time-invariant entity-specific characteristics.

Examples:

  • Brand strength
  • Historical reputation
  • Permanent organizational culture
  • Stable geographic characteristics

The critical feature is:

Fixed effects allow these entity-specific characteristics to be correlated with the explanatory variables.

For example:

Brand strength may be correlated with advertising expenditure.

A company with a stronger brand may systematically spend differently on advertising.

Fixed effects are designed for precisely this situation.

Core principle

FE controls for unchanging entity characteristics even when those characteristics correlate with regressors.

21. Random Effects Model

The Random Effects (RE) model treats entity-specific effects differently.

The standard RE assumption is that the entity-specific effects are uncorrelated with the explanatory variables.

This assumption is crucial.

If that assumption is violated, RE estimates can be inappropriate.

Simplified distinction

Fixed Effects:

Entity-specific effect may be correlated with regressors.

Random Effects:

Entity-specific effect is assumed to be uncorrelated with regressors.

22. Fixed Effects vs Random Effects

Feature Fixed Effects Random Effects
Controls for entity-specific effects Yes Yes
Allows correlation with regressors Yes No, under standard assumption
Uses within-entity variation Strongly emphasizes it Uses within and between variation
Suitable when unobserved entity traits correlate with regressors Yes Generally no
Example Brand strength correlated with advertising Entity effects assumed independent of advertising

The most important conceptual distinction is the correlation assumption.

23. Hausman Test

The Hausman test is commonly used to help choose between Fixed Effects and Random Effects.

The conceptual null hypothesis is associated with the validity of the RE assumption.

Simplified interpretation:

If p-value > 0.05

At the 5% significance level:

Do not reject the null hypothesis.

This supports the use of Random Effects, assuming the rest of the model is appropriate.

If p-value \< 0.05

Reject the null hypothesis.

This provides evidence against the RE assumptions and generally favors Fixed Effects.

Quiz example

Given:

Hausman p-value \= 0.08
Significance level \= 0.05

Since:

0.08 > 0.05

the correct decision is:

Do not reject the null → choose Random Effects

24. Hausman Test Decision Rule

A useful revision table:

Hausman p-value Decision Typical model choice
p \< 0.05 Reject null Fixed Effects
p > 0.05 Do not reject null Random Effects

This is a simplified exam-oriented rule; actual model selection should also consider the research design, assumptions, specification, and diagnostics.

25. Dynamic Panel Models

A dynamic panel model includes lagged dependent variables.

For example:

Current sales depend partly on last year’s sales.

Conceptually:

Salesᵢ,ₜ \= β₀ + β₁ Advertisingᵢ,ₜ + β₂ Salesᵢ,ₜ₋₁ + entity effect + error

The inclusion of:

Salesᵢ,ₜ₋₁

makes the model dynamic.

26. Why Dynamic Panel Models Are Different

Suppose a researcher studies:

Advertising → Sales

but sales are persistent:

Last year’s sales → This year’s sales

Then ignoring lagged sales may omit an important source of persistence.

A dynamic panel model accounts for this temporal dependence.

The quiz specifically referenced:

Arellano–Bond type models

These are designed for dynamic panel settings and use instrumental-variable/GMM-style approaches to address endogeneity issues associated with lagged dependent variables and other regressors under appropriate assumptions.

27. Fixed Effects vs Dynamic Panel

These should not be treated as mutually exclusive concepts in every situation.

Fixed effects

Main concern:

Controlling for unobserved, time-invariant entity characteristics.

Dynamic panel

Main concern:

Modeling dependence on past values of the dependent variable.

A dataset can require both considerations.

For example:

Firms have persistent sales + firms have permanent characteristics correlated with advertising.

A dynamic panel framework may therefore be more appropriate than a simple static FE model.

28. Forecast Accuracy Metrics

The quiz identified the common forecast accuracy measures:

MAE, RMSE, MAPE

These compare forecasts with actual observations.

Mean Absolute Error — MAE

MAE measures the average absolute forecast error.

Conceptually:

MAE \= average |Actual − Forecast|

It is easy to interpret because it expresses error in the same units as the target variable.

For example, if sales are measured in ₹ millions, MAE is also expressed in ₹ millions.

Root Mean Squared Error — RMSE

RMSE is based on squared errors and then takes the square root.

Conceptually:

RMSE \= √(average error²)

Because errors are squared, RMSE gives relatively greater influence to large errors.

Thus, RMSE is useful when large forecast errors are particularly undesirable.

Mean Absolute Percentage Error — MAPE

MAPE expresses forecast error in percentage terms.

Conceptually:

MAPE \= average absolute percentage error

It can be intuitive because it expresses the typical error as a percentage.

However, MAPE has limitations, especially when actual values are zero or very close to zero.

29. Forecast Accuracy vs Model Selection Metrics

The quiz included several groups of statistical terms that should not be confused.

Forecast accuracy

MAE, RMSE, MAPE

Used to evaluate how close forecasts are to actual observations.

Information criteria

AIC, BIC, HQIC

Often used for model comparison and model selection, balancing model fit and complexity.

AR/MA/ARMA

These refer to time-series model structures, not forecast accuracy metrics.

  • AR \= Autoregressive
  • MA \= Moving Average
  • ARMA \= Autoregressive Moving Average

ACF/PACF/Q-statistic

These are primarily tools for understanding time-series dependence and model diagnostics.

  • ACF \= Autocorrelation Function
  • PACF \= Partial Autocorrelation Function
  • Q-statistics can be used for testing serial correlation.

30. Forecasting vs Panel Regression

Another important distinction from the quiz is that not every dataset involving time should be analyzed as a time-series forecasting problem.

Single store over time

Example:

36 months of sales from one store

This is primarily a time-series forecasting problem.

Many firms over multiple years

Example:

100 firms observed from 2015–2025

This is panel data.

The appropriate methodology depends on the research objective.

If the objective is:

Predict next month’s sales for one store

a time-series forecasting model may be appropriate.

If the objective is:

Estimate the effect of advertising on firm sales across firms and years

a panel regression model may be appropriate.

31. Data Structure Determines the Method

A central lesson from this quiz is:

Do not select a statistical method before understanding the structure and purpose of the data.

Ask:

Question 1: Is there a time dimension?

If no → likely cross-sectional data.

If yes → continue.

Question 2: Is there only one entity?

If yes → likely time-series data.

Question 3: Are there multiple entities observed repeatedly over time?

If yes → panel data.

Question 4: What is the research objective?

  • Forecasting → time-series forecasting methods
  • Estimating causal/associational effects across entities → panel regression
  • Understanding dynamic persistence → dynamic models may be required

32. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: Any data with dates are time-series data

Not necessarily.

Multiple entities observed over multiple dates constitute panel data.

Misconception 2: Missing years mean the data cannot be panel data

False.

Panel data can be unbalanced.

The key is whether entities can be tracked across time.

Misconception 3: Fixed effects assumes entity characteristics are unrelated to regressors

False.

That is closer to the standard Random Effects assumption.

Fixed effects allows correlation between time-invariant entity characteristics and regressors.

Misconception 4: Random effects is always better because it is more efficient

False.

RE depends on the assumption that entity-specific effects are uncorrelated with the regressors.

If that assumption is inappropriate, FE may be preferable.

Misconception 5: Hausman p > 0.05 means FE is proven wrong

Not exactly.

A p-value above 0.05 means the test does not provide sufficient evidence to reject the null under the chosen significance level.

In the simplified FE/RE decision framework, this supports RE.

Misconception 6: Correlation and regression are forecast accuracy metrics

False.

They are analytical methods for studying relationships.

Forecast accuracy is commonly evaluated using measures such as:

MAE, RMSE, MAPE

Misconception 7: Seasonality and cyclicity are the same

False.

Seasonality is regularly recurring.

Cyclicity is associated with longer-term fluctuations that do not necessarily repeat at fixed intervals.

Misconception 8: A recession-related rise and fall is necessarily seasonal

False.

Economic cycles are typically classified as cyclical rather than seasonal.

Misconception 9: Forecast uncertainty stays constant over the horizon

Generally false.

As the forecast horizon increases, uncertainty often increases, which can be visualized using a fan chart.

Misconception 10: Regression automatically establishes causation

False.

Regression identifies statistical relationships under a model. Causal inference requires appropriate assumptions and research design.

35. Final Revision Checklist

Before an exam, make sure you can answer these immediately:

  • Holiday effect occurring several days later? → Lagged effect.
  • Regular repeating annual pattern? → Seasonality.
  • Recession-related irregular economic cycle? → Cyclicity.
  • Long-term upward/downward direction? → Trend.
  • Unpredictable residual variation? → Irregularity/noise.
  • Forecast uncertainty widening into the future? → Fan chart.
  • One store over 36 months? → Time-series data.
  • Many firms over many years? → Panel data.
  • Missing firm-years? → Can still be an unbalanced panel.
  • Entity effects correlated with regressors? → Fixed Effects.
  • Entity effects assumed uncorrelated with regressors? → Random Effects.

Case Study

GreenLeaf Retail is a regional chain of five outlets tracking weekly sales over 20 weeks. Each week, store teams record sales volume and promotional spending, while sensors log foot traffic and a third-party service provides local weather data. This creates a panel dataset of 100 observations with metrics: Sales_it, Promo_it, Traffic_it, and Rain_i,t. One week’s promotion data at the downtown store is missing, requiring a decision on handling the gap. Managers suspect unobserved store-specific traits, like store layout, may influence sales. David, the data analyst, evaluates three model options: Pooled OLS, Random Effects, and Fixed Effects, following the progression Pooled OLS → Random Effects → Fixed Effects based on trait correlations. He applies a Hausman test; a p-value of 0.03 suggests correlated traits, favoring Fixed Effects. He also notes that foot traffic and weather rainfall show minor multicollinearity, prompting a decision on covariate inclusion. Because past sales often drive current outcomes, David incorporates a one-period lagged Sales_it in a dynamic panel model to capture persistence effects, then checks for autocorrelation. An AI-assisted coding tool (Gemini in Colab) helps generate syntax but hits a package conflict, so David adapts the code manually, relying on theoretical reasoning. Maria, the operations manager, the CFO, and the marketing lead must choose among: (A) Fixed Effects for robust control of unobserved traits, (B) Random Effects if the panel proves uncorrelated and more efficient, or (C) a dynamic panel specification to model sales persistence explicitly. They consider interpretability, computation time, and strategic insight into promotional timing. The decision will guide resource allocation and the next quarterly plan.

Comprehensive Notes: GreenLeaf Retail — Panel Data, Fixed Effects, Dynamic Panels, and Model Selection

1. Case Overview

GreenLeaf Retail is a regional retail chain with:

  • 5 stores
  • 20 weeks of observations
  • Weekly data
  • 100 total store-week observations

Each store is observed repeatedly over time, making this a panel dataset.

The core variables are:

  • Salesᵢₜ — sales for store i at time t
  • Promoᵢₜ — promotional spending
  • Trafficᵢₜ — foot traffic
  • Rainᵢₜ — rainfall/weather conditions

The business objective is to understand sales drivers and support decisions about:

  • Promotional spending
  • Promotional timing
  • Store-level resource allocation
  • Quarterly planning

The case introduces several important methodological issues:

  1. Panel-data structure
  2. Missing data
  3. Unobserved store-specific characteristics
  4. Pooled OLS
  5. Random Effects
  6. Fixed Effects
  7. Hausman testing
  8. Multicollinearity
  9. Dynamic panel modeling
  10. Autocorrelation
  11. AI-assisted coding
  12. Statistical reasoning and managerial interpretation

2. Identifying the Data Structure

The dataset contains:

5 stores × 20 weeks \= 100 store-week observations

Each observation is associated with both:

  • A store
  • A week

Therefore, the data are panel data.

Conceptually:

Store Week Sales Promo Traffic Rain
Downtown 1 … … … …
Downtown 2 … … … …
Downtown 3 … … … …
… … … … … …
Store 5 20 … … … …

The same store appears repeatedly across different weeks.

Key rule

Multiple entities + repeated observations over time \= panel data.

This distinguishes the case from a simple time series.

Time series

One store tracked over 20 weeks:

Store A → Week 1, Week 2, …, Week 20

Panel

Five stores tracked over 20 weeks:

Store A, B, C, D, E → each observed across multiple weeks

3. The Panel Dimensions

The dataset has two fundamental dimensions.

Cross-sectional dimension

The five stores.

Time dimension

The 20 weeks.

Therefore:

Entity dimension \= 5 stores

Time dimension \= 20 weeks

Total observations \= 5 × 20 \= 100

This is sometimes described as an N × T panel, where:

  • N \= number of entities
  • T \= number of time periods

Here:

N \= 5, T \= 20

4. The Core Research Question

The underlying analytical question can be expressed as:

How do promotional spending, foot traffic, weather, and previous sales relate to current sales while accounting for differences between stores?

A basic model could be written conceptually as:

Salesᵢₜ \= β₀ + β₁Promoᵢₜ + β₂Trafficᵢₜ + β₃Rainᵢₜ + errorᵢₜ

However, managers suspect that stores have permanent characteristics that are not explicitly measured.

For example:

  • Store layout
  • Location characteristics
  • Permanent customer demographics
  • Store size
  • Long-standing local reputation
  • Other stable characteristics

This leads directly to the fixed-effects/random-effects question.

5. Unobserved Store-Specific Traits

The case says managers suspect that unobserved store-specific traits, such as store layout, influence sales.

This is important because not every factor affecting sales is necessarily included in the dataset.

For example:

Store layout → Sales

But store layout might not have a numerical variable in the dataset.

If store layout differs between stores and remains relatively stable over the 20-week period, it is a:

Time-invariant store-specific characteristic

This is precisely the type of effect that panel-data models can help address.

6. Why Ignoring Store Differences Can Be Problematic

Suppose Downtown has:

  • Better layout
  • Better visibility
  • More attractive product placement

while another store has a less favorable layout.

If these differences are ignored, a regression might incorrectly attribute some of the sales difference to:

Promotion spending

when some of the difference actually comes from:

Store layout

7. Pooled OLS

The simplest model considered is:

Pooled OLS

Pooled OLS treats the panel observations as one combined dataset.

A simplified model is:

Salesᵢₜ \= β₀ + β₁Promoᵢₜ + β₂Trafficᵢₜ + β₃Rainᵢₜ + εᵢₜ

The model does not explicitly account for permanent store-specific effects.

Advantages

  • Simple
  • Easy to interpret
  • Fast to estimate
  • Computationally inexpensive

Limitation

If stores differ systematically in unobserved ways, pooled OLS can produce biased or misleading estimates when those omitted characteristics are related to the explanatory variables.

8. Random Effects

The next model considered is:

Random Effects (RE)

Random Effects recognizes that entities may have their own unobserved characteristics.

However, the standard RE framework assumes that these entity-specific effects are uncorrelated with the explanatory variables.

For example, RE would require an assumption along the lines of:

Store layout is uncorrelated with promotional spending, traffic, rainfall, etc., in the relevant model structure.

If that assumption is reasonable, Random Effects can be attractive because it can use both:

  • Within-store variation
  • Between-store variation

and can be more efficient than Fixed Effects under its assumptions.

9. Fixed Effects

The third model is:

Fixed Effects (FE)

Fixed Effects controls for time-invariant store-specific characteristics.

For example:

Store layout
Store’s permanent location characteristics
Stable customer base characteristics
Long-term store-specific culture

The major advantage is that these characteristics can be correlated with the regressors.

For example:

Store layout ↔ Promotional spending

A store with a better layout may systematically receive different promotional investment.

Fixed Effects can control for such time-invariant differences.

A statistical property of the errors or series where observations/residuals are correlated over time.

A dynamic model can reduce some unexplained temporal dependence, but researchers still need to check residual behavior.

Time-invariant differences between stores.

FE does not automatically control for:

Time-varying omitted factors.

Examples of time-varying factors:

  • A new manager
  • A competitor opening nearby
  • A temporary store renovation
  • A special event
  • A store-specific shock

These may require additional variables or time effects.

14. Time Fixed Effects

A useful extension is to include time fixed effects.

Suppose all five stores experience unusually high sales during Week 12 because of a regional festival.

A model with only store FE may not fully account for that common weekly shock.

A richer model could include:

Store Fixed Effects + Week Fixed Effects

This is commonly referred to as:

Two-way fixed effects

It controls for:

  • Permanent store-specific differences
  • Common time-specific shocks

Multicollinearity generally affects the precision of coefficient estimates.

Potential consequences include:

  • Larger standard errors
  • Less precise estimates
  • Difficulty separating individual predictor effects
  • Greater coefficient instability

Critical distinction

Multicollinearity does not itself create systematic coefficient bias in the same way that omitted-variable endogeneity can.

This distinction was central to Question 9.

No.

22. Dynamic Panel Model

A dynamic panel model includes a lagged dependent variable.

For GreenLeaf:

is the one-period lag of current sales.

The defining feature is:

The dependent variable depends partly on its own past value.

24. Dynamic Panel vs Fixed Effects

These address different analytical questions.

Fixed Effects asks:

How can we control for permanent store-specific differences that may be correlated with regressors?

Dynamic Panel asks:

How does previous sales performance influence current sales?

Therefore:

Dynamic modeling addresses temporal persistence.

They are not inherently competing alternatives.

A model can incorporate both.

25. The Important Econometric Problem with Lagged Dependent Variables

Adding:

Salesᵢ,ₜ₋₁

to a standard Fixed Effects model introduces an important problem.

In a dynamic panel with fixed effects, the lagged dependent variable can become correlated with the transformed error term.

This can produce:

Nickell bias

particularly when the time dimension is relatively short.

GreenLeaf has:

N \= 5

T \= 20

So this issue deserves consideration.

26. Nickell Bias

Nickell bias is associated with estimating dynamic panel models using conventional fixed-effects methods when a lagged dependent variable is included.

The simplified intuition is:

Lagged dependent variable + fixed-effects transformation → correlation with transformed error

Therefore:

Naïvely adding lagged sales to FE is not necessarily enough.

This is why Question 10 favored a specialized dynamic-panel estimator.

27. Dynamic Panel Estimators

When dynamic panel problems arise, specialized estimators may be used.

One important example is:

Arellano–Bond Difference GMM

These approaches use suitable instruments, often based on lagged variables, to address endogeneity under the appropriate assumptions.

The broad logic is:

Dynamic model → endogeneity concern → instrumental-variable/GMM strategy

28. Arellano–Bond

Arellano–Bond is associated with:

Difference GMM estimation for dynamic panel data.

It is designed for situations involving:

  • Lagged dependent variables
  • Individual-specific effects
  • Potential endogeneity
  • Panel structures with relatively many entities and comparatively shorter time dimensions

The estimator transforms the model and uses suitable internal instruments under specified assumptions.

30. Autocorrelation

David checks for autocorrelation after modeling persistence.

Autocorrelation means that errors or unexplained components are correlated across time.

For example:

This can affect:

  • Standard errors
  • Statistical inference
  • Model validity
  • Efficiency

31. Lagged Sales vs Autocorrelation

These are related but not identical.

Lagged sales

A variable deliberately included in the model:

Salesᵢ,ₜ₋₁

to model persistence.

Autocorrelation

A property of the error process or residuals:

εᵢₜ related to εᵢ,ₜ₋₁

A lagged dependent variable can capture some temporal dependence, but it does not guarantee that residual autocorrelation disappears.

Therefore:

Always perform appropriate post-estimation diagnostics.

Second-order serial correlation

The standard Arellano–Bond framework expects no problematic second-order serial correlation in the differenced residuals for the usual moment conditions to remain valid.

Thus, after estimation, David should conduct appropriate post-estimation tests.

Time-series essentials

Time-series analysis studies ordered observations collected over time—daily stock prices, daily retail sales, monthly demand, quarterly GDP, annual rainfall—to find temporal structure and forecast future values. Observations are generally dependent, so pooling 36 monthly values as if they were 36 independent cross-sectional cases is inappropriate.

Components and stationarity

  • Trend: long-term rise, fall, or stable level.
  • Seasonality: predictable fixed-frequency variation—day of week, month, holiday period.
  • Cycle: longer wave without a fixed duration, such as business-cycle contraction and recovery.
  • Irregular/noise: random disturbances.
  • Stationarity: key statistical properties do not change over time. Many ARIMA-style techniques require stationarity or transformations/differencing that achieve it.

Practical use case: Café sales rise each year (trend), peak every December (seasonality), fall during a recession (cycle), and drop unexpectedly during a one-day transport strike (irregular shock).

Common techniques

  • decomposition into components;
  • moving averages/smoothing to reduce noise;
  • exponential smoothing (older observations receive declining weights);
  • ARIMA and seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA);
  • autocorrelation/partial autocorrelation analysis;
  • Meta/Facebook Prophet, designed for business series with trend changes, multiple seasonalities, holidays, missing values, and outliers.

Prophet is often represented conceptually as outcome = trend + seasonal terms + holiday/event effects + error. It outputs fitted/forecast values (yhat), uncertainty bounds, trend/weekly/yearly/holiday components, and change points where trend shifts.

Sound forecasting workflow

  1. Verify date parsing, ordering, frequency, duplicates, gaps, and target field.
  2. Visualize before fitting: trend, seasonal patterns, breaks, anomalies, missingness.
  3. Partition training/test periods or use rolling-origin/time-series cross-validation; never randomly shuffle temporal observations for ordinary validation.
  4. Compare plausible models rather than accepting the first recommendation.
  5. Evaluate residual structure and out-of-sample error.
  6. Produce point forecasts plus widening uncertainty bands/fan charts.
  7. Add defensible holidays/events/regressors and scenario assumptions.
  8. Translate change points and forecasts into operational decisions.

Forecast accuracy

  • MAE = mean(|actual−forecast|): easy to interpret in outcome units.
  • RMSE = sqrt(mean(error²)): penalizes large errors more strongly.
  • MAPE = mean(|error/actual|)×100: percentage interpretation, but unstable/undefined near zero.

Lower values indicate better prediction on comparable data. Accuracy must be assessed on unseen periods. Correlation and regression coefficients are not forecast-error metrics.

Panel-data essentials

Panel data combine cross-sectional and temporal dimensions—for example, sales of 10 stores over 12 months, or 30 firms from 2015–2024. Each row must identify an entity and time period.

  • Balanced panel: every entity has all periods.
  • Unbalanced panel: some entity–period observations are missing; it can still be valid panel data.

Models

Pooled OLS: assumes no relevant persistent unit-specific heterogeneity (or ignores it). Easy baseline, but repeated rows are not truly independent and omitted store traits can confound coefficients.

Fixed Effects (FE): estimates relationships from within-entity change, absorbing all time-invariant entity traits, observed or unobserved. It permits those entity effects to correlate with regressors. It cannot estimate the effects of time-invariant entity variables and does not automatically handle time-varying confounders, serial correlation, heteroskedasticity, or endogeneity.

Random Effects (RE): models entity differences as random and assumes they are uncorrelated with regressors. It can estimate time-invariant predictors and may be more efficient when the assumption holds.

Hausman test

  • H₀: the RE estimator is consistent; systematic FE–RE coefficient differences are absent.
  • p > .05: fail to reject H₀; RE may be used if assumptions and substantive design support it.
  • p < .05: reject H₀; RE’s no-correlation assumption is doubtful, so FE is generally favored.

A Hausman result does not prove FE perfect or test the significance of the substantive sales model. In GreenLeaf, p=.03 favors FE over RE under the test.

Dynamic panels

If current sales depend on prior sales due to loyalty, habit, momentum, adjustment, or persistence, specify a lagged dependent variable:

Sales_it = ρ Sales_i,t−1 + βX_it + entity/time effects + error_it

Simply adding lagged Y to a short FE panel creates correlation with transformed errors (Nickell bias/endogeneity). Arellano–Bond/difference GMM and related dynamic-panel estimators use suitable deeper lags as instruments, but require valid-instrument and serial-correlation checks. Dynamic panel modeling is not a replacement name for FE; it answers a separate temporal-persistence question.

GreenLeaf Retail applied case

Structure and question

GreenLeaf has five stores observed across 20 weeks (N×T=100 rows), with sales, promotions, traffic, rain, and other conditions. This is panel data, not a single 100-observation time series. The goal is to explain within-store sales changes and turn results into promotional decisions.

Why heterogeneity matters

Stores differ persistently in location, layout, customer base, management, and brand familiarity. If these traits correlate with promotions or traffic, pooled OLS/RE can be misleading. FE compares each store with itself over time, while time FE can absorb common weekly shocks such as festivals, inflation changes, or market-wide weather/event effects.

Missing promotion value

  1. Investigate why it is missing.
  2. Recover the actual value from operational records if possible.
  3. If justified, impute using a documented method and perform sensitivity analysis.
  4. Otherwise omit that entity-period observation for the affected model and disclose the impact.

Do not replace it automatically with zero, mean, or lagged sales: “missing” does not mean “no promotion,” and the outcome cannot logically supply the missing predictor without a defensible imputation model.

Traffic, rain, and multicollinearity

Correlated predictors increase standard errors and make individual contributions hard to separate. Diagnose correlation/VIF/condition information, then retain variables if they are theoretically distinct and strategically important; combine, redesign, or remove only with justification. Rain may influence both traffic and sales, suggesting a causal structure that cannot be solved merely by dropping the variable with the weaker p-value.

Recommended progression

  1. Validate identifiers, time order, missingness, units, duplicates, and coding.
  2. Explore within- and between-store variation and temporal patterns.
  3. Diagnose multicollinearity and missing promotion data.
  4. Fit pooled OLS as a baseline with panel-appropriate uncertainty.
  5. Estimate FE and RE; use theory plus Hausman to choose.
  6. Add time FE when common week shocks matter.
  7. Examine lagged sales/persistence.
  8. If dynamics matter, use an appropriate dynamic-panel estimator rather than naïve pooled/FE lag regression.
  9. Test serial correlation, heteroskedasticity, instrument validity, and robustness.
  10. Compare interpretability, computation, predictive performance, and decision value.

Strategic use case: If FE shows promotions increase sales within the same store, allocate promotions by expected within-store return. If lagged sales is strong and validly estimated, plan sustained campaigns rather than isolated one-week actions. Coefficients are evidence, not automatic causal effects, unless identification supports causality.

  • Fitting before plotting/cleaning the series.

  • Selecting a forecasting model only because software recommends it.
  • Using in-sample fit as forecast validation.
  • Assuming missing years destroy a panel.
  • Treating 100 entity-period rows as 100 independent units.
