0 to 1

2. Why Logistic Regression?

Suppose HR wants to predict:

Attrition \= Yes or No

The outcome is binary:

  • 0 \= No / stayed
  • 1 \= Yes / left

A linear regression model could theoretically produce predictions such as:

-0.20

or:

1.17

Those cannot be interpreted as probabilities.

A probability must satisfy:

0 ≤ P ≤ 1

Logistic regression solves this by modeling the probability through the logistic function.

3. Logistic Regression Output

Suppose the model produces:

0.72

for an employee.

This is interpreted as:

Estimated probability that the employee will leave \= 72%

It is not:

  • A coefficient
  • A raw continuous prediction
  • A log-odds value, if the software is reporting predicted probability
  • An automatic leave/stay classification

4. Probability to Classification

Once a probability has been estimated, HR can apply a classification threshold.

A common threshold is:

0.50

For example:

Predicted probability \= 0.72

Since:

0.72 ≥ 0.50

classify as:

Leave

Predicted probability \= 0.32

Since:

0.32 \< 0.50

classify as:

Stay

Therefore:

Logistic regression → probability → threshold → classification

5. The Threshold Is a Decision Rule

The 0.5 cutoff is common, but it is not a universal law.

The appropriate threshold depends on the business problem.

For HR:

False negative

An employee is predicted to stay but actually leaves.

This could be costly if the organization loses a critical employee.

False positive

An employee is predicted to leave but actually stays.

This could lead HR to waste retention resources.

Therefore, the optimal cutoff may differ from 0.5 depending on:

  • Cost of false positives
  • Cost of false negatives
  • Class balance
  • Business objectives

6. Logistic Regression and the Logit

Logistic regression models the log-odds of the event.

The basic form is:

log(p / (1 − p)) \= β₀ + β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + …

where:

  • p \= probability of the event
  • p/(1−p) \= odds
  • log(p/(1−p)) \= log-odds or logit

The logistic transformation then converts the model’s linear predictor into a probability between 0 and 1.

7. Probability, Odds, and Log-Odds

These three quantities should not be confused.

Suppose:

p \= 0.75

Probability

0.75

Odds

0.75 / 0.25 \= 3

So the odds are:

3:1

Log-odds

ln(3)

These are mathematically related but have different interpretations.

8. Changing the Event Category

Suppose the original model predicts:

Leave \= Yes

and the researcher changes the event to:

Stay \= No

The model is now expressed in terms of the opposite outcome.

The underlying information in the dataset has not changed.

However:

The interpretation of the coefficients changes.

For a binary outcome, switching the event to its complement reverses the direction of the log-odds relationship.

Thus, coefficients typically change sign.

9. Example of Outcome Recoding

Suppose:

β \= +0.80

when modeling:

Probability of leaving.

A positive coefficient means higher X is associated with higher log-odds of leaving.

If the model instead predicts:

Probability of staying,

the corresponding relationship reverses.

The same underlying relationship is being represented from the opposite perspective.

10. What Does Not Necessarily Change When the Event Is Recoded?

Changing which binary category is treated as the event does not fundamentally change the underlying data relationship.

It changes:

  • Event interpretation
  • Coefficient direction
  • Intercept/coefficients representation

It does not mean:

The data suddenly have a different causal structure.

11. Model Fit: Deviance

One of the major fit measures discussed was:

Deviance

For logistic regression, deviance is based on the log-likelihood.

Conceptually:

Deviance \= −2 × log-likelihood

For models fitted to the same outcome data:

Lower deviance generally indicates better likelihood-based fit.

Therefore:

Smaller deviance → better fit

provided the models are being compared appropriately.

12. Why Lower Deviance Is Better

Higher log-likelihood means the model assigns greater likelihood to the observed data.

Because:

Deviance \= −2 × log-likelihood

a larger log-likelihood corresponds to a smaller deviance.

Thus:

Higher likelihood → lower deviance → better fit

13. Comparing Two Models

Suppose:

Model 1

Residual deviance:

420

Model 2

Residual deviance:

435

If both models are fit to the same data and have the same complexity:

Model 1 is preferred on likelihood-based fit.

The fact that Model 2 might have slightly higher classification accuracy at a 0.5 cutoff does not override this conclusion.

14. Accuracy vs Deviance

This distinction is important.

Accuracy

Measures:

Percentage of observations classified correctly at a particular cutoff.

Example:

0.5 threshold.

Deviance

Measures:

Likelihood-based model fit.

Therefore:

A model can have higher classification accuracy but worse likelihood-based fit.

Accuracy and deviance answer different questions.

15. AIC

AIC stands for:

Akaike Information Criterion

AIC compares model fit while penalizing model complexity.

Conceptually:

AIC \= fit measure + complexity penalty

The preferred model generally has:

Lower AIC

when comparing appropriate candidate models fitted to the same data.

16. BIC

BIC stands for:

Bayesian Information Criterion

Like AIC, it balances:

  • Model fit
  • Model complexity

The preferred model generally has:

Lower BIC

when comparing suitable models on the same data.

BIC typically imposes a stronger complexity penalty than AIC, especially as sample size grows.

17. AIC and BIC Are Not Significance Tests for Individual Predictors

This was explicitly tested.

AIC and BIC are primarily:

Model-comparison criteria

They do not answer:

“Is β₁ statistically different from zero?”

For an individual coefficient, researchers typically consider:

  • Standard error
  • Wald statistic
  • p-value
  • Confidence interval

depending on the analysis framework.

Therefore:

AIC/BIC ≠ individual coefficient significance test

18. Deviance, AIC, and BIC Do Not Require a 0.5 Cutoff

Another important misconception addressed in the quiz:

Deviance, AIC, and BIC are not dependent on choosing a classification cutoff.

They operate on the model’s likelihood/probability structure.

A threshold such as 0.5 becomes relevant when converting predicted probabilities into:

Predicted classes

It is not required to calculate:

  • Deviance
  • AIC
  • BIC

19. AIC/BIC vs Accuracy

AIC/BIC

Used for:

Comparing statistical models based on fit and complexity.

Accuracy

Used for:

Evaluating classification decisions at a selected cutoff.

Therefore, one should not automatically say:

“The model with higher accuracy is the better model.”

The correct choice depends on the analytical objective.

20. Pseudo-R²

Logistic regression does not have the exact same R² interpretation as ordinary linear regression.

Therefore, logistic models often use:

Pseudo-R² measures

Examples include:

  • McFadden’s R²
  • Cox & Snell R²
  • Nagelkerke R²

These provide useful measures of model performance, but they should not simply be interpreted as:

“The percentage of variance explained”

in exactly the same way as ordinary least squares R².

21. Why Pseudo-R² Should Not Automatically Determine Model Choice

A higher pseudo-R² may be useful evidence, but it does not mean:

The model is automatically superior in every respect.

Researchers should consider:

  • Likelihood
  • AIC
  • BIC
  • Predictive performance
  • Classification performance
  • Theoretical justification
  • Model complexity

22. AUC

AUC stands for:

Area Under the ROC Curve

The quiz’s key interpretation is:

Overall class separation across cutoffs.

AUC summarizes the model’s ability to discriminate between the two classes across a range of thresholds.

23. ROC Curve

ROC stands for:

Receiver Operating Characteristic

It examines the trade-off between:

  • True positive rate / sensitivity
  • False positive rate

across different classification thresholds.

Therefore, unlike accuracy at 0.5:

ROC/AUC considers performance across many possible cutoffs.

24. Interpretation of AUC

AUC can be understood as a measure of how well the model ranks positive cases above negative cases.

Broadly:

AUC near 0.5 → little discrimination

Higher AUC → better discrimination

AUC near 1.0 → excellent discrimination

Exact interpretation should consider context and sample uncertainty.

25. AUC vs Accuracy

Accuracy

Depends on:

One chosen threshold

AUC

Summarizes:

Discrimination across thresholds

Therefore:

AUC is not simply “percentage correct.”

26. AUC vs Sensitivity

Sensitivity at a particular threshold answers:

Of the employees who actually leave, what proportion did the model correctly identify?

AUC is broader.

It evaluates discrimination across the range of thresholds.

Thus:

AUC ≠ sensitivity at 0.5

27. jamovi Variable Setup

The quiz included a practical jamovi question.

Suppose:

Attrition \= 0/1

is the binary outcome.

And:

Job Satisfaction \= 1–4

is a predictor.

Job Satisfaction should be treated as a:

Continuous variable

when the research design treats the 1–4 score as a quantitative covariate.

It should then be placed in:

Covariates

28. Dependent Variable vs Covariate

In the logistic regression setup:

Dependent variable

The binary outcome:

Attrition

Covariate

The predictor:

Job Satisfaction

Therefore:

Job Satisfaction → Covariates

not:

Dependent Variable.

29. Why Coding Alone Does Not Determine Measurement Level

A variable being represented by numbers does not automatically make it continuous.

For example:

Gender \= 1, 2

is usually categorical.

But:

Job Satisfaction \= 1–4

may be treated as a quantitative predictor if the analytical design justifies that treatment.

The measurement level should reflect:

What the variable means and how it is being analyzed, not merely the fact that it contains numbers.

30. Multicollinearity

The quiz included:

VIF \= 6

and:

Tolerance \= 0.15

This indicates:

Multicollinearity among predictors.

The predictors contain substantial overlapping information.

31. VIF

VIF stands for:

Variance Inflation Factor

It measures how much the variance of an estimated coefficient is inflated because of relationships among predictors.

Higher VIF indicates greater multicollinearity.

A VIF of:

6

would generally warrant investigation.

32. Tolerance

Tolerance is related to VIF:

Tolerance \= 1 / VIF

Therefore, if:

VIF \= 6

then:

Tolerance ≈ 0.167

The example’s:

Tolerance \= 0.15

is consistent with substantial multicollinearity.

Lower tolerance indicates greater multicollinearity.

33. What Multicollinearity Does

Multicollinearity can lead to:

  • Inflated standard errors
  • Less precise coefficient estimates
  • Unstable coefficient estimates
  • Difficulty separating individual predictor effects

It is not primarily:

  • Class imbalance
  • Nonlinearity
  • Overfitting

although severe multicollinearity can contribute to broader modeling difficulties.

34. Multicollinearity vs Nonlinearity

These are different problems.

Multicollinearity

Predictors are strongly related to each other.

Nonlinearity

The relationship between a predictor and the outcome is not adequately represented by the assumed functional form.

A high VIF indicates:

Multicollinearity

not automatically:

Nonlinearity.

35. Multicollinearity vs Class Imbalance

Class imbalance concerns the distribution of the outcome classes.

Example:

95% stay

5% leave

This is a class imbalance problem.

It is unrelated to a VIF of 6.

36. Multicollinearity vs Overfitting

Overfitting occurs when a model captures noise or idiosyncrasies of the training data and performs poorly on new data.

Multicollinearity concerns relationships among predictors.

The two can coexist but are not synonymous.

37. Outcome Reference Level

Changing the reference/event category in binary logistic regression changes the way the model is expressed.

For example:

Original

Yes \= event

New

No \= event

The model is now predicting the opposite outcome.

Consequently:

Coefficient signs switch.

The underlying data relationship has not changed.

38. What Does Not Change Fundamentally?

Changing the event category does not mean:

  • The dataset has changed
  • Predictors have changed
  • The underlying observations have changed
  • A different regression type is required

It is a change in:

Outcome interpretation/coding

39. Changing Reference Category Does Not Create Ordinal Regression

A binary outcome remains binary even if categories are coded:

0 and 1

or:

1 and 2

or:

Yes and No

Changing the event category does not create an order.

Therefore:

Binary logistic regression remains binary logistic regression.

40. Model Fit and Reference Coding

For the same binary data and predictor set, changing which category is treated as the event generally changes the representation of the coefficients, but it does not fundamentally make the model a different underlying fit to the data.

Thus, the key effect is:

Interpretation and coefficient direction change.

41. Logistic Regression vs Linear Regression

This was particularly important in the final question.

Suppose a straight-line model predicts:

-0.15

or:

1.20

If interpreted as a probability, these are invalid.

A proper probability must be:

0 to 1

42. Why Logistic Regression Solves the Problem

Logistic regression uses the logistic function:

p \= 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ)

where:

z \= β₀ + β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + …

Regardless of the value of z:

p always lies between 0 and 1.

This makes logistic regression appropriate for binary-event probabilities.

43. The Correct HR Prediction Workflow

HR requires:

  1. An interpretable probability of resignation.
  2. An automatic leave/stay classification rule.

The appropriate workflow is:

Logistic regression → estimated probability → threshold → classification

For example:

Probability \= 0.72

↓

Compare with 0.50

↓

Leave

44. Why Clamping Linear Predictions Is Not Appropriate

One tempting workaround is:

Linear regression → predictions → clamp values below 0 to 0 and above 1 to 1.

This is an ad hoc fix.

It does not turn linear regression into a proper probability model.

The fundamental issue remains:

Linear regression does not naturally model binary-event probabilities.

Therefore:

Use logistic regression instead.

45. Why Thresholding Linear Regression Is Also Inadequate

Another workaround is:

Linear regression → fitted value → threshold → classification

Although this may produce a classification rule, the fitted values are not properly modeled probabilities.

Therefore, if HR specifically requires:

An interpretable chance of resignation

logistic regression is the appropriate approach.

46. Likelihood-Based Model Comparison

When comparing logistic models fit to the same outcome data:

Deviance

Lower is better.

AIC

Lower is generally preferred.

BIC

Lower is generally preferred.

These metrics incorporate likelihood-based fit, with AIC/BIC also accounting for model complexity.

47. Equal Model Complexity

If two models have:

Same data

and:

Same residual degrees of freedom / same effective complexity

then comparing deviance becomes particularly straightforward.

The model with lower deviance has:

Higher likelihood

and therefore:

Better likelihood-based fit.

48. Why Accuracy Does Not Always Win

Suppose:

Model 1

Accuracy \= 80%

Deviance \= 400

Model 2

Accuracy \= 82%

Deviance \= 415

Model 2 has better 0.5-cutoff accuracy.

But Model 1 has better likelihood-based fit.

Therefore, if the question specifically asks for:

Likelihood-based model comparison

Model 1 should be preferred.

49. Model Selection Should Match the Objective

Different metrics answer different questions.

If the goal is likelihood-based fit:

Use:

Deviance / AIC / BIC

If the goal is discrimination:

Use:

AUC

If the goal is classification at a chosen threshold:

Use:

Accuracy / sensitivity / specificity / confusion matrix

If the goal is individual predictor inference:

Use:

Coefficient, standard error, p-value, confidence interval, odds ratio

50. Odds Ratios

Although not directly tested in the quiz, odds ratios are important for interpreting logistic regression.

For coefficient β:

Odds Ratio \= eᵝ

If:

OR > 1

then higher X is associated with higher odds of the event.

If:

OR \< 1

then higher X is associated with lower odds of the event.

If:

OR \= 1

then there is no multiplicative change in odds associated with a one-unit increase in X.

51. Odds Ratio Is Not Probability Ratio

An odds ratio of:

2

does not mean:

Probability doubles.

Odds and probabilities are different quantities.

This is a common interpretation error.

52. Predictor Sign Interpretation

Suppose the event is:

Employee leaves

and:

β > 0

Then higher predictor values are associated with:

Higher log-odds of leaving.

If:

β \< 0

then higher predictor values are associated with:

Lower log-odds of leaving.

But if the event category is switched to:

Employee stays

the coefficient direction reverses.

Therefore:

Always identify the event category before interpreting coefficient signs.

54. Common Misconceptions to Remember

Misconception 1

“Higher deviance means better fit.”

False.

Lower deviance is better.

Misconception 2

“Higher AIC/BIC means better model.”

False.

Lower AIC/BIC is generally preferred.

Misconception 3

“AIC tells whether an individual coefficient is significant.”

False.

AIC compares models.

Misconception 4

“Deviance requires a 0.5 cutoff.”

False.

Deviance is likelihood-based and does not require classification.

Misconception 5

“AUC is accuracy.”

False.

AUC measures discrimination across thresholds.

Misconception 6

“AUC is sensitivity at 0.5.”

False.

Sensitivity is threshold-specific.

AUC considers discrimination across thresholds.

Misconception 7

“A prediction of 0.72 means the model is 72% accurate.”

False.

It means:

Estimated event probability \= 72%.

Misconception 8

“A linear regression prediction can simply be clamped to 0–1.”

Not as a principled replacement for logistic regression.

Use a probability model designed for binary outcomes.

Misconception 9

“Changing the event category changes the underlying relationship.”

False.

It changes the model’s perspective and typically reverses coefficient signs.

Misconception 10

“VIF \= 6 means the model is nonlinear.”

False.

It indicates:

Multicollinearity.

Misconception 11

“Pseudo-R² is exactly the same as ordinary R².”

False.

Pseudo-R² measures should be interpreted within their logistic-regression framework.

Central takeaway

Logistic regression should be understood as a probability model first and a classification model second.

The model estimates an event probability in the valid 0–1 range. A threshold such as 0.5 can then convert that probability into a leave/stay decision. Model fit should be evaluated with likelihood-based measures such as deviance, AIC, and BIC, while AUC evaluates discrimination across thresholds and VIF/tolerance diagnose multicollinearity. Changing the event category changes the interpretation and signs of coefficients but does not change the underlying data relationship.

Comprehensive Notes: Northfield Retail — Logistic Regression for Employee Attrition Prediction

1. Case Study Overview

Northfield Retail has created an HR analytics group to predict which employees are likely to leave within the next six months.

The team uses binary logistic regression, with employee attrition as the binary outcome.

The initial predictors are:

  • Tenure in months
  • Performance rating
  • Training hours

The model produces an estimated probability of attrition, which is then converted into a leave/retain classification using a 0.5 cutoff.

The first model has:

50% sensitivity for the attrition class

This means the model is missing a substantial proportion of employees who actually leave.

The team therefore considers three broad strategies:

  1. Expand the predictor set
  2. Prune weak predictors
  3. Change the classification cutoff

The key methodological lesson is:

Model improvement and classification-threshold adjustment solve different problems. The team should diagnose whether the weakness is in the model’s explanatory/predictive structure or in the decision threshold before choosing an intervention.

2. The Business Problem

The practical objective is:

Identify employees who are likely to leave within six months so HR can target retention interventions.

This is fundamentally a binary classification and probability-estimation problem.

The outcome can be represented as:

  • 1 \= employee leaves
  • 0 \= employee stays

The model therefore estimates:

P(Attrition \= 1 | employee characteristics)

3. Why Logistic Regression Is Appropriate

Attrition is a binary outcome.

Ordinary linear regression is not ideal because a linear model can produce predictions below 0 or above 1.

Logistic regression solves this by transforming a linear predictor into a probability.

The model is:

\[ P(Y=1)=\\frac{1}{1+e^{-Z}} \]

where:

\[ Z=b\_0+b\_1X\_1+b\_2X\_2+\\cdots+b\_kX\_k \]

The logistic transformation guarantees:

\[ 0\

4. The Meaning of Z

The quantity:

\[ Z=b\_0+b\_1X\_1+b\_2X\_2+\\cdots \]

is the linear predictor.

It is not itself the probability.

The logistic function transforms Z into the probability of attrition.

For example:

Z \= 0

produces:

P \= 0.50

If:

Z > 0

then:

P > 0.50

If:

Z \< 0

then:

P \< 0.50

This relationship explains why the 0.5 probability cutoff corresponds to:

Z \= 0

5. Initial Predictors

The first model contains:

Tenure

Measures how long the employee has been with the organization.

It could capture relationships between organizational attachment, career stage, or early-stage turnover.

Performance rating

Represents observed employee performance.

It may be associated with:

  • Engagement
  • Career prospects
  • Managerial relationships
  • Retention

Training hours

The proportion of employees who actually stay that the model correctly identifies as staying.

1. Case Overview

Northfield Retail’s HR analytics team developed a binary logistic regression model to predict whether store employees would leave within six months.

The initial model uses:

  • Tenure
  • Performance rating
  • Training hours

The model estimates each employee’s probability of attrition and converts that probability into a leave/stay classification using a 0.5 cutoff.

The initial model achieved only:

50% sensitivity for the attrition class

The team therefore considered:

Statistical model fit, predictive discrimination, classification performance, and business usefulness are related but different concepts.

2. Logistic Regression Framework

Because attrition has two possible outcomes, logistic regression is appropriate.

The model is:

\[ P(Y=1)=\\frac{1}{1+e^{-Z}} \]

where:

\[ Z=b\_0+b\_1X\_1+b\_2X\_2+\\cdots+b\_kX\_k \]

Here:

  • (Y=1) represents the event, such as employee leaves.
  • (X_1, X_2,\ldots) are predictors.
  • (b_0) is the intercept.
  • (b_i) are regression coefficients.
  • (Z) is the linear predictor.
  • The logistic transformation converts (Z) into a probability between 0 and 1.

Key distinction

The predictors first produce:

Z / linear predictor

which is then transformed into:

Probability of attrition

The probability is the employee’s risk score.

3. Employee-Level Risk Prediction

For a new employee, Northfield inserts the employee’s:

  • Tenure
  • Performance rating
  • Training hours

into the equation.

The result is a probability such as:

0.72

This means:

The model estimates a 72% probability of attrition.

It does not mean:

  • 72% model accuracy
  • A coefficient of 0.72
  • A guaranteed 72% chance that the employee will leave

It is a model-generated probability estimate.

4. The 0.5 Classification Cutoff

The model uses:

0.5

as the initial classification threshold.

The rule is:

\[ P(\\text{attrition})\\geq0.5 \]

→ classify as likely attrition

and:

\[ P(\\text{attrition})\<0.5 \]

The cutoff therefore converts a continuous probability into a binary decision.

  • Fit

Likelihood-based model fit while accounting for complexity.

Business performance must still be considered.

34. Statistical Fit vs Business Utility

This is perhaps the central conceptual lesson.

Statistical fit

Asks:

How well does the model fit the observed data, accounting for complexity?

Measures include:

  • Deviance
  • AIC
  • BIC

Predictive discrimination

Asks:

How well does the model distinguish leavers from stayers?

Measure:

  • AUC

Classification performance

Asks:

At a particular threshold, how well does the model classify cases?

Measures:

  • Sensitivity
  • Specificity
  • Precision
  • Accuracy

Business utility

Asks:

Is this model useful and affordable for the organization?

Considers:

  • Intervention costs
  • Data collection costs
  • Manager usability
  • Actionability
  • Cost of false positives
  • Cost of false negatives

35. Why AIC/BIC Cannot Automatically Override Business Requirements

A lower AIC or BIC means:

Better likelihood-based fit relative to complexity.

It does not mean:

Automatically better operational decisions.

Model selection must reflect the actual objective.

If HR needs to catch at least 70% of leavers while retaining acceptable specificity, then those requirements matter directly.

This distinction matters.

“The store manager likes simple models.”

“The CFO wants to save money.”

The strongest justification was:

Simplicity and cost-effectiveness are additional benefits.

37. Threshold Selection as a Business Decision

A threshold should ultimately reflect:

Relative costs of false negatives and false positives.

If missing a potential leaver is extremely costly:

Lower threshold may be appropriate.

If unnecessary interventions are very expensive:

Higher threshold may be appropriate.

Thus, the optimal cutoff is a:

Decision-theoretic choice, not simply a statistical default.

38. Common Misconceptions

1. “0.5 is the correct cutoff.”

Not necessarily.

It is merely a common starting point.

2. “Lowering the cutoff improves the model.”

Not exactly.

It changes classification performance, not the underlying fitted model.

3. “AIC/BIC tell us which model predicts attrition best.”

Not directly.

They measure likelihood-based fit with complexity considerations.

4. “The model with the lowest AIC/BIC must be deployed.”

Not necessarily.

Business objectives and out-of-sample performance also matter.

5. “More predictors always make the model better.”

False.

More predictors can increase:

  • Complexity
  • Cost
  • Overfitting
  • Multicollinearity

6. “A simpler model is always better.”

Also false.

A model that is too simple may omit important information.

The correct principle is:

Choose the simplest model that provides adequate predictive and business performance.

7. “70% sensitivity means 70% accuracy.”

False.

Sensitivity specifically concerns:

Actual members of the attrition class.

8. “High sensitivity alone means the model is good.”

False.

Sensitivity must be considered alongside:

  • Specificity
  • Precision
  • AUC
  • Business costs

9. “AUC and sensitivity measure the same thing.”

They do not.

AUC \= discrimination across thresholds

Sensitivity \= performance at a particular threshold

10. “Changing the cutoff changes AIC and BIC.”

No.

AIC/BIC are properties of the fitted model and likelihood.

The classification cutoff is applied afterward.

41. Final Takeaway

The Northfield case demonstrates that there is no single universal definition of the “best” logistic regression model.

The best model is the one that appropriately balances:

  • Statistical fit
  • Predictive discrimination
  • Sensitivity
  • Specificity
  • Model complexity
  • Data collection cost
  • Interpretability
  • Operational actionability
  • Business consequences of errors

The most important conceptual distinction is:

A model estimates risk; the cutoff determines how that risk becomes a decision.

Exam Study Guide Appendix

Week 15 Exam Appendix

Rapid-recall chain

Define binary event → choose theoretically relevant predictors → code measurement levels/reference categories → fit logistic model → interpret coefficients/odds ratios → assess likelihood fit and calibration → assess discrimination → inspect confusion matrix at candidate thresholds → validate out of sample → choose a threshold from business error costs → monitor deployment

Logistic regression estimates the probability of a binary outcome such as leave/stay, buy/not buy, default/no default, pass/fail, or treatment success/failure. It does not merely force linear-regression predictions into 0–1.

Core equations and interpretation

Linear predictor:

Z = β₀ + β₁X₁ + … + βkXk

Probability:

P(Y=1|X) = 1 / (1 + e^(−Z))

Logit:

log[p/(1−p)] = Z

  • Probability p: chance of the event.
  • Odds p/(1−p): event chance relative to non-event chance.
  • Coefficient β: change in log-odds for a one-unit increase in X, holding other predictors constant.
  • Odds ratio exp(β): multiplicative change in odds. OR=1.30 means 30% higher odds, not 30 percentage points more probability. OR=.70 means 30% lower odds.

High-value distinctions

Distinction Exam-ready answer
Probability vs. classification Probability is continuous risk (e.g., .42); classification applies a chosen cutoff to turn risk into an action label.
Model vs. threshold Predictor/model changes alter estimated probabilities and discrimination; threshold changes only the decision rule applied to existing scores.
Sensitivity vs. specificity Sensitivity=TP/(TP+FN), fraction of actual events detected; specificity=TN/(TN+FP), fraction of non-events correctly rejected.
Precision vs. recall Precision=TP/(TP+FP), fraction of flagged cases truly positive; recall is sensitivity.
False negative vs. false positive FN: actual leaver predicted stay; FP: actual stayer predicted leave. Their business costs determine the cutoff.
Accuracy vs. AUC Accuracy is threshold- and prevalence-dependent; AUC measures ranking/discrimination across all thresholds.
AUC vs. sensitivity AUC is global discrimination; sensitivity is performance for positives at one chosen cutoff.
Deviance vs. AIC/BIC Deviance reflects likelihood lack-of-fit (lower better); AIC/BIC add complexity penalties for model comparison on the same outcome/data. BIC penalizes complexity more strongly.
AIC/BIC vs. p-value AIC/BIC compare whole models; predictor p-values assess individual coefficient evidence.
Pseudo-R² vs. OLS R² Pseudo-R² summarizes relative likelihood improvement; it is not literally the percentage of outcome variance explained and versions (McFadden, Cox–Snell, Nagelkerke) differ.
Multicollinearity vs. nonlinearity Multicollinearity is predictor overlap; nonlinearity in logistic regression concerns whether continuous predictors relate linearly to the logit.
Multicollinearity vs. class imbalance VIF/tolerance concern predictors; imbalance concerns rare outcome classes. They require different remedies.
Binary vs. ordinal/multinomial logistic Binary has two outcome levels; ordinal has ordered 3+ levels; multinomial has unordered 3+ levels. Reversing a binary event does not create ordinal regression.
Logistic vs. linear regression Linear regression assumes a continuous outcome and may predict outside 0–1; logistic uses a likelihood-based logit/S-curve appropriate for binary outcomes. Clamping or thresholding OLS does not fix its error/distribution assumptions.

Classification and the confusion matrix

Actual event Actual non-event
Predicted event True positive (TP) False positive (FP)
Predicted non-event False negative (FN) True negative (TN)
  • Accuracy=(TP+TN)/N.
  • Sensitivity/Recall=TP/(TP+FN).
  • Specificity=TN/(TN+FP).
  • Precision=TP/(TP+FP).
  • False-positive rate=FP/(FP+TN)=1−specificity.

A .50 cutoff is conventional, not universally optimal. Lowering it usually increases sensitivity and false positives while reducing specificity; raising it usually does the reverse. Threshold choice should reflect prevalence, intervention capacity, and the relative cost of FN versus FP.

Practical use case: If missing a likely leaver costs ₹3 lakh in replacement and knowledge loss but an unnecessary retention meeting costs ₹5,000, HR may rationally choose a lower threshold. It should still check that the queue of flagged employees is operationally manageable and fair.

Model evaluation toolkit

Overall fit and comparison

  • Compare null/intercept-only and fitted model using likelihood-ratio chi-square/deviance; a small p-value indicates the predictor set improves likelihood over the null.
  • Lower deviance is better for comparable/nested models.
  • Lower AIC/BIC is preferred among models fit to the same cases and outcome, but these are relative scores, not absolute deployment rules.
  • Likelihood-ratio tests are appropriate for nested models; AIC/BIC can compare non-nested candidates under comparable data/likelihood.
  • If models have equal complexity, lower deviance corresponds to better likelihood fit; with unequal complexity, AIC/BIC incorporate parsimony.

Discrimination

The ROC curve plots sensitivity against false-positive rate across thresholds. AUC is the probability-like ability to rank a randomly chosen event above a randomly chosen non-event.

  • .50: no better than chance ranking.
  • .60–.70: weak.
  • .70–.80: acceptable.
  • .80–.90: good.

  • .90: excellent (also check leakage/overfitting).

These are heuristics, not universal business standards.

Predictor inference and diagnostics

Report β, SE, Wald/likelihood evidence, p-value, CI, and OR. If an OR confidence interval includes 1, evidence for a nonzero odds effect is weak at that confidence level.

Check:

  • missingness/outliers and coding;
  • adequate event count/sample size;
  • VIF/tolerance (the lecture prefers VIF <3.3, with looser rules sometimes 5 or 10; tolerance below .20/.10 is concerning);
  • linearity of continuous predictors with the logit;
  • influential observations;
  • calibration/predicted-vs-observed risk;
  • class imbalance;
  • overfitting via holdout/cross-validation.

Multicollinearity inflates SEs and destabilizes coefficients; it does not mean predictions must fail or that logistic regression becomes nonlinear.

Event/reference coding

The outcome event must be stated explicitly. If “leave” is changed from event to reference/non-event, coefficients generally reverse signs and ORs become reciprocals because the model now predicts the complementary probability. Fitted probabilities correspondingly become 1−p; ranking information, likelihood fit, deviance, AIC/BIC, and underlying discrimination are fundamentally unchanged when recoding is done consistently.

For categorical predictors, changing the reference group changes the interpretation of group coefficients, not the raw data or overall model substance.

Coding values as 1, 2, 3, 4 does not itself make a variable continuous. Department and gender remain categorical; age/income are continuous; ordered satisfaction ratings require an explicit modeling judgment. In Jamovi, continuous predictors go to Covariates, categorical predictors to Factors, and the binary outcome to Dependent Variable.

Northfield Retail attrition case

Baseline

Northfield predicts whether an employee will leave within six months. Initial predictors are tenure, performance rating, and training hours; the .50 threshold produces only 50% sensitivity, missing half the actual leavers. The operational target is at least 70% sensitivity, but not at the cost of unusably low specificity.

Three strategies

  1. Expand predictors: add theory-relevant manager-relationship quality and career-growth signals. Potentially improves discrimination, explanation, and sensitivity, but adds survey cost, respondent burden, multicollinearity, complexity, and overfitting risk.
  2. Prune weak predictors: consider removing weak variables such as income, salary hike, working hours, or training only after theory, coefficient stability, incremental performance, and validation. Benefits include interpretability, lower collection burden, and parsimony; a variable should not be removed solely because one sample gives p>.05.
  3. Adjust threshold: test .20, .30, .40, .50, .60 and compare sensitivity, specificity, precision, FN/FP, and capacity/cost. This can improve the operational trade-off immediately but cannot repair weak AUC/discrimination.

Stakeholder trade-off

  • HR Director: catch more leavers quickly → sensitivity/recall.
  • Store Manager: simple actionable factors → interpretability/parsimony.
  • CFO: control survey and intervention cost → value of information and false-positive cost.

Recommended approach:

  1. Preserve the baseline.
  2. Diagnose AUC, calibration, sensitivity/specificity, confusion matrix, deviance/AIC/BIC, coefficients, and VIF.
  3. Build an expanded theory-based model and a defensible lean model.
  4. Validate each out of sample/cross-validation; avoid selecting on training performance.
  5. Compare likelihood fit, discrimination, calibration, stability, data cost, and actionability.
  6. On the best candidate, tune threshold using the cost matrix and ≥70% sensitivity target.
  7. Pilot deployment, monitor drift/fairness and realized retention benefit, and recalibrate.

A pruned model can reasonably be deployed despite slightly higher AIC/BIC if it validates well, reaches the sensitivity target with acceptable specificity, is stable/actionable, and produces enough cost savings to outweigh a small likelihood-fit loss. This is business utility, not a claim that higher AIC is statistically better.

Frequent mistakes to avoid

  • Saying a .72 probability is “72% certainty”; it is estimated risk conditional on model/data.

  • Treating 70% sensitivity as 70% accuracy.
  • Judging an imbalanced attrition model only by accuracy.

  • Removing every nonsignificant variable without theoretical or validation review.
  • Choosing threshold on training data without validation.
