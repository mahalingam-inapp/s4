1. Overview

A theoretical framework provides the foundation for a research study by explaining:

  • Why certain variables should be related.
  • How concepts are connected.
  • Which theories support the proposed relationships.
  • How abstract ideas can be measured and tested.

A strong research model moves from:

Theory → Constructs → Variables → Relationships → Hypotheses → Empirical Testing

2. Purpose of a Theoretical Framework

A theoretical framework is not simply a summary of previous studies. Its purpose is to organize existing knowledge into a logical explanation of how and why relationships occur.

It helps researchers:

  • Identify important variables.
  • Define concepts clearly.
  • Explain expected relationships.
  • Develop hypotheses.
  • Create a research model.

3. Role of Literature Review in Building a Framework

A literature review provides the foundation for a theoretical framework.

It involves:

  • Reviewing existing theories.
  • Examining previous findings.
  • Identifying important constructs.
  • Understanding relationships already studied.

However, a literature review alone is not a theoretical framework.

Literature Review vs Theoretical Framework

Literature Review Theoretical Framework
Summarizes existing research Explains relationships among concepts
Identifies previous findings Builds a conceptual model
Shows what is known Explains why relationships should exist
Provides evidence Provides theoretical structure

Example

Literature review:

Previous studies show that advertising influences consumer decisions.

Theoretical framework:

Advertising exposure increases purchase intention because advertising improves awareness and perceived value.

Variables:

  • Advertising exposure
  • Purchase intention
  • Sales performance

4. Constructs and Variables

Constructs

Constructs are abstract concepts that cannot be directly observed.

Examples:

  • Trust.
  • Satisfaction.
  • Environmental concern.
  • Perceived risk.
  • Employee motivation.

Researchers must define how these concepts will be measured.

Variables

Variables are measurable representations of constructs.

Example:

Construct:

Employee satisfaction

Measured through:

  • Survey questions.
  • Satisfaction scores.
  • Rating scales.

5. Operationalization

Definition

Operationalization means converting abstract concepts into measurable variables.

Example:

Abstract construct:

Employee engagement

Operational measures:

  • Work involvement score.
  • Commitment scale.
  • Survey responses.

Importance

Without operationalization:

  • Concepts remain vague.
  • Hypotheses cannot be tested.
  • Data collection becomes inconsistent.

6. Types of Variables in Research Models

Research models commonly contain:

  1. Independent variables.
  2. Dependent variables.
  3. Mediating variables.
  4. Moderating variables.
  5. Control variables.

7. Independent Variable

Definition

The independent variable is the factor expected to influence another variable.

It represents:

  • Cause.
  • Predictor.
  • Input.

Example

Research question:

Does perceived risk influence EV adoption intention?

Independent variable:

Perceived risk

Dependent variable:

Adoption intention

Relationship:

Perceived risk → Adoption intention

8. Dependent Variable

Definition

The dependent variable is the outcome being explained or predicted.

Examples:

  • Purchase intention.
  • Customer loyalty.
  • Employee turnover intention.
  • Adoption intention.

Example

EV adoption study:

Independent variable:

Perceived risk

↓

Dependent variable:

Adoption intention

9. Mediating Variable

Definition

A mediator explains how or why one variable affects another.

It describes the mechanism through which an effect occurs.

Relationship Structure

Independent variable → Mediator → Dependent variable

Example

Contract breach and employee turnover:

Contract breach

↓

Job satisfaction

↓

Turnover intention

Interpretation

Contract breach does not directly create turnover intention alone.

Instead:

  1. Employees perceive a breach.
  2. Their job satisfaction decreases.
  3. Lower satisfaction increases intention to leave.

Job satisfaction explains the process.

Mediation Question

Ask:

How does X influence Y?

10. Moderator Variable

Definition

A moderator changes the strength or direction of the relationship between two variables.

It answers:

When or for whom does this relationship become stronger or weaker?

Diagram Representation

A moderator points to the relationship/path, not directly between variables.

Example:

Environmental concern moderates:

Perceived risk → EV adoption intention

Diagram:

         Environmental Concern  
                   |  
                   ↓

Perceived Risk ----------------→ Adoption Intention

11. Moderator Examples

Example 1: EV Adoption

Relationship:

Perceived risk → Adoption intention

Moderating variable:

Environmental concern

Meaning:

For people with high environmental concern:

  • Risk has weaker negative effects.

For people with low environmental concern:

  • Risk has stronger negative effects.

Example 2: Investor Forecast Accuracy

Relationship:

Information availability → Forecast accuracy

Moderator:

Financial training

Meaning:

The effect of information is stronger for investors with higher financial training.

Example 3: Entrepreneur Networks

Relationship:

Social networks → Startup success

Moderator:

Effectuation decision logic

Meaning:

The relationship is stronger among entrepreneurs using more effectuation.

12. Mediator vs Moderator

This is one of the most important distinctions.

Feature Mediator Moderator
Main question How/why? When/for whom?
Role Explains mechanism Changes strength
Position Between variables Affects relationship
Diagram X → M → Y M points to X→Y path

Example Comparison

↓

Brand awareness:

↓

Purchase intention

Brand awareness explains why advertising works.

Moderation

Advertising exposure:

↓

Purchase intention

Moderator:

Consumer involvement

The effect of advertising depends on involvement level.

13. Control Variables

Definition

Control variables are factors included to remove alternative explanations.

They are not the main focus.

Example:

Studying:

Education → Salary

Control variables:

  • Age.
  • Experience.
  • Location.

Control vs Moderator

Control variable:

Keep constant.

Moderator:

Changes the relationship.

14. Building Hypotheses

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about relationships between variables.

Good Hypothesis Characteristics

A hypothesis should:

  • Identify variables.
  • Specify expected direction.
  • Be theoretically justified.

Example

Poor:

Advertising affects customers.

Better:

Advertising exposure positively influences purchase intention.

15. Theoretical Basis of Relationships

Relationships between variables should be based on:

  • Established theories.
  • Previous research evidence.

Not simply:

  • Personal opinion.
  • Manager experience.
  • One isolated finding.

16.1 Equity Theory

Purpose

Explains employee reactions to perceived fairness.

Core idea:

Employees compare:

  • Their contributions.
  • Their rewards.

with others.

Example

  • Complaints.
  • Lower commitment.

16.2 Relationship Marketing Theory

Purpose

Explains long-term customer relationships.

Common relationship:

Satisfaction → Trust → Loyalty

Explanation

  1. Good service creates satisfaction.
  2. Satisfaction builds trust.
  3. Trust creates loyalty.

16.3 Expectancy Disconfirmation Theory

Explains satisfaction formation.

Basic flow:

Expectations

↓

Actual experience

↓

Satisfaction

16.4 Diffusion of Innovation Theory

Explains adoption of new technologies.

Variables may include:

  • Innovation characteristics.
  • Perceived usefulness.
  • Adoption intention.

17. Common Research Model Mistakes

Mistake 1:

Including a variable without explaining its role.

Example:

Environmental concern included but not explained.

Correct:

Explain it as a moderator affecting the risk-adoption relationship.

Mistake 2:

Confusing mediation and moderation.

Incorrect:

Environmental concern sits between:

Risk → Adoption

Correct:

Environmental concern changes the strength of:

Risk → Adoption

Mistake 3:

Using correlations as theory.

A correlation shows:

Variables move together.

It does not explain:

  • Why.
  • How.
  • Under what conditions.

Mistake 4:

Creating hypotheses without theoretical support.

A hypothesis should come from:

Theory + prior evidence.

Final Principle

A well-designed theoretical framework transforms a collection of observations into a testable explanation. The key skill is correctly identifying whether a variable:

  • Causes an outcome (independent variable),
  • Is explained by another factor (dependent variable),
  • Explains the mechanism (mediator),
  • Changes the strength of a relationship (moderator),
  • Or simply accounts for alternative influences (control variable).

Case Study

Elena is Training Manager at SkillUp Solutions, tasked with improving online workshop satisfaction. Drawing on a customer satisfaction study where Independent Variables: Product Attributes and Store Attributes are controllable factors, she defines Content Quality and Instructor Credibility as independent variables. She recalls that in that study the Mediator: Customer Value explains why product/store attributes lead to satisfaction. Identifying a knowledge gap in how training features translate into satisfaction, Elena proposes exploring Perceived Usefulness as a mediator.The team screens candidate variables based on knowledge deficit and classifies roles: Mediator – Perceived Usefulness (the reason why content quality and instructor credibility lead to satisfaction); Moderators – Prior Experience and Learning Motivation (customer characteristics that change the strength of the IV–Mediator or IV–DV links). They sketch a model: Content Quality → Perceived Usefulness → Trainee Satisfaction, with arrows showing direction of effect. They also note the Influencer Marketing Study example, where Independent Variables: Attitude and Homophily affect purchase intention, moderated by Social Attractiveness, illustrating how moderator selection shapes model focus.Key stakeholders hold differing views: Elena wants a focused mediated model; Raj (Analytics Lead) advocates adding a direct path from Instructor Credibility to Satisfaction and testing both moderators; Maria (Finance Director) suggests including Time Spent as a control variable. At this decision point, the team must choose to (A) include the direct path and all moderators, (B) test only the mediated path with Learning Motivation and Prior Experience, or (C) add the control variable and simplify. Students must apply framework steps—screen variables, classify roles, draw diagrams, propose hypotheses—to recommend the optimal model structure.

Research problem → Literature review → Identify variables → Classify roles → Draw model → Develop hypotheses

19. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1:

A mediator and moderator are the same.

Reality:

Mediator explains:

Why an effect happens.

Moderator explains:

When an effect changes.

Misconception 2:

More variables always create a better model.

Reality:

A good model requires:

  • Theoretical justification.
  • Research relevance.
  • Manageable complexity.

Misconception 3:

Every variable that influences satisfaction should be included.

Reality:

Only variables that contribute to the research objective should be included.

Misconception 4:

Control variables are main predictors.

Reality:

Controls account for alternative explanations.

Misconception 5:

Direct paths are always better.

Reality:

A mediated explanation may provide deeper understanding.

Week 4 Exam Appendix

Rapid-recall chain

Business/research problem → literature and existing theories → constructs → operationalized variables → proposed relationships → hypotheses → research-model diagram → empirical testing → managerial insight

A theoretical framework is the logical, theory-supported explanation of what variables matter and how/why they relate. It is not merely a list or summary of earlier papers. It is the study’s backbone: it narrows the investigation, prevents arbitrary variable selection, guides hypotheses, measurement, analysis, and interpretation, and connects the research problem to established knowledge.

High-value distinctions

Distinction Exam-ready answer
Theory vs. theoretical framework A theory is a generalized explanation of a phenomenon; a framework selects and organizes relevant theory/constructs for the present study and proposes testable relationships.
Literature review vs. theoretical framework The review reports what is known, theories used, contexts studied, methods, and gaps. The framework uses that evidence to explain why the study’s variables should relate.
Construct vs. variable A construct is abstract (trust, satisfaction, motivation); a variable is its measurable representation.
Operationalization The conversion of an abstract construct into observable indicators or a scale, enabling consistent collection and hypothesis testing.
Independent vs. dependent variable Independent variable (IV) is the predictor/cause/input; dependent variable (DV) is the outcome to be explained or predicted.
Mediator vs. moderator A mediator explains how/why X affects Y and lies in the causal chain (X → M → Y). A moderator explains when/for whom the X→Y effect changes and acts on that path.
Full vs. partial mediation Full: X affects Y only through M. Partial: the indirect X→M→Y path and a remaining direct X→Y path both exist.
Moderator vs. control A moderator is substantively hypothesized to change a focal effect. A control is included to rule out an alternative explanation and is not the primary theoretical focus.
Correlation vs. theory/causation Correlation shows co-movement; it does not explain the mechanism, direction, or boundary condition and alone cannot establish causality.
Categorical vs. continuous variable Categorical variables represent groups/labels; continuous variables take numerical values along a scale. This classification is central to selecting a statistical test.
Nominal vs. ordinal Nominal categories have no rank (department); ordinal categories have an order but unequal/unknown intervals (low/medium/high).
Interval vs. ratio Both have meaningful equal intervals; ratio variables additionally possess a true zero, making ratios meaningful.
Discrete vs. continuous Discrete variables are counts (complaints); continuous variables can take values across a range (time, income, weight).
Parsimony vs. complexity Parsimony retains the smallest theory-justified model adequate to the problem; complexity is warranted only when extra paths/variables add meaningful explanation and remain testable.

Variable-role decision test

For every proposed variable, ask:

  1. Is it the outcome the study seeks to explain? → DV.
  2. Is it a focal predictor expected to produce/change that outcome? → IV.
  3. Does it describe the mechanism through which the IV works? → Mediator.
  4. Does it alter the strength/direction of a focal relationship? → Moderator.
  5. Is it included mainly to remove a rival explanation? → Control.
  6. How is the construct observed? → Specify its operational definition, indicators, scale, and measurement level.

Do not classify a variable permanently by name. Its role depends on the model. “Satisfaction,” for example, may be a DV in one study, a mediator between service quality and loyalty in another, or even a predictor in a retention model.

Practical model patterns

1. Direct effect

Advertising exposure → Purchase intention

Possible hypothesis: Advertising exposure positively influences purchase intention. This is superior to “advertising affects customers” because it identifies variables, direction, and a testable claim.

2. Mediation: psychological-contract breach

Contract breach → Lower job satisfaction → Turnover intention

Job satisfaction explains the process: perceived breach reduces satisfaction, which increases intention to leave. Exam cue: “How does breach cause turnover intention?”

3. Moderation: electric-vehicle adoption

Perceived risk → EV adoption intention, moderated by environmental concern.

The negative risk effect may be weaker among highly environmentally concerned consumers and stronger among those with low concern. Environmental concern acts on the risk→adoption path; it is not automatically a mediator.

4. Other moderation applications

  • Information availability improves investor forecast accuracy more strongly when investors have greater financial training.
  • Social networks contribute more strongly to start-up success when entrepreneurs use effectuation decision logic.
  • Advertising’s effect on purchase intention may depend on consumer involvement.

5. Relationship marketing

Service experience → Satisfaction → Trust → Loyalty

Relationship Marketing Theory supports the long-term formation of customer relationships: satisfactory exchanges build trust, and trust strengthens loyalty.

6. Expectancy Disconfirmation Theory

Prior expectations compared with actual performance/experience → Confirmation or disconfirmation → Satisfaction

Positive disconfirmation (performance exceeds expectations) raises satisfaction; negative disconfirmation lowers it. Use for products, hotels, apps, delivery services, and training programs.

7. Equity Theory

Employees compare their input–outcome ratio with that of referent others. Perceived unfairness can produce reduced effort, complaints, lower commitment, or turnover intention.

Use case: Two employees perceive equal effort but unequal recognition. Fairness perception can mediate or predict motivation/retention depending on the research model.

8. Diffusion of Innovation Theory

Explains adoption of innovations through perceived innovation characteristics and adopter responses. It can support models involving relative advantage/usefulness, compatibility, complexity/risk, trialability/observability, and adoption intention.

SkillUp Solutions integrated case

Problem: SkillUp must understand what drives trainee satisfaction in an online training platform and construct a useful but manageable research model.

Core variables

  • IVs: content quality and instructor credibility.
  • DV: trainee satisfaction.
  • Mediator: perceived usefulness.
  • Moderators: prior experience and learning motivation.
  • Possible control: time spent on the platform/training.

Core mediation logic

Content quality → Perceived usefulness → Trainee satisfaction

Instructor credibility → Perceived usefulness → Trainee satisfaction

High-quality content and credible instructors make training seem more useful; usefulness then improves satisfaction. A direct IV→satisfaction path may be added to test partial rather than full mediation, but it must be supported theoretically.

Moderation logic

  • Prior experience may change how strongly content quality or instructor credibility affects usefulness/satisfaction because novices and experienced learners evaluate the same material differently.
  • Learning motivation may strengthen the conversion of useful content into satisfaction or change other proposed paths.

Control logic

Time spent may independently influence satisfaction or usefulness. Controlling it estimates focal relationships after accounting for exposure. It becomes a moderator only if theory predicts that the relevant focal effect itself differs at different time-spent levels.

Stakeholder positions and model choice

  • Elena, Training Manager: favors a focused mediation model. Strength: theoretical clarity and manageability; weakness: may omit meaningful boundary conditions/direct paths.
  • Raj, Analytics Lead: favors a more comprehensive model, including direct paths/moderators. Strength: richer explanation; risk: over-complexity, larger sample needs, harder interpretation, and weakly justified paths.
  • Maria, Finance Director: emphasizes practical control variables such as time spent. Strength: reduces rival explanations; weakness: controls alone do not explain the central mechanism.

Best answer: retain the theory-supported core mediation, add only well-justified moderators, and treat time spent as a control unless moderation is explicitly theorized. Balance explanatory power with parsimony.

Illustrative hypotheses

  • H1: Content quality positively influences perceived usefulness.
  • H2: Instructor credibility positively influences perceived usefulness.
  • H3: Perceived usefulness positively influences trainee satisfaction.
  • H4: Perceived usefulness mediates the relationship between content quality and trainee satisfaction.
  • H5: Perceived usefulness mediates the relationship between instructor credibility and trainee satisfaction.
  • H6: Prior experience moderates the specified focal relationship, such that its strength differs across experience levels.
  • H7: Learning motivation moderates the specified focal relationship, such that it is stronger/weaker at higher motivation.

When writing H6/H7 in an exam, identify the exact path and expected direction; “X moderates the model” is too vague.

Model-development checklist

  1. Define the business problem, including a performance deviation or a decision-critical knowledge deficit.
  2. Convert it into a specific research problem.
  3. Search prior research and relevant theories.
  4. Identify constructs repeatedly shown to predict/explain the intended outcome.
  5. Screen candidate variables for relevance, theoretical support, knowledge contribution, and manageability.
  6. Operationalize every construct.
  7. Assign roles: IV, DV, mediator, moderator, or control.
  8. Justify direct, indirect, interaction, and control paths with theory and prior findings.
  9. Draft directional, testable hypotheses.
  10. Draw a model with arrows that accurately represent the hypotheses.
  11. Check scope, sample/data feasibility, statistical test requirements, parsimony, and contribution.

Literature-review use in framework building

A literature review should establish:

  • whether earlier researchers addressed the problem and with what solution/method;
  • theories used and variables/relationships identified;
  • contexts, samples, and methodologies previously studied;
  • consensus, contradictions, limitations, and gaps;
  • whether prior findings remain applicable to the present context.

Review approaches vary by structure and rigor, from narrative review to systematic approaches with explicit search, inclusion, screening, and synthesis rules. The TCCM framework organizes review findings into Theory, Context, Characteristics, and Methodology, helping identify where knowledge is concentrated and where gaps remain.

Search and source-quality reminders

  • Search Scopus or Google Scholar with relevant concepts/keywords and combinations.
  • Scopus indexes sources under quality/coverage criteria and allows subject/source searching; institutional subscription may be required.
  • Scopus AI can assist with textual summaries, but the underlying papers and evidence still require verification.
  • Google Scholar is free and broad but does not itself restrict results to a uniform quality tier.
  • Citation count indicates how often later works reference a paper and can be a preliminary signal of influence, not conclusive proof of quality or correctness.
  • Citation means a later researcher uses/refers to a published observation or finding and credits its source. Citation formats available in Scholar include APA, MLA, Chicago, Harvard, Vancouver, etc.; always verify formatting.
  • If a paper is inaccessible, seek institutional-library help or request it from the author.
  • There is no universal required number of papers. Depth and breadth depend on topic maturity, scope, and available research; a justified time boundary such as the latest five years can narrow an extensive literature.
  • Begin reading once a research topic/proposal is clear; do not wait unnecessarily. Research decisions require evidence, and citation supplies an auditable basis for those decisions.

Frequent mistakes to avoid

  • Summarizing studies without building an explanatory framework.
  • Adding a variable because a manager suggested it, without theory or evidence.
  • Leaving a variable’s role unexplained.
  • Placing a moderator between X and Y as though it were a mediator.
  • Treating a control as a moderator without an interaction hypothesis.
  • Calling correlation a theory or causal proof.
  • Writing non-directional, vague, or untestable hypotheses.
  • Drawing paths that do not correspond to hypotheses.
  • Overloading the model so it becomes untestable or uninterpretable.
  • Failing to operationalize abstract constructs.
  • Selecting statistical tests without checking categorical/continuous status and measurement level.
  • Assuming citation count alone guarantees research quality.
