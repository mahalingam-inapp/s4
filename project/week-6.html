1. Overview

This quiz focuses on the practical design of questionnaires and selecting appropriate data collection approaches.

A good questionnaire must balance:

  • Research objectives.
  • Respondent characteristics.
  • Data quality.
  • Ease of completion.
  • Measurement accuracy.
  • Ethical considerations.

The main concepts covered:

  1. Survey modes.
  2. Questionnaire testing.
  3. Measurement scales.
  4. Data collection tools.
  5. Data types.
  6. Questionnaire structure.
  7. Introduction and confidentiality.
  8. Placement of questions.

2. Selecting the Right Survey Mode

A survey mode determines how respondents provide answers.

Common modes:

  • Web-based questionnaires.
  • Mail surveys.
  • Telephone interviews.
  • Face-to-face interviews.

The correct choice depends on:

  • Population characteristics.
  • Accessibility.
  • Cost.
  • Interviewer influence.
  • Required depth.

3. Web-Based Self-Administered Questionnaires

Definition

A questionnaire completed independently by respondents online.

Examples:

  • Website survey.
  • Email survey.
  • In-app questionnaire.

When Suitable

Best when:

  • Population is literate.
  • Respondents are geographically dispersed.
  • Interviewer influence should be minimized.

Advantages

1. Low Interviewer Bias

Because no interviewer is present:

  • Respondents are less influenced.
  • Answers may be more honest.

2. Geographic Reach

Can reach people across many locations.

3. Efficient Data Collection

Responses can be collected automatically.

Limitation

Uneven internet access can reduce coverage.

4. Mail-Out Self-Administered Questionnaires

Definition

Paper questionnaires sent by mail.

Advantages

  • No interviewer influence.
  • Useful where internet access is limited.

Limitations

  • Slow response.
  • Printing and postage costs.
  • Lower response rates.

5. Telephone Interviews

Definition

Researchers ask questions verbally over the phone.

Advantages

  • Clarification possible.
  • Higher completion rates than some self-administered methods.

Limitations

  • Interviewer influence.
  • Limited questionnaire length.
  • Requires phone access.

6. Face-to-Face Interviews

Definition

An interviewer collects responses directly.

Advantages

  • Detailed information.
  • Can clarify questions.
  • Useful for complex topics.

Limitations

Interviewer Bias

The interviewer may influence:

  • Responses.
  • Interpretation.
  • Social desirability.

7. Questionnaire Testing

Before full deployment, questionnaires should be tested.

The main process:

  1. Draft questionnaire.
  2. Pilot test.
  3. Identify problems.
  4. Refine questions.
  5. Conduct final survey.

8. Pilot Testing

Definition

Testing a questionnaire on a small group before the main study.

Purpose

To identify:

  • Confusing wording.
  • Poor question flow.
  • Technical problems.
  • Response issues.

Example

A company tests a customer survey with 20 users before sending it to thousands.

Benefits

Improves:

  • Reliability.
  • Validity.
  • Respondent experience.

9. Face Validity vs Content Validity

These concepts are related but different.

9.1 Face Validity

Question:

Does the questionnaire appear clear and relevant to ordinary respondents?

Who Evaluates?

Typical members of the target population.

Example

Customers say:

“I understand what this question is asking.”

9.2 Content Validity

Question:

Do the items adequately cover the entire concept?

Who Evaluates?

Usually:

  • Subject experts.
  • Researchers.

Example

Experts check whether service quality questions cover:

  • Reliability.
  • Responsiveness.
  • Assurance.

10. Measurement Scales

Different variables require different measurement levels.

The main scales:

  1. Nominal.
  2. Ordinal.
  3. Interval.
  4. Ratio.

11. Nominal Scale

Definition

Categories with no order.

Examples:

  • Department.
  • Gender.
  • Location.
  • Product category.

Example

Department codes:

10 \= Finance

20 \= Operations

30 \= HR

The numbers are only labels.

Important:

Higher number does not mean more.

30 is not greater than 10.

12. Ordinal Scale

Definition

Categories with meaningful order but unequal intervals.

Examples:

  • Satisfaction level.
  • Rankings.
  • Education level.

Example:

Low

↓

Medium

↓

High

The order matters, but differences are not measurable.

13. Interval Scale

Definition

Numbers have equal intervals but no true zero.

Examples:

  • Temperature in Celsius.
  • Some attitude scales treated statistically as interval.

Difference between values is meaningful.

14. Ratio Scale

Definition

Has:

  • Equal intervals.
  • True zero.

Examples:

  • Age.
  • Income.
  • Weight.
  • Distance.

Example:

Age:

0 years has real meaning.

A person aged 40 is twice as old as someone aged 20.

15. Example Classification

Department

Nominal.

Reason:

Categories only.

Satisfaction

Ordinal.

Reason:

Low/Medium/High has order.

Age

Ratio.

Reason:

True zero and meaningful differences.

16. Structured Interview Schedule

Definition

A fixed set of questions asked verbally in the same order.

Useful when:

  • Respondents have difficulty reading.
  • Consistency is required.

Example:

Researcher asks every participant:

  1. How satisfied are you?
  2. How often do you use the service?

Advantages

  • Standardized data.
  • Reduced variation between interviews.
  • Easier coding.

18. Types of Data Collected

Research data can be classified as:

  1. Perceptual data.
  2. Behavioural data.
  3. Demographic data.
  4. Performance data.

19. Perceptual Data

Definition

Measures opinions, feelings, beliefs, and attitudes.

Examples:

  • Ease of use.
  • Trust.
  • Satisfaction.
  • Fear of job loss.

Best collection method:

Questionnaires.

Example:

“AI tools are easy to use.”

Respondent rates agreement.

20. Behavioural Data

Definition

Measures actual actions.

Examples:

  • System usage.
  • Purchase frequency.
  • Login records.

Collection methods:

  • Observation.
  • System logs.
  • Records.

21. Demographic Data

Definition

Describes respondent characteristics.

Examples:

  • Age.
  • Gender.
  • Education.
  • Department.

Purpose:

Compare groups.

Example:

Compare satisfaction across departments.

22. Performance Data

Definition

Measures outcomes or results.

Examples:

  • Productivity.
  • Sales.
  • Performance scores.

23. Questionnaire Length and Method Choice

Short surveys are often better for:

  • Mobile users.
  • Busy respondents.
  • High-response environments.

Example:

5–6 questions:

Best option:

Short electronic questionnaire.

Avoid:

  • Long surveys.
  • Time-consuming interviews.

24. Questionnaire Structure

A questionnaire should have logical sections.

Typical order:

  1. Introduction.
  2. Screening questions.
  3. Background questions.
  4. Main questions.
  5. Open-ended questions.

25. Screening Questions

Purpose

Determine whether respondents qualify.

Example:

“Have you purchased online before?”

Yes → Continue.

No → Exit.

Placement

Usually near the beginning.

Reason:

Avoid collecting unnecessary answers from ineligible participants.

26. Demographic Questions

Examples:

  • Age.
  • Department.
  • Education.

Placement

Often early or near background section.

Purpose:

Allow subgroup comparisons.

Example:

Compare satisfaction:

  • By age.
  • By department.

27. Open-Ended Questions

Definition

Questions allowing free responses.

Example:

“What improvements would you suggest?”

Placement

Usually at the end.

Reason

Open-ended questions can influence later responses.

If placed early:

  • Respondents may think deeply about issues.
  • Later ratings may become biased.

Example:

Open comment first:

“The service is terrible because…”

Then:

Rate satisfaction.

The rating may be influenced.

28. Questionnaire Introduction

A good introduction includes:

1. Study Title

Identifies topic.

2. Purpose

Explains why research is conducted.

3. Instructions

Explains how to answer.

Example:

“Select one option from 1 to 5.”

4. Confidentiality Statement

Addresses privacy concerns.

29. Confidentiality and Privacy

Respondents may worry:

“Can my answers be linked to me?”

A questionnaire should state:

  • Responses are confidential.
  • Identifying information is not collected.
  • Data will be used only for research purposes.

Importance

Improves:

  • Trust.
  • Participation.
  • Honest responses.

30. Consent Statement

Consent ensures:

  • Participants understand participation.
  • Participation is voluntary.
  • Ethical requirements are followed.

Difference:

Confidentiality:

Protects information.

Consent:

Obtains permission.

31. Common Questionnaire Design Mistakes

Mistake 1:

Using the wrong survey mode.

Example:

Using online surveys for a population without internet access.

Mistake 2:

Skipping pilot testing.

Result:

Confusing questions reach the full sample.

Mistake 3:

Poor question order.

Example:

Asking sensitive questions too early.

Mistake 4:

Mixing eligibility and main questions.

Example:

Allowing non-users to answer user satisfaction questions.

Mistake 5:

Ignoring confidentiality concerns.

Result:

Less honest answers.

Questionnaire Design Principles

Good questionnaires:

  • Use clear wording.
  • Follow logical order.
  • Protect confidentiality.
  • Avoid unnecessary bias.
  • Are tested before deployment.

Core Principle

A successful survey is not just a list of questions. It is a carefully designed measurement instrument where:

The right respondents → receive the right questions → through the right method → in the right order → producing reliable and meaningful data.

Case Study

Sara, the project lead at HealthTrack Institute, is tasked with evaluating user engagement for a new health-tracking app. The team needs a clear sampling design to ensure diverse representation across age groups, regions, and usage patterns. They consider stratified random sampling to cover key demographics and cluster sampling in major cities. To capture different response styles, they plan a Mixed Questionnaire with both closed-ended scales and open-ended prompts, allowing them to quantify satisfaction and gather user stories in one instrument.Daniel, the data analyst, evaluates several data collection tools. He recommends deploying the “Mixed Questionnaire” online for broad reach, supplemented by an “Interview Schedule (Unstructured/Semi-structured)” for in-depth feedback from a smaller group. Mia, the field researcher, proposes an “Observation Schedule” to record real-time interactions at health clinics and inviting participants to keep “Diaries, Journals & Field Notes” over a week. The team anticipates challenges such as non-response bias if certain users ignore the survey, social desirability effect in interviews, and survey fatigue from repeated contact.At the decision point, the team must choose a final plan. Option B: Combine the questionnaire with semi-structured interviews and on-site observation at clinics, using cluster sampling by region. Comprehensive Notes: HealthTrack Institute Case Study — Sampling Design, Data Collection Methods, Mixed Questionnaires, Bias Control, and Research Design Trade-offs

1. Case Overview

HealthTrack Institute wants to evaluate user engagement for a new health-tracking application.

The research challenge is to design a study that captures:

  • How users interact with the app.
  • How satisfied users are.
  • Why users behave in certain ways.
  • Differences across user groups.

The project requires decisions about:

  1. Sampling design.
  2. Questionnaire design.
  3. Data collection methods.
  4. Bias management.
  5. Balancing research depth with available resources.

2. Research Objective

The main objective:

Understand user engagement with the health-tracking app.

User engagement is a broad concept involving:

  • Frequency of use.
  • Feature interaction.
  • Satisfaction.
  • Motivation.
  • Barriers to adoption.
  • Long-term behaviour.

Because engagement has both measurable and experiential aspects, multiple data sources may be required.

3. Sampling Design

Definition

Sampling design determines:

  • Who will participate.
  • How participants are selected.
  • How representative the sample is.

A good sampling strategy ensures that findings can be applied beyond the participants studied.

4. Importance of Representation

HealthTrack wants representation across:

  • Age groups.
  • Regions.
  • Usage patterns.

Why?

Because different users may behave differently.

Example:

Young users:

  • May use fitness features frequently.

Older users:

  • May focus more on health monitoring.

Urban users:

  • May have better connectivity.

Rural users:

  • May face access challenges.

5. Stratified Random Sampling

Definition

A probability sampling method where the population is divided into meaningful subgroups (strata), and samples are randomly selected from each group.

HealthTrack Example

Population divided by:

  • Age.
  • Region.
  • Usage frequency.

Then users are randomly selected from each category.

Advantages

1. Better Representation

Ensures important groups are included.

Example:

Without stratification:

Young urban users may dominate.

With stratification:

Older and rural users are represented.

2. Improved Comparisons

Allows analysis between groups.

Example:

Compare engagement:

  • Age groups.
  • Regions.

Limitation

Requires knowledge of population characteristics.

6. Cluster Sampling

Definition

Population is divided into natural groups (clusters), and entire clusters or samples within clusters are selected.

HealthTrack Example

Cities are clusters.

Example:

Select:

  • Chennai.
  • Mumbai.
  • Delhi.

Then survey users in those cities.

Advantages

1. Lower Cost

Useful when population is geographically spread.

2. Practical Field Operations

Researchers can focus resources in selected locations.

Limitation

Selected clusters may not represent the whole population.

Goal Representation Efficiency
Example Age groups Cities
Main benefit Accuracy Lower cost

8. Mixed Questionnaire

Definition

A questionnaire combining:

  1. Closed-ended questions.
  2. Open-ended questions.

8.1 Closed-Ended Questions

Examples:

Rating scales:

“How satisfied are you?”

1–5 scale.

Advantages

  • Easy analysis.
  • Quantitative comparison.
  • Statistical testing.

8.2 Open-Ended Questions

Example:

“What improvements would you suggest?”

Advantages

  • Rich explanations.
  • User stories.
  • Unexpected insights.

Limitations

  • Harder analysis.
  • More respondent effort.

9. Why Use a Mixed Questionnaire?

HealthTrack wants both:

Quantitative Data

Examples:

  • Satisfaction score.
  • Usage frequency.
  • Feature ratings.

Qualitative Data

Examples:

  • Reasons for engagement.
  • Problems users experience.
  • Suggestions.

A mixed questionnaire combines breadth and depth.

10. Data Collection Tools

The team considers multiple tools.

Each tool provides different types of information.

11. Online Mixed Questionnaire

Purpose

Collect structured and open-ended responses digitally.

Strengths

  • Large reach.
  • Low cost.
  • Fast collection.
  • Automated data capture.

Weaknesses

  • Non-response bias.
  • Digital access limitations.
  • Less personal depth.

12. Interview Schedule

Definition

A structured guide used during interviews.

HealthTrack uses:

Unstructured/Semi-structured interviews

12.1 Semi-Structured Interviews

Combination of:

  • Prepared questions.
  • Flexible discussion.

Advantages

Provides:

  • Detailed explanations.
  • Personal experiences.
  • Reasons behind behaviour.

Example:

Question:

“How do you use the app?”

Follow-up:

“What prevents you from using it daily?”

Limitations

Social Desirability Bias

Participants may provide socially acceptable answers.

Example:

“I always exercise regularly.”

even if actual usage differs.

Resource Intensive

Requires:

  • Interviewers.
  • Time.
  • Analysis effort.

13. Observation Schedule

Definition

A structured method to record observed behaviour.

HealthTrack example:

Observe:

  • User interactions at health clinics.
  • How users interact with technology.

Advantages

Captures actual behaviour.

Not just what people say.

Example:

User claims:

“I use the app daily.”

Observation may show:

Occasional usage.

Limitations

  • Observer influence.
  • Limited context.
  • Time consuming.

14. Diaries, Journals, and Field Notes

Definition

Participants record experiences over time.

HealthTrack example:

Users maintain:

  • Weekly usage diary.
  • Health tracking experiences.
  • Challenges faced.

Advantages

Captures:

  • Longitudinal behaviour.
  • Changes over time.
  • Personal experiences.

Limitations

  • Participant burden.
  • Incomplete entries.
  • Dropout risk.

15. Online Analytics Tools

Definition

Automatically collect digital behaviour data.

Examples:

  • Clickstreams.
  • Feature usage.
  • Session duration.
  • Login frequency.

Advantages

1. Objective Behaviour Data

Measures actual usage.

2. Continuous Monitoring

Can track engagement over time.

Limitations

Cannot explain:

  • Why users behave this way.
  • User feelings.
  • Motivation.

16. Combining Data Sources

A strong research design may combine:

Survey

Answers:

“What do users think?”

Interviews

Answers:

“Why do users think this?”

Observation

Answers:

“What do users actually do?”

Analytics

Answers:

“What behaviours occur digitally?”

This creates methodological triangulation.

17. Research Bias Challenges

The case identifies several possible biases.

18. Non-Response Bias

Definition

Occurs when people who do not respond differ from those who respond.

Example:

Highly satisfied users complete surveys.

Frustrated users ignore them.

Result:

Survey overestimates satisfaction.

Control Methods

  • Reminders.
  • Incentives.
  • Multiple contact methods.
  • Comparing respondents and non-respondents.

19. Social Desirability Bias

Definition

Participants answer in a way that appears socially acceptable.

Example:

Interview response:

“I always follow healthy habits.”

Actual behaviour:

Irregular usage.

Control Methods

  • Confidentiality.
  • Neutral wording.
  • Self-administered surveys.

20. Survey Fatigue

Definition

Respondent tiredness due to repeated or lengthy surveys.

Effects:

  • Short answers.
  • Random responses.
  • Dropout.

Control Methods

  • Short questionnaires.
  • Avoid unnecessary questions.
  • Reduce contact frequency.

21. Option Evaluation

The team considers three strategies.

Option A

Online Mixed Questionnaire + Stratified Sampling + Diaries

Components:

  • Broad survey.
  • Representative sampling.
  • Longitudinal depth.

Strengths

  • Good representation.
  • Quantitative measurement.
  • Some qualitative depth.

Weaknesses

  • Diaries increase respondent burden.
  • Still depends on self-reported data.
  • Limited explanation compared with interviews.

Option B

Questionnaire + Semi-Structured Interviews + Clinic Observation + Cluster Sampling

Components:

  • Survey.
  • Interviews.
  • Observation.
  • Regional clusters.

Strengths

1. Rich Data

Captures:

  • Opinions.
  • Experiences.
  • Behaviour.

2. Triangulation

Multiple methods validate findings.

3. Bias Reduction

Observation reduces dependence on self-report.

Weaknesses

  • More expensive.
  • More complex.
  • Cluster sampling may reduce generalisability.

Option C

Analytics + Diaries + Limited Questioning

Components:

  • Passive data.
  • Longitudinal records.

Strengths

  • Low respondent burden.
  • Objective behavioural information.

Weaknesses

Misses:

  • User motivations.
  • Satisfaction.
  • Perceptions.

22. Best Strategic Approach

The strongest balanced approach is generally:

Option B:

Combine questionnaire with semi-structured interviews and observation.

Reason:

It balances:

Data Richness

From:

  • Interviews.
  • Observation.
  • Open-ended responses.

Bias Control

Through:

  • Behavioural observation.
  • Multiple sources.

Practical Constraints

Cluster sampling makes fieldwork manageable.

23. Triangulation Principle

Definition

Using multiple methods to study the same phenomenon.

Example:

User engagement:

Survey:

“I am satisfied.”

Analytics:

User logs in frequently.

Interview:

“I use the app because it helps manage my health.”

When all agree:

Confidence increases.

24. Mixed Methods Research

Definition

Combines quantitative and qualitative approaches.

Quantitative:

Measures:

  • How much?
  • How often?

Qualitative:

Explains:

  • Why?
  • How?

Example:

Survey:

80% users are satisfied.

Interview:

Users explain why.

25. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1:

Analytics alone can explain engagement.

Reality:

Analytics shows behaviour, not motivation.

Misconception 2:

Surveys always represent users.

Reality:

Non-response bias may distort results.

Misconception 3:

More methods always mean better research.

Reality:

Methods should match objectives and resources.

Misconception 4:

Cluster sampling is always inferior.

Reality:

Cluster sampling is useful when geography and cost are important.

Misconception 5:

Interviews provide completely accurate answers.

4. Stratified Random Sampling

Definition

A probability sampling method where the population is divided into important subgroups (strata), and samples are selected from each group.

HealthTrack Example

Users divided by:

  • Age.
  • Region.
  • Usage pattern.

Then participants are randomly selected from each category.

Purpose

To ensure important groups are represented.

With stratification:

  • Young users.
  • Older users.
  • Rural users.
  • Urban users.

Advantages

1. Better Representation

2. Group Comparisons

  • Frequent vs occasional users.

Limitation

Requires information about the population structure.

Definition

A probability sampling method where natural groups are treated as clusters, and selected clusters are studied.

Clusters may not represent the entire population.

Respondents provide answers that make them appear better.

  • Limit repeated contact.

People change behaviour because they know they are being observed.

Exam Study Guide Appendix

Exam-Oriented Appendix

A. Practical examples and use cases

1. Defining and operationalising a construct

Business problem: An organisation wants to study employee motivation.

  • Conceptual definition: internal and external forces that stimulate employees’ energy, interest, and commitment toward work.
  • Contextual definition: a nonprofit employee’s self-reported work drive, with emphasis on mission-oriented rather than financial motivation.
  • Operational definition: the summed score on ten Likert-scale statements measuring interest, energy, persistence, and commitment.

The conceptual definition establishes meaning; the contextual definition fixes the study boundary; the operational definition states exactly how data will represent the construct.

2. Developing a new construct: digital well-being

  1. Give the phenomenon a clear, non-jargon label and provisional conceptual definition.
  2. Delineate its conceptual space: distinguish digital well-being from physical well-being, digital literacy, satisfaction, and technology addiction.
  3. Build a theoretical canvas of dimensions/indicators.
  4. Decide whether indicators are reflective (effects/reflections of the construct) or formative (jointly create the construct).
  5. Establish discrimination from related constructs.
  6. Test whether it can be measured reliably in practice.
  7. Refine the definition and locate it in a theoretical model by specifying antecedents and outcomes.

Use case: A platform studies whether notification overload and loss of control reduce digital well-being, which then predicts discontinuance intention.

3. SERVQUAL as construct-development illustration

Service quality was created to capture intangible quality that product-quality concepts did not adequately represent. Its developers separated the construct from tangible product quality, identified five dimensions represented by 22 items, tested it across settings such as banking, insurance, and audit services, and linked perceived service quality to satisfaction. The well-known definition compares a customer’s service expectations with perceptions of service received.

Exam lesson: A useful construct must be conceptually distinct, measurable, and theoretically connected to meaningful outcomes.

5. Questionnaire development example

Study: adoption of online banking.

  • Define constructs from theory: perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, trust, and intention.
  • Generate/adapt several scale items for every latent construct plus preference-mapping questions.
  • Ask subject experts to evaluate whether items are essential and cover the construct (content validity).
  • Ask target respondents whether wording appears clear and relevant (face validity).
  • Pilot the draft on a small group resembling the target population.
  • Inspect ambiguity, completion time, missing answers, weak items, reliability, and preliminary validity.
  • Revise/drop weak items, then administer to the full sample.

If five constructs each use five indicators, the construct section contains 25 statements. The exact questionnaire length is driven by objectives and respondent burden, not a universal rule.

6. Questionnaire structure in practice

  1. Introduction: study title/purpose, researcher identity where appropriate, estimated time, instructions, voluntary participation/consent, confidentiality/privacy, and contact/ethical information.
  2. Screening questions: establish eligibility early.
  3. General behaviour and preference-mapping questions.
  4. Scale items measuring theoretical constructs.
  5. Sensitive/demographic questions, usually later unless needed for screening.
  6. Open-ended comments, normally near the end.

Avoid leading, loaded, ambiguous, technical, double-barrelled, double-negative, excessively long, and overlapping-response questions.

7. Survey-mode choice

  • Web self-administered: geographically dispersed, digitally reachable population; inexpensive, fast, low interviewer bias, but affected by coverage and non-response bias.
  • Mail-out questionnaire: useful where postal lists are available or internet access is weak; no interviewer pressure, but slow, costly, and commonly low response.
  • Telephone interview: quick clarification and moderate reach; excludes inaccessible numbers and may be constrained by brevity, interruption, and interviewer effects.
  • Face-to-face interview: best for complex questions, probing, low-literacy populations, or visual aids; richer and often more complete, but expensive, slow, and susceptible to interviewer/social-desirability bias.

9. HealthTrack mixed-methods use case

Objective: understand engagement with a digital health service across age, gender, location, socioeconomic status, and clinics.

  • Stratified random sampling is preferable when representation and subgroup comparison are the priority: split the population into relevant strata and randomly sample within each.
  • Cluster sampling is preferable when travel/field cost dominates: randomly select clinics/geographic clusters and study people within them, accepting potentially greater sampling error because members of a cluster may resemble each other.
  • Closed-ended questionnaire items quantify use, satisfaction, barriers, and attitudes.
  • Open-ended questions/interviews explain motives and experiences.
  • Observation captures clinic behaviour.
  • Diaries record engagement over time.
  • Analytics show actual logins, feature use, and duration.

The duplicated review material recommends different “best” options under different assumed priorities. The defensible exam answer is conditional: choose the stratified online-questionnaire/diary design when representation and lower cost dominate; choose questionnaire + interviews + observation with selected clinics when depth, triangulation, and practical field access dominate. State the trade-off instead of claiming a universally superior option.

10. Bias-control applications

  • Non-response bias: reminders, multiple contact modes/times, short accessible survey, incentives where ethical, compare respondents with known population characteristics, and report response rates.
  • Social-desirability bias: anonymity/confidentiality, neutral indirect wording, self-administration for sensitive items, trained neutral interviewers.
  • Survey fatigue: keep only purpose-relevant items, use clear sections/progress indication, pilot completion time, avoid repetitive items.
  • Hawthorne effect: unobtrusive or repeated observation, allow acclimatisation, avoid signalling desired behaviour, triangulate with routine analytics.
  • Interviewer bias: standard schedule, training, neutral probes, consistent recording.

B. High-yield distinctions

Construct vs concept vs variable vs indicator

  • A concept is a general abstract idea.
  • A construct is deliberately defined for theoretical/scientific use, often latent and multidimensional.
  • A variable is a characteristic capable of taking different values in a study.
  • An indicator/item is an observable response used to measure a construct.

Conceptual vs contextual vs operational definition

  • Conceptual: what the construct means generally.
  • Contextual: what it means within this population, place, sector, or situation.
  • Operational: exactly how it will be observed, scored, or calculated.

Reflective vs formative measurement

  • Reflective: construct → indicators; indicators are manifestations and should generally covary. Satisfaction causes respondents to endorse several satisfaction statements.
  • Formative: indicators → construct; indicators jointly form it and need not covary. Income, education, and occupation may form a socioeconomic-status index.

Qualitative vs quantitative data

  • Qualitative data records meanings, experiences, explanations, text, images, or categories.
  • Quantitative data records numbers suitable for mathematical/statistical analysis.
  • A coded number is not automatically continuous quantitative measurement: 1 = HR, 2 = Finance remains nominal.

Primary vs secondary data

  • Primary is collected specifically for the present study.
  • Secondary already exists and is reused. Secondary data is efficient but must be checked for relevance, definitions, quality, period, coverage, access, and ethics.

Perceptual vs behavioural vs demographic vs performance data

  • Perceptual: attitudes, beliefs, satisfaction, intention.
  • Behavioural: actions, purchase/usage frequency, choice.
  • Demographic: age, gender, education, income, location.
  • Performance: sales, profit, productivity, service time, conversion.

Nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio scales

  • Nominal: identity only; no order.
  • Ordinal: order exists; gaps are not demonstrably equal.
  • Interval: equal intervals; no true zero.
  • Ratio: equal intervals and true zero; meaningful ratios.

Mnemonic: N-O-I-R = Name, Order, equal Intervals, real zero (each level adds a property).

Questionnaire vs interview schedule

A questionnaire is generally completed by the respondent; an interview schedule is administered and recorded by an interviewer. Both may be structured, but the schedule permits clarification while creating interviewer effects and higher cost.

Open-ended vs closed-ended questions

  • Closed: fast, standardised, easy to code/compare; may constrain answers.
  • Open: depth and unexpected insight; greater effort, non-response, coding time, and subjectivity.
  • A mixed questionnaire combines both to obtain breadth and depth.

Face validity vs content validity vs pilot testing

  • Face validity: does the item appear clear and appropriate, especially to target respondents?
  • Content validity: do experts judge that the item set adequately and relevantly covers the construct domain?
  • Pilot test: does the complete instrument and administration process work with a small target-like sample?

The source mentions an expert-based content-validity score/ratio and a tentative threshold around 0.60; the acceptable critical value depends on the number of experts and chosen method, so state the method rather than treating 0.60 as universal.

Stratified vs cluster sampling

  • Stratified: sample from every stratum; improves representation and subgroup precision.
  • Cluster: select some clusters and study members within them; improves operational efficiency but can reduce precision.
  • Strata should be internally relatively homogeneous and differ across strata; clusters ideally resemble mini-populations but often contain correlated members.

Mixed questionnaire vs mixed methods vs triangulation

  • Mixed questionnaire: closed and open questions in one instrument.
  • Mixed methods: intentionally integrates quantitative and qualitative approaches/data.
  • Triangulation: compares evidence from multiple methods/sources/observers/theories to improve credibility and reveal convergence or contradiction.

  2. Describe the stages in creating a new construct. Label/core meaning → conceptual boundary → theoretical canvas/indicator direction → discrimination → operational potential → measurement items/refinement → theoretical linkages.
  3. Explain SERVQUAL as construct development. Intangibility gap, distinct domain, five dimensions/22 items, testing across service contexts, expectation–perception logic, satisfaction link.
  4. Classify research data in different ways. Nature (qualitative/quantitative), source (primary/secondary), measurement level (NOIR), time (cross-sectional/time series/panel), and context (perceptual/behavioural/demographic/performance).
  5. Why does scale type matter? It determines valid summaries and analysis; labels cannot be averaged merely because they have numeric codes.
  6. Describe questionnaire-development steps. Constructs/theory → item pool and preference questions → expert content validation → respondent face validation → pilot → reliability/validity checks → revise/finalise → full administration.
  7. Differentiate face and content validity. Evaluator, question answered, and example.
  8. Design a questionnaire introduction and order. Purpose, instructions, confidentiality/privacy, consent/voluntariness, eligibility screening, logical sections, sensitive demographics later, optional open comment at end.
  9. Compare web, mail, telephone, and face-to-face collection. Reach, cost, speed, clarification, digital/literacy requirements, response, and interviewer bias.

  11. Explain quantitative and qualitative collection tools. Surveys/structured schedules/experiments versus interviews/focus groups/observation/diaries; note mixed designs.

D. Rapid revision traps

  • A construct is not adequately defined by giving only its measurement items.
  • Operationalisation before conceptual boundary-setting risks measuring the wrong phenomenon.
  • Numeric codes do not change nominal categories into interval data.
  • Ordinal categories have rank but not guaranteed equal distances.
  • A Likert item is ordinal; a carefully validated multi-item scale is often treated as approximately continuous, but this requires justification.
  • A pilot test is not the full study and not merely proofreading.
  • Face validity alone does not prove actual construct validity.
  • Open-ended questions do not automatically make an entire study qualitative.
  • Closed-ended questions do not automatically eliminate bias.
  • Online surveys reduce interviewer bias but may increase coverage/non-response bias.
  • Observation captures behaviour, not necessarily motive; observed people may alter behaviour.
  • Analytics is behavioural trace data, not direct evidence of satisfaction or meaning.
  • Cluster sampling and stratified sampling are not interchangeable.
  • More methods are not automatically better: every method must answer a defined question and be feasibly integrated.
  • Confidentiality means identities are protected; anonymity means identities are not collected/linkable. Do not promise anonymity if follow-up identifiers exist.
