1. Overview

This quiz focuses on the measurement stage of quantitative research using Jamovi, particularly:

  • Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA).
  • Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA).
  • Structural Equation Modelling (SEM).
  • Measurement assessment.
  • Reliability and validity.
  • Common Method Bias (CMB).
  • Data suitability checks.

The central principle:

Before testing relationships between constructs, researchers must first establish that the constructs are measured correctly.

A good research workflow is:

Data preparation → Exploratory analysis → Measurement validation → Structural modelling → Hypothesis testing

2. Measurement Assessment

Definition

Measurement assessment evaluates whether survey items properly represent the intended constructs.

Example:

A researcher wants to measure:

  • Service quality.
  • Customer satisfaction.
  • Purchase intention.

Each construct is represented by multiple questionnaire items.

Questions answered during measurement assessment:

  1. Do the items group together as expected?
  2. Are items reliable?
  3. Are constructs distinct from each other?

Main techniques:

  • EFA.
  • CFA.
  • Reliability analysis.
  • Validity assessment.

3. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

Purpose

EFA is used when the underlying factor structure is uncertain.

It explores:

  • How many factors exist.
  • Which items belong together.
  • Possible latent dimensions.

Example:

A questionnaire contains 20 items.

Researcher does not know whether they represent:

  • 3 factors.
  • 4 factors.
  • 5 factors.

EFA discovers the structure.

4. When to Use EFA

Use EFA when:

  • Developing a new scale.
  • Adapting items from different sources.
  • Exploring unknown dimensions.

Example:

A new student engagement questionnaire.

The researcher wants to discover:

  • Motivation factor.
  • Participation factor.
  • Technology acceptance factor.

5. EFA Process

Typical EFA workflow:

  1. Check data suitability.
  2. Extract factors.
  3. Decide number of factors.
  4. Rotate factors.
  5. Interpret loadings.

6. KMO Test

Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO)

Measures sampling adequacy.

It evaluates whether correlations among items are suitable for factor analysis.

Interpretation:

Higher KMO \= better factorability.

Common guidelines:

KMO Interpretation
\<0.5 Poor
0.5–0.7 Acceptable
0.7–0.8 Good
0.8+ Very good

Example:

KMO \= 0.84

Interpretation:

Very good suitability.

7. Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity

Purpose

Tests whether the correlation matrix contains sufficient relationships for factor analysis.

Null hypothesis:

Items are uncorrelated.

Desired result:

p \< 0.05

Meaning:

Significant correlations exist.

Example:

p \= 0.30

Interpretation:

Not significant.

Problem:

Items may not correlate enough for meaningful factors.

8. KMO and Bartlett Together

Both should be considered.

Example 1:

KMO \= 0.84

Bartlett p \< 0.001

Conclusion:

Suitable for EFA.

Example 2:

KMO \= 0.84

Bartlett p \= 0.30

Conclusion:

Sampling adequacy is good, but correlations may be insufficient.

Need to review:

  • Item correlations.
  • Poor items.
  • Measurement design.

9. Handling Poor Bartlett Results

Correct approach:

Review the correlation matrix.

Look for:

  • Near-zero correlations.
  • Items unrelated to others.
  • Poorly worded questions.

Possible action:

Remove or revise problematic items.

Then rerun EFA.

Incorrect approaches:

Simply changing rotation

Rotation changes interpretation, not whether correlations exist.

Ignoring Bartlett

A factor solution does not automatically mean meaningful factors.

Only collecting more data

Larger samples may increase statistical power, but weak relationships among items remain a conceptual problem.

10. Factor Retention

A key EFA decision:

How many factors should be retained?

Common approaches:

Eigenvalue > 1 rule

Traditional method.

Limitation:

Can overestimate factors.

Parallel Analysis

More recommended.

Compares observed eigenvalues with random data.

Advantages:

More accurate factor retention.

11. Factor Rotation

Rotation improves interpretation.

Two major types:

Orthogonal Rotation

Example:

Varimax.

Assumes factors are unrelated.

Oblique Rotation

Allows factors to correlate.

Often appropriate for social science constructs.

Important:

Rotation does not create correlations.

It only improves interpretability.

12. PCA vs EFA

These are often confused.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Purpose:

Data reduction.

Creates components explaining maximum variance.

Question:

“How can I summarize many variables?”

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

Purpose:

Discover latent constructs.

Question:

“What hidden factors explain item relationships?”

Difference:

PCA focuses on observed variance.

EFA focuses on underlying constructs.

13. Common Method Bias (CMB)

Definition

Common Method Bias occurs when the measurement method creates artificial relationships among variables.

Example:

Same questionnaire.

Same respondent.

Same time.

May inflate correlations.

14. Harman’s Single Factor Test

A traditional CMB screening approach.

Procedure:

  1. Put all items into PCA.
  2. Do not rotate.
  3. Allow all components to emerge.
  4. Examine first unrotated component variance.

Interpretation:

If one factor dominates heavily, common method bias may be a concern.

15. Correct PCA Setup for CMB

Correct:

  • PCA.
  • No rotation.
  • No forced number of components.
  • Examine first unrotated component.

Incorrect:

Using rotated PCA

Rotation redistributes variance.

Not suitable for single-factor bias assessment.

Extracting only one component

This forces a one-factor solution.

It does not test whether one factor naturally dominates.

16. VIF and CMB

Variance Inflation Factor (VIF):

Measures multicollinearity.

Low VIF:

Suggests predictors are not highly redundant.

However:

VIF does not directly prove absence of CMB.

Correct interpretation:

Low VIF supports analysis quality but does not replace CMB testing.

17. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)

Purpose

CFA tests a predefined measurement structure.

Unlike EFA:

EFA asks:

“What structure exists?”

CFA asks:

“Does my proposed structure fit?”

Example:

Theory states:

Customer satisfaction has five items:

SA1–SA5.

CFA tests:

Do these five items load onto one satisfaction factor?

18. EFA vs CFA

EFA CFA
Exploratory Confirmatory
No fixed structure Predefined model
Discovers factors Tests factors
Early scale development Validation stage

Researcher finds:

5 factors.

Test whether the five-factor model works.

Use:

CFA.

22. SEM Output Interpretation

A complete SEM assessment includes:

1. Standardised path estimates (Beta)

Shows relationship strength.

Example:

Service Quality → Satisfaction

β \= 0.65

2. Model fit indices

Evaluate whether the proposed model fits the data.

Examples:

  • CFI.
  • TLI.
  • GFI.

3. Reliability measures

Assess consistency.

Examples:

  • Cronbach’s Alpha.
  • Composite Reliability.

4. Validity measures

Assess construct quality.

Examples:

  • AVE.
  • HTMT.

23. AVE (Average Variance Extracted)

Purpose

Measures convergent validity.

Question:

Do items strongly represent their construct?

Higher AVE indicates:

The construct explains more variance in its indicators.

24. HTMT (Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio)

Purpose

Tests discriminant validity.

Question:

Are different constructs actually distinct?

Example:

Customer satisfaction and service quality should not be identical constructs.

HTMT helps verify separation.

25. Reliability vs Validity

Reliability

Question:

Are items consistent?

Example:

Do satisfaction items behave similarly?

Validity

Question:

Are we measuring the correct concept?

A scale can be:

Reliable but invalid.

Example:

A consistently wrong measurement.

26. Measurement Assessment Phase

The correct phase name:

Measurement assessment

It includes:

  • EFA.
  • CFA.
  • Reliability.
  • Validity checks.

Not:

Data preparation.

Not:

Assumption testing.

27. SEM vs Regression

Regression

Tests relationships among observed variables.

Example:

Advertising → Sales.

SEM

Tests:

  • Measurement model.
  • Structural relationships.

Allows:

Latent variables.

Example:

Service Quality → Satisfaction.

29. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1:

EFA proves the model is correct.

Reality:

EFA only explores.

CFA is required for confirmation.

Misconception 2:

Good KMO means EFA is automatically valid.

Reality:

Bartlett’s test and correlations must also be considered.

Misconception 3:

Rotation determines factor existence.

Reality:

Rotation only improves interpretation.

Misconception 4:

PCA and EFA are the same.

Reality:

PCA reduces data.

EFA identifies latent constructs.

Misconception 5:

Low VIF proves no common method bias.

Reality:

VIF only addresses multicollinearity.

Misconception 6:

Significant paths prove good measurement.

Reality:

Measurement validity must be established separately.

Explore → Validate → Test relationships

Case Study

Mei Chen, a data analyst at FreshCafé Foods, is tasked with refining a customer satisfaction survey. Using Jamovi, she begins with Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) to assess sample-level measurement quality. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure is 0.58 (above the required 0.5), and Bartlett’s test is significant. Most factor loadings exceed 0.5, but one item loads at 0.28, below the acceptable threshold of 0.3. Cross-loading on another factor is 0.35, above the 0.3 concern level. The team must decide whether to drop or retain this item. Next, Dr. Patel runs Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) in Jamovi’s SEM module. Two items show standardized loadings under 0.5. Cronbach’s alpha for each construct is above 0.7, and composite reliability exceeds 0.7. Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for brand image is 0.47, below the required 0.5. The HTMT ratio between service and product quality is 0.87, exceeding the 0.85 threshold. Fit indices report RMSEA \= 0.09 (target \< 0.08), CFI \= 0.88 (> 0.90), TLI \= 0.89 (> 0.90), and SRMR \= 0.07 (\< 0.06). James Lopez, the marketing manager, worries that removing items may overlook customer insights, while Mei emphasizes improving model fit. Dr. Patel suggests an iterative approach: if items with loadings below 0.5 are dropped, then rerun CFA and reassess AVE and fit indices. If HTMT remains above 0.85, they might refine item wording. Students must recommend which adjustments to prioritize, balancing content validity, reliability benchmarks, and project deadlines.

1. Case Overview

Mei Chen, a data analyst at FreshCafé Foods, is refining a customer satisfaction survey.

The objective is to ensure that the survey:

  • Measures customer satisfaction constructs accurately.
  • Contains reliable items.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) → Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) → Reliability and Validity Assessment → Model Refinement

2. Importance of Measurement Quality

Before using survey constructs for decision-making, researchers must verify that the questionnaire actually measures what it intends to measure.

A survey may produce statistically significant relationships but still be poor if:

  • Items do not represent the construct.
  • Constructs overlap too much.
  • The measurement model fits poorly.

Measurement quality has three major dimensions:

  1. Reliability.
  2. Convergent validity.
  3. Discriminant validity.

3. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

Purpose

EFA is used during early measurement development to discover the underlying structure of survey items.

It answers:

  • How many factors exist?
  • Which items belong to each factor?
  • Do items group according to expectations?

In FreshCafé:

4. EFA Suitability Checks

Before interpreting factor loadings, researchers must verify that data is suitable for factor analysis.

Two major checks:

  1. Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO).
  2. Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity.

5. Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO)

Purpose

KMO measures sampling adequacy.

It evaluates whether the correlation pattern among items is suitable for factor extraction.

General interpretation:

KMO Value Interpretation
Below 0.5 Unsuitable
0.5–0.7 Acceptable
0.7–0.8 Good
Above 0.8 Very good

Interpretation:

However:

Important:

A good KMO does not guarantee good factors.

Tests whether variables have sufficient correlations.

Null hypothesis:

The correlation matrix is an identity matrix.

Items are unrelated.

p \< 0.05

10. Decision: Drop or Retain Problematic Items

The team faces a trade-off.

Argument for Dropping Items

Advantages:

  • Improves factor clarity.
  • Improves model fit.
  • Increases construct validity.

Argument for Retaining Items

  • Avoids losing important aspects of the construct.

11. Content Validity vs Statistical Improvement

Important balance:

A statistically cleaner model is not always better.

Example:

Removing an item may improve CFA fit.

But:

If the removed item captures an important customer experience, the construct becomes incomplete.

The decision should consider:

  • Loading strength.
  • Cross-loading.
  • Theoretical importance.
  • Managerial relevance.

Exam Study Guide Appendix

Week 10 Exam Appendix

Rapid-recall workflow

Prepare and label data → inspect missing values/outliers/distribution → test independence and multicollinearity → check sample-size adequacy and common-method bias → explore measurement structure (EFA) → confirm it (CFA) → establish reliability and convergent/discriminant validity → judge overall model fit → refine with theory and content validity intact → test structural relationships

The central lesson is that relationships between constructs should not be interpreted until the data and measurement model are sufficiently trustworthy. A statistically improved model is not necessarily a better substantive instrument if important content has been deleted.

High-value distinctions

Distinction Exam-ready answer
General data quality vs. measurement quality vs. analysis-specific assumptions General checks concern missingness, outliers, distribution, independence, sample adequacy, etc.; measurement checks ask whether items measure latent constructs reliably/validly; analysis-specific checks depend on the final test/model.
PCA vs. EFA PCA reduces total observed variance into components; EFA models shared/common variance to discover latent factors. PCA can be used for Harman’s diagnostic, but EFA is conceptually appropriate for latent measurement exploration.
EFA vs. CFA EFA discovers a plausible factor structure when it is uncertain; CFA tests a prespecified theory-based item–factor structure. “EFA explores; CFA confirms.”
CFA vs. SEM CFA evaluates the measurement model (constructs and indicators); SEM combines measurement with structural paths among constructs.
Reliability vs. validity Reliability is consistency; validity is whether the intended construct is actually measured. Reliability is necessary but does not guarantee validity.
Convergent vs. discriminant validity Convergent: indicators of the same construct share sufficient variance. Discriminant: theoretically different constructs remain empirically distinct.
Cronbach’s alpha vs. composite reliability Both assess internal consistency; alpha makes stronger equal-loading assumptions, whereas CR uses estimated CFA loadings/error and is often preferred in SEM.
Factor loading vs. cross-loading Loading is an item’s strength on its intended factor; cross-loading means it also loads materially on another factor, weakening interpretability/discriminant separation.
KMO vs. Bartlett KMO assesses whether correlation patterns/sample adequacy suit factor analysis; Bartlett tests whether the correlation matrix differs from an identity matrix. Neither proves the final factor solution is good.
Model fit vs. path significance Fit indices judge the model as a whole; a significant loading/path judges an individual parameter. Good paths can coexist with poor overall fit and vice versa.
CMB vs. multicollinearity Common-method bias is systematic covariance caused by the shared measurement method; multicollinearity is excessive association among predictors/items. High VIF may be a warning, but is not a complete CMB test.

Preliminary Jamovi/data checks

1. Define variables correctly

Before analysis, assign measurement types and value labels. Demographic/group fields such as gender, shopping mode, education, income category, and shop preference are categorical/nominal as defined in the case; Likert responses from strongly disagree to strongly agree are ordinal items (even if later treated approximately as continuous under justified practice). Incorrect typing produces inappropriate menus/results.

2. Missing values and outliers

Use descriptives to report N, missing values, minima/maxima, and extreme observations. In bounded 1–5 Likert items, extreme allowed responses are not automatically erroneous outliers. Continuous values such as monthly sales can contain unusual observations that materially distort means and models; investigate their validity before retaining, correcting, transforming, or excluding them.

3. Normality

Shapiro–Wilk tests:

  • H₀: sample distribution does not significantly depart from normality.
  • p < .05: reject H₀; evidence of non-normality.
  • p ≥ .05: fail to reject H₀; normality is not contradicted (not proof of perfect normality).

With social-science samples, Shapiro–Wilk can detect small departures. Inspect skewness, kurtosis, plots, residuals, sample size, and the robustness of the intended estimator. The lecture’s practical thumb rules are approximately |skewness| < 2 and |kurtosis| < 5; its case had all items within those bounds even though Shapiro–Wilk was significant, so parametric analysis proceeded cautiously.

4. Independence and multicollinearity

  • Durbin–Watson near 2 indicates little residual autocorrelation; the lecture uses roughly 1.5–2.5 as acceptable (case ≈1.8).
  • Tolerance should exceed approximately .20.
  • VIF should remain low; a large VIF indicates overlapping predictors and unstable coefficient estimates. Report the rule used rather than treating one cutoff as universal.

5. Sample-size adequacy/power

Use power analysis rather than only a rule of thumb. Inputs include alpha, desired power, smallest effect worth detecting, predictors/model complexity, and analysis type. The demonstration used effect ≈.20 and found minimum n≈191 for power .80 and n≈255 for power .90; the actual n=385 was adequate for the demonstrated general linear model assumptions. A larger SEM may require different planning.

6. Common-method bias (CMB)

CMB can occur when one respondent provides all variables with one questionnaire at one time using similar response formats.

Harman single-factor diagnostic: place all measurement items in an unrotated PCA/EFA-style extraction and inspect variance explained by the first factor. If the first factor exceeds about 50%, CMB is a concern; in the case it explained only 17.2%, so the basic diagnostic did not indicate a dominant common factor.

Do not rotate the solution or force a one-component extraction and then misapply the rule. Harman’s test and VIF are only diagnostics, not definitive proof of absence. Prefer procedural prevention too: clear wording, anonymity, separating predictor/outcome sections or collection times/sources when feasible.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

Suitability checks

  • KMO: below .50 unsuitable; .50–.70 acceptable/marginal; .70–.80 good; above .80 very good.
  • Bartlett’s test: p < .05 indicates correlations are collectively sufficient to reject an identity matrix.

FreshCafé: KMO=.58 (only marginal/acceptable) and significant Bartlett. Factor analysis may proceed cautiously, but item correlations/sample adequacy warrant scrutiny. Increasing sample size alone will not always fix poor inter-item structure; changing rotation does not repair a nonsignificant Bartlett test.

Factor retention

Do not rely exclusively on eigenvalue >1. Use theory, scree plot, parallel analysis, interpretability, and expected construct count. Parallel analysis compares observed eigenvalues with those generated from random data and generally offers a stronger retention rule.

Rotation

  • Orthogonal rotation assumes factors are uncorrelated.
  • Oblique rotation allows factors to correlate and is often realistic for business/behavioral constructs such as service quality, satisfaction, and brand image.

Items and loadings

Useful heuristics in the source:

  • >.70: strong loading;
  • .50–.70: acceptable;
  • .30–.50: weak/moderate;
  • <.30: problematic.

FreshCafé had a weak item loading .28 and another cross-loading .35 (e.g., .45 on service and .35 on product quality). These are review flags—not automatic deletion commands.

Before deletion, ask:

  1. Is the item poorly or ambiguously worded?
  2. Is it reverse-coded incorrectly?
  3. Does it measure a unique, theoretically essential facet?
  4. Does removal improve reliability/validity/fit materially?
  5. Would deletion narrow the construct and damage content validity?

CFA, reliability, and validity

CFA specification

Assign each indicator to its hypothesized construct; estimate standardized loadings and errors; check individual significance/CI; then evaluate reliability, validity, and global fit. Standardized loadings generally should be about .50 or higher, preferably .70+, though theoretical/content considerations remain essential.

For a constrained reference indicator, software may not report a p-value because its unstandardized loading is fixed for scale identification. Its standardized loading/CI can still be inspected; an interval excluding zero supports a nonzero loading.

Reliability

  • Cronbach’s alpha >.70: common internal-consistency guideline.
  • Composite reliability >.70: common SEM guideline.

FreshCafé met both. Yet reliability alone cannot establish that the right construct is measured or that different constructs are distinct.

Convergent validity

Average Variance Extracted (AVE) is the average proportion of indicator variance captured by its construct. Guideline: AVE ≥ .50.

FreshCafé’s Brand Image AVE was .47, slightly low: indicators collectively contain more error than ideal. Review weak indicators and theory rather than mechanically deleting until .50 is reached.

Discriminant validity

HTMT estimates similarity between constructs. Guideline: <.85 (some contexts use <.90). FreshCafé’s .87 fails the stricter criterion and suggests overlap. Remedies: inspect wording, remove genuinely redundant items, reconsider whether constructs are conceptually distinct, or re-specify theory. Do not hide overlap through arbitrary deletion.

CFA model fit

Interpret a bundle of indices, not a single pass/fail number:

Index Common Week 10 guideline Meaning
RMSEA <.08 acceptable; lower is better Approximation error per degree of freedom
CFI >.90 acceptable Improvement over an independence/null model
TLI >.90 acceptable Comparative fit with complexity penalty
SRMR lecture emphasizes <.06 (many references use <.08) Average standardized residual discrepancy

FreshCafé: RMSEA=.09, CFI=.88, TLI=.89, SRMR=.07—each indicates some weakness under the case criteria. This does not authorize blind item deletion. Identify theoretically meaningful sources of misfit, revise one defensible element at a time, re-estimate, and disclose changes.

The lecture’s separate demonstration produced SRMR=.044, CFI=.994, and TLI=.988, while RMSEA remained slightly above the desired cutoff. Removing a weaker item made fit worse, so it was restored—an important example of evidence-based iteration rather than automatic pruning.

FreshCafé decision framework

Scenario: FreshCafé Foods wants a reliable and valid customer-experience/satisfaction survey. Results show acceptable but marginal factorability, some weak/cross-loading items, good alpha/CR, Brand Image AVE=.47, HTMT=.87, and weak overall CFA fit.

Recommended sequence:

  1. Recheck data coding, reverse items, missingness, distribution, and wording.
  2. Review the .28 item and cross-loading item against construct definitions.
  3. Remove/rewrite only if statistical weakness and substantive ambiguity align.
  4. Rerun EFA when the structure is genuinely exploratory.
  5. Specify CFA from theory, ideally validate on a separate sample or split sample.
  6. Reassess loadings, alpha/CR, AVE, HTMT, and multiple fit indices.
  7. Compare before/after models and preserve content coverage.
  8. Only after measurement is defensible, interpret structural effects and managerial conclusions.

Practical use case: If an item “The café feels modern and serves high-quality food” loads on Brand Image and Product Quality, it may be double-barreled. Reword into two single-focus items rather than deleting the customer insight entirely.

Frequent mistakes to avoid

  • Treating significant Shapiro–Wilk as a ban on all parametric methods without judging magnitude/robustness.
  • Calling an allowed endpoint on a Likert scale an outlier merely because it is extreme.
  • Using an arbitrary regression only to claim all assumptions are satisfied without matching the final model.
  • Treating Harman’s test as conclusive proof that CMB is absent.
  • Using rotated PCA or a forced single factor incorrectly for Harman’s diagnostic.
  • Assuming good KMO guarantees a good factor solution.
  • Switching rotation to “fix” poor factorability.
  • Treating PCA and EFA as synonyms.
  • Running CFA merely to explore an unknown structure.
  • Removing every loading below .50 regardless of content validity.
  • Equating high alpha with unidimensionality or validity.
  • Reporting AVE without CR/loadings or HTMT without theory.
  • Accepting/rejecting CFA from one fit index.
  • Adding correlated errors or modification-index paths without theoretical justification.
  • Testing structural hypotheses before validating measurement.
