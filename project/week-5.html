1. Overview

This quiz focuses on the measurement stage of research design, where researchers convert abstract concepts into measurable variables.

Many important research concepts cannot be directly observed:

Examples:

  • Employee engagement.
  • Customer satisfaction.
  • Trust.
  • Service quality.
  • Motivation.
  • Perceived usefulness.

These are called latent constructs.

The researcher must design measurement scales that accurately capture these hidden concepts.

The main topics covered:

  • Measurement models.
  • Reflective and formative measurement.
  • Scale development.
  • Construct definition.
  • Indicator selection.
  • Reliability and validity.
  • Objective vs subjective measures.

2. Latent Constructs

Definition

A latent construct is an abstract concept that cannot be directly observed or measured.

Examples:

Construct Possible Indicators
Employee engagement Energy, dedication, enthusiasm
Trust Confidence, reliability, belief
Service quality Reliability, responsiveness, competence
Job satisfaction Satisfaction ratings

Why Measurement Is Needed

Because researchers cannot directly measure:

“How engaged is an employee?”

They measure observable indicators:

  • Survey responses.
  • Behavioural records.
  • Ratings.

The goal is to ensure that indicators accurately represent the underlying construct.

3. Measurement Model vs Structural Model

A major distinction in research modeling.

3.1 Measurement Model

Purpose

Explains:

How observed indicators represent latent constructs.

It focuses on:

  • Survey items.
  • Indicators.
  • Factor loadings.
  • Reliability.
  • Validity.

Example:

Latent construct:

Employee motivation

↓

Survey items:

  • “I feel energetic at work.”
  • “I am enthusiastic about my job.”
  • “I am dedicated to my work.”

The measurement model asks:

  • Do these items measure motivation?
  • Are they reliable?
  • Do they represent the same concept?

3.2 Structural Model

Purpose

Explains:

Relationships between constructs.

Example:

Employee motivation

↓

Job attitude

The structural model tests:

  • Direction of effects.
  • Strength of relationships.
  • Hypotheses.

Difference

Measurement Model Structural Model
Measures constructs Tests relationships
Indicators → Constructs Construct → Construct
Scale quality Hypothesis testing

4. Scale Development Process

Developing a good measurement scale involves multiple stages.

Stage 1: Define the Construct

Before writing questions, researchers must clearly define the concept.

Example:

Employee engagement:

Includes:

  • Energy.
  • Dedication.
  • Involvement.

Does not include:

  • Salary satisfaction.
  • Office facilities.

Importance

Poor definition creates a mixed scale.

Example:

Employee engagement items:

✔ “I feel enthusiastic about my work.”

✔ “I feel committed to my tasks.”

Incorrect additions:

✘ “My salary is satisfactory.”

✘ “My office environment is comfortable.”

These measure different concepts.

Stage 2: Generate Initial Items

Researchers create a pool of possible questions.

Purpose:

  • Capture all dimensions.
  • Avoid missing important aspects.

At this stage, having more items is acceptable.

Stage 3: Pilot Testing

The draft scale is tested with participants.

Researchers examine:

  • Item clarity.
  • Factor loadings.
  • Reliability.

Stage 4: Scale Refinement

Weak items are:

  • Removed.
  • Rewritten.
  • Improved.

Then researchers retest the scale.

Example:

A pilot study shows:

Item 4 has a weak factor loading.

Action:

Remove item 4.

Run confirmatory factor analysis again.

Stage 5: Final Scale Validation

The final scale is used in the main research study.

5. Reflective Measurement Model

Definition

A reflective model assumes:

The latent construct causes the observed indicators.

Direction:

Latent Construct
↓
Indicator 1
Indicator 2
Indicator 3

Example: Employee Motivation

Underlying motivation causes:

  • Feeling energetic.
  • Feeling committed.
  • Showing enthusiasm.

Characteristics

Reflective indicators:

1. Should correlate

If motivation increases:

All motivation items should increase.

Example:

A highly motivated employee likely agrees with:

  • “I enjoy my work.”
  • “I feel energized.”
  • “I am committed.”

2. Are interchangeable

Removing one item usually does not change the meaning of the construct.

Example:

Removing one trust question does not redefine trust.

3. Measure effects of the construct

The construct exists first.

Indicators reflect it.

6. Examples of Reflective Constructs

Common reflective constructs:

  • Satisfaction.
  • Trust.
  • Motivation.
  • Attitude.
  • Perceived usefulness.
  • Employee engagement (when defined as psychological state).

7. Formative Measurement Model

Definition

A formative model assumes:

Indicators create or form the construct.

Direction:

Indicator 1
Indicator 2 → Construct
Indicator 3

Example: Meal Quality

Overall meal quality is created by:

  • Food taste.
  • Service friendliness.
  • Cleanliness.
  • Price fairness.

Characteristics

1. Indicators do not need to correlate

Example:

A restaurant can have:

  • Excellent food.
  • Poor service.

These dimensions may differ.

2. Each indicator contributes unique meaning

Removing an indicator changes the construct.

Example:

Remove cleanliness.

The meaning of “meal quality” changes.

3. Indicators are not interchangeable

Food taste and price fairness represent different aspects.

8. Reflective vs Formative Comparison

Feature Reflective Formative

Indicators Effects Causes/components
Correlation Expected Not required
Removing items Usually acceptable Changes meaning

Expected result:

All trust items increase together.

  • “I trust this provider.”
  • “This provider is reliable.”

  • Price.

Together create quality.

Why formative?

Because each component contributes separately.

10. Objective vs Subjective Measurement

Subjective Measures

Collected directly from people.

Examples:

  • Surveys.
  • Interviews.
  • Ratings.

Example:

“Rate your satisfaction from 1 to 5.”

Objective Measures

Based on existing records.

Examples:

  • Sales data.
  • Repeat purchases.
  • Store visits.
  • Customer retention records.

Example:

Customer satisfaction through:

  • Number of repeat visits.
  • Purchase frequency.

11. Measuring Customer Satisfaction

Customer satisfaction can be measured through:

Subjective Method

Ask customers:

How satisfied are you?

Example:

1–5 satisfaction scale.

Objective Method

Use company records:

  • Repeat purchases.
  • Visit frequency.
  • Complaints.

Both methods measure different aspects.

12. Avoiding Idealized Responses in Surveys

A common measurement problem is social desirability bias.

People may answer according to:

  • What seems acceptable.
  • What they think they should say.
  • Ideal standards.

Example

Poor question:

“How important is excellent equipment when choosing a provider?”

Problem:

Measures importance, not actual perception.

Better:

“This provider uses modern equipment.”

Measures perceived performance.

13. Importance of Specific Referents

When measuring perceptions, questions should focus on the actual object.

Better:

“This bank provides up-to-date technology.”

Not:

“Banks provide up-to-date technology.”

Reason:

Generic wording measures general beliefs, not evaluation of the specific provider.

14. Normative Wording Problems

Words like:

  • Should.
  • Essential.
  • Must.

can trigger ideal responses.

Example:

“A good bank should have advanced equipment.”

This measures standards, not actual perception.

Better:

“My bank has advanced equipment.”

15. Employee Engagement Measurement

Employee engagement is a complex construct.

Possible dimensions:

Psychological Engagement

Reflective indicators:

  • Enthusiasm.
  • Dedication.
  • Energy.

Behavioural Indicators

Examples:

  • Absenteeism.
  • Turnover.
  • Participation.

Researchers must decide:

Is engagement:

  1. A psychological state?
  2. A composite index?

16. Reflective vs Formative Employee Engagement

Reflective View

Engagement is an underlying state.

It causes:

  • Enthusiasm.
  • Dedication.
  • Energy.

Indicators should move together.

Formative View

Engagement is an index created from:

  • Attendance.
  • Turnover.
  • Participation.

Indicators may not correlate.

Each contributes unique information.

17. Common Measurement Mistakes

Mistake 1:

Mixing different constructs in one scale.

Example:

Employee engagement scale:

✔ Motivation.

✘ Salary satisfaction.

✘ Office facilities.

Mistake 2:

Choosing items before defining the construct.

Correct order:

  1. Define construct.
  2. Decide dimensions.
  3. Create items.
  4. Test scale.

Mistake 3:

Assuming all indicators should correlate.

Only reflective models require strong correlation.

Mistake 4:

Using one question for complex concepts.

A single item often cannot capture:

  • Multiple dimensions.
  • Reliability.
  • Construct validity.

Mistake 5:

Confusing measurement and structural models.

Measurement:

“Do these questions measure trust?”

Structural:

“Does trust influence loyalty?”

18. Reliability and Validity

Reliability

Means:

Consistency of measurement.

Example:

A trust scale gives similar results across items.

Common measure:

  • Cronbach’s alpha.

Validity

Means:

Accuracy of measurement.

Question:

Are we measuring the intended concept?

Examples:

A service quality scale should measure service quality, not customer mood.

19. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)

Purpose

Tests whether items represent expected constructs.

Example:

Does this group of questions really measure:

  • Trust?
  • Satisfaction?
  • Motivation?

Used during:

  • Scale validation.
  • Refinement.

20. Final Revision Summary

Measurement Model

Explains:

How indicators measure constructs.

Structural Model

Explains:

How constructs influence each other.

Reflective Model

Construct causes indicators.

Example:

Trust → Trust items.

Characteristics:

  • Indicators correlate.
  • Items interchangeable.

Formative Model

Indicators create construct.

Example:

Meal quality \= taste + service + cleanliness.

Characteristics:

  • Indicators need not correlate.
  • Each indicator adds unique meaning.

Scale Development

Sequence:

  1. Define construct.
  2. Generate items.
  3. Pilot test.
  4. Check reliability and validity.
  5. Refine items.
  6. Finalize scale.

Core Principle

Good measurement begins with a clear understanding of what the construct represents.

Before collecting data, researchers must decide:

  • What is being measured?
  • Are indicators effects or causes?
  • Are items measuring one concept or multiple concepts?

A strong measurement model ensures that later statistical analysis and hypothesis testing are based on accurate and meaningful data.

21. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1:

Create items and immediately launch the survey.

Reality:

Scale development requires testing.

Misconception 2:

More items always mean better measurement.

Reality:

Weak items reduce quality.

Misconception 3:

Low item correlation means remove everything.

Reality:

First investigate:

  • Wording.
  • Concept alignment.
  • Interpretation.

Misconception 4:

Content validity and reliability are the same.

Reality:

Content validity:

Are we measuring the right thing?

Reliability:

Are we measuring consistently?

Misconception 5:

Pilot results are enough for final conclusions.

Reality:

Pilot testing identifies problems; larger studies validate the scale.

2. Latent Constructs and Measurement

Latent Construct

A latent construct is an abstract concept that cannot be measured directly.

Examples:

  • Service responsiveness.
  • Customer satisfaction.
  • Trust.
  • Motivation.
  • Perceived quality.

Researchers measure these concepts using observable indicators.

3. Construct Conceptualisation

Definition

Construct conceptualisation is the first stage of scale development.

It involves defining:

  • What the construct means.
  • What dimensions it includes.
  • What it does not include.

NovaBank Example

  2. Speed.
  3. Accessibility.

Importance

A clear definition ensures that:

  • Different researchers interpret the construct consistently.
  • Later validity tests have a clear foundation.

Creating survey items before defining the construct.

Incorrect approach:

Create questions first → decide what they measure later.

Correct approach:

Define construct → create items.

Are measurements consistent?

A scale can be:

24. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1:

Existing scales should always be copied exactly.

Reality:

Existing scales provide guidance but often require adaptation.

Misconception 2:

More survey items always improve measurement.

Reality:

Poor items reduce reliability.

Misconception 3:

Low inter-item correlation always means delete immediately.

Reality:

First investigate:

  • Wording.
  • Interpretation.
  • Construct alignment.

Misconception 4:

Pilot testing is only about collecting preliminary data.

Reality:

Pilot testing identifies problems before expensive full-scale research.

Misconception 5:

Reliability proves validity.

Reality:

A scale can be consistent but measure the wrong thing.

Customer loyalty repurchase-intention and positive-word-of-mouth items actual repurchase/renewal rate Useful for detecting intention–behaviour gaps.
Service quality courteous/responsive-service ratings response time and complaint-resolution rate Perception matters to customers, while metrics diagnose process performance.
Innovation capability manager rating that the firm encourages creativity number of new products launched The metric may miss novelty/quality, while ratings may contain optimism bias.
Investor confidence stated confidence in portfolio returns portfolio turnover/holding behaviour Behaviour is objective but may reflect constraints besides confidence.
Training effectiveness trainee/manager ratings productivity change and post-training error rate A before/after operational metric strengthens the evaluation.
Supply-chain agility managers’ ratings of responsiveness/flexibility fulfilment speed and lead-time variability Useful when the construct has strategic as well as operational aspects.

Exam caution: Metric-based measurement is generally more objective and less affected by response bias, emotion and social desirability, but it can omit context and psychological meaning. Perception-based measurement captures the stakeholder’s internal experience but needs an adequate number of respondents and careful control of bias. “Objective” does not automatically mean “valid”: repeat visits may reflect lack of alternatives rather than satisfaction.

2. Computing a simple construct score

A respondent rates three job-satisfaction items on a five-point scale:

  • satisfaction with pay = 4;
  • satisfaction with supervisor = 5;
  • satisfaction with work–life balance = 3.

The unweighted mean is (4 + 5 + 3) / 3 = 4. This places the respondent’s score at 4 on the original 1–5 scale. An average is often easier to interpret than a sum because it preserves the response scale. A sum is also possible, and differential weights may be assigned when theory justifies unequal importance. Before aggregation, reliability, validity and dimensionality must be checked; arithmetic alone does not prove that the three items form one sound scale.

3. Measurement model versus structural model

Suppose a study proposes:

Employee engagement → organizational performance, moderated by quality of HR.

  • The measurement model asks whether engagement items E1–E3 measure engagement, HR1–HR3 measure HR quality, and OP1–OP3 measure performance.
  • The structural model estimates whether engagement predicts performance and whether HR quality changes the strength/direction of that path.

The measurement model must be acceptable before interpreting structural paths. Otherwise a weak or incorrect measure can make an apparent relationship meaningless.

4. Reflective versus formative measurement

Reflective use case: hotel satisfaction

Satisfaction exists first and produces manifestations such as:

  • “I appreciate this hotel.”
  • “I look forward to staying here again.”
  • “I recommend this hotel to others.”

The arrows point construct → indicators. Items should normally correlate and are partly interchangeable because each is an effect/reflection of the same underlying feeling. Removing one may not change the construct’s conceptual domain substantially, although item deletion still needs evidence.

Formative use case: restaurant experience/quality

The overall evaluation is formed by distinct components:

  • food taste/quality;
  • staff service;
  • ambience;
  • price/value.

The arrows point indicators → construct. Components need not correlate: good food does not guarantee fair price or good ambience. They are not interchangeable, and removing one deletes a unique part of the meaning.

Diagnostic test

Ask:

  1. Does the construct cause the indicators, or do the indicators create the construct?
  2. If the construct changed, should all indicators change together?
  3. Are indicators expected to correlate?
  4. Can one indicator be removed without narrowing the definition?

“Yes” to questions 2–4 generally suggests reflective measurement; unique non-interchangeable ingredients suggest formative measurement. A hybrid/mixed model contains both types.

5. Higher-order construct use case: service quality

SERVQUAL conceptualizes perceived service quality as a higher-order construct composed of five first-order dimensions and 22 items:

  • Tangibles (4): modern/up-to-date equipment, visually appealing facilities, neat employee appearance, visually appealing service materials.
  • Reliability (5): promises kept on time, sincere problem solving, service right the first time, service at the promised time, error-free records.
  • Responsiveness (4): customers told when service will occur, prompt service, willingness to help, not too busy to respond.
  • Assurance (4): employee confidence-building, transaction safety, courtesy, knowledge to answer questions.
  • Empathy (5): individual attention, convenient operating hours, personal attention, customer interests at heart, understanding specific needs.

Each dimension is measured by items; the dimensions together represent service quality. Tangibles alone can be treated as a first-order/unidimensional construct. If service quality predicts an even more abstract concept, a third order can be modeled.

6. Turning an indicator into a good survey statement

Indicator: up-to-date equipment.

  • General performance statement: “The company has up-to-date equipment.”
  • Context-specific statement: “Bank X has up-to-date equipment.”
  • Importance statement: “Up-to-date equipment is essential for quality service delivery.”

The first two measure perceived performance; the third measures normative importance and may invite an idealized/socially desirable answer. If the research concerns Bank X, the specific referent focuses the respondent on actual experience. Response anchors must match the question: strongly disagree–strongly agree for an agreement statement, or least important–most important for an importance question.

7. FinTech adoption questionnaire for MSMEs

A practical questionnaire can contain at least three sections:

  1. Respondent/enterprise profile: enterprise type, industry, years of operation, employees and turnover. These reveal population heterogeneity and may act as group/control variables.
  2. Exploratory/current-behaviour mapping: services used, decision influencers, bases of trust, barriers/concerns and supplier-payment methods. For an exploratory project, this section alone may answer the problem.
  3. Construct scales: Technology Acceptance Model variables such as perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, adoption and perceived risk. Usefulness/ease predict adoption; risk may moderate those effects.

Example usefulness item: “FinTech services improve the efficiency of my enterprise.” Eligible respondents include owners or financial managers able to judge use. With four constructs and five items each, a respondent’s construct total can be scored, but statistical checks are required before claiming perfect measurement or population generalizability.

8. NovaBank scale-development decision

NovaBank wants a service-responsiveness scale. A defensible workflow is:

  1. define responsiveness precisely;
  2. review established scales such as SERVQUAL;
  3. generate a broad item pool from theory, interviews/focus groups and practice;
  4. ask experts to assess relevance, clarity and domain coverage (content validity/CVR/CVI);
  5. translate items into clear statements and matching response levels;
  6. cognitively interview target respondents to detect interpretation problems;
  7. pilot the instrument;
  8. inspect item behaviour, inter-item correlations, reliability and factor structure;
  9. revise weak/ambiguous items and retest;
  10. administer the refined scale to the full sample and validate it.

If operations wants immediate launch but the pilot reveals weak items, the recommended decision is to revise and retest. A fast, unreliable instrument contaminates every later analysis. A seven-point scale can offer greater response variation and diagnostic sensitivity than a five-point scale, but more categories do not repair unclear items.

B. High-yield distinctions

Distinction Exam-ready explanation
Latent vs non-latent/manifest variable Latent constructs cannot be directly observed and are inferred through indicators; manifest variables can be directly measured/observed.
Variable vs construct A variable merely varies; calling it a construct adds conceptual meaning, indicators and assumptions about how indicators relate to it.
Conceptual vs contextual vs operational definition General theoretical meaning; meaning bounded by the present situation/scope; specification of how it will be measured.
Indicator/item vs survey statement Indicator is the aspect used to represent a construct; the statement/question is the wording used to elicit its score.
Scale (instrument) vs response scale A set of items measuring a construct versus the response levels attached to an item (e.g., 1–5).
Measurement model vs structural model Construct–indicator relationships versus hypothesized relationships among constructs.
Reflective vs formative Construct causes correlated/interchangeable manifestations versus distinct indicators jointly causing/forming the construct.
Unidimensional vs multidimensional One underlying dimension versus multiple first-order dimensions forming/reflecting a higher-order construct.
Perception-based vs metric-based Subjective judgments gathered through surveys/interviews versus objective records/sensors/analytics metrics.
Likert vs semantic differential Agreement with statements using ordered anchors versus rating between bipolar adjectives (e.g., slow–fast).
Content validity vs construct validity Expert/domain judgment that items cover the concept versus empirical evidence that the scale behaves as the intended construct.
Reliability vs validity Consistency/repeatability versus measuring the intended phenomenon accurately. A measure can be reliable but invalid.
Cognitive interview vs pilot test Probes how respondents understand wording versus trial administration used to evaluate the instrument and preliminary statistics.
EFA vs CFA EFA discovers an underlying factor structure; CFA tests whether a prespecified measurement structure fits the data.
Inter-item correlation vs item loading Association between two items versus strength with which an item represents a latent factor.

C. Likely exam questions with answer points

1. Why is measurement fundamental in business research?

Explain that measurement quantifies the extent of variables, diagnoses whether improvement is required, produces analyzable data, makes relationships testable, supports comparison/generalization and ultimately links theory to a decision on the original business problem. Poor measurement invalidates later statistical precision.

2. Define latent and non-latent variables with examples from management domains.

Give definitions and examples: engagement/loyalty/risk perception/supply-chain agility/dynamic capability as latent; age, salary, sales, stock price, delivery time, login time and production volume as observed. State that a latent construct is inferred from multiple observed indicators.

3. Compare perception- and metric-based measurement.

Cover nature, sources, methods, bias/objectivity, contextual/psychological insight, examples and selection criteria. Conclude that neither is universally superior and triangulation may be valuable.

4. Explain measurement and structural models using a diagram/example.

Define both; identify the construct–item blocks and arrows among constructs; use engagement, performance and HR quality; state that measurement quality precedes structural interpretation.

5. Distinguish reflective and formative models.

Discuss causal direction, correlation, interchangeability, consequence of item deletion and examples. Mention outward arrows for reflective and inward arrows for formative. Do not classify solely from item wording; theory determines the model.

6. Explain first-order and higher-order constructs using SERVQUAL.

Define dimensionality, name all five SERVQUAL dimensions, state 22 items and explain how first-order dimensions represent higher-order service quality.

7. Describe the full scale-development process.

Expected flow: construct conceptualization → existing-scale/literature review → broad item generation (often 30–50 initially) → expert content validation → cognitive pretest (roughly 10–30 in the lecture’s rule of thumb) → statement/response-scale design → pilot/full administration → item analysis/EFA → reliability and validity → remove/reword weak items → retest/finalize. Mention the lecture’s rough full-administration range of 150–300 only as a thumb rule, not a universal sample-size formula.

8. What are reliability and validity? Can a reliable measure be invalid?

Reliability is consistent performance across items/uses; validity is accurate measurement of the intended construct. Yes: a miscalibrated scale can repeatedly give the same wrong weight. Discuss Cronbach’s alpha as internal-consistency evidence, not proof of validity or unidimensionality.

9. Why are expert review, cognitive interviews and pilot testing all needed?

They answer different questions: experts assess relevance/domain coverage, target respondents reveal interpretation/wording problems, and pilot statistics reveal item/factor/reliability behaviour. One cannot substitute fully for the others.

10. Design a questionnaire for FinTech adoption among MSMEs.

Include profile, behavioural/exploratory and construct sections; identify eligible respondents; state TAM relationships, risk moderation, controls/group comparisons, contextual statements, matching anchors, pilot/validation and ethical/access considerations.

11. Discuss common measurement mistakes.

  • treating an observable proxy as identical to the latent construct;
  • selecting reflective/formative form without theoretical logic;
  • deleting formative indicators simply because correlations are low;
  • accepting high Cronbach’s alpha as proof of validity;
  • using normative, vague or referent-free wording;
  • collecting data before defining constructs and intended analysis;
  • moving from a weak pilot directly to full administration;
  • assuming a longer or seven-point scale automatically improves quality;
  • ignoring population/context differences when adapting an established scale.

D. Rapid revision logic

Memorize the chain:

Business problem → theory and variables → conceptual/contextual/operational definitions → latent/observed classification → measurement approach → indicator direction (reflective/formative) → dimensionality → item pool → content validation → cognitive pretest → pilot → EFA/item/reliability analysis → refinement → final validation/CFA → structural testing → managerial insight.

Two non-negotiable principles:

  1. A sophisticated structural model cannot compensate for a poor measurement model.
  2. The conceptual definition determines what must be measured; convenient available data should not silently redefine the construct.
