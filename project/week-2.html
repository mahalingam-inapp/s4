1. Overview of the Research Planning Process

Effective business research requires careful planning before collecting data. The planning stage creates the foundation for the entire research process by ensuring that:

  • The research problem is clearly defined.
  • The required information is identified.
  • Appropriate variables are selected.
  • Data collection methods match the research objectives.
  • Analysis methods are considered before data collection begins.

A well-designed research process follows the sequence:

Research problem → Objectives → Research questions/hypotheses → Theoretical framework → Measurement design → Data collection → Data analysis → Interpretation

2. Defining a Research Problem

Meaning of a Research Problem

A research problem identifies the specific issue that requires investigation and defines what information is needed for decision-making.

A good research problem should specify:

  1. Variables being studied
  2. Target population
  3. Context of the study
  4. Relationship being investigated

Example: Employee Satisfaction Study

A weak research problem:

Study employee satisfaction.

This is too broad.

A stronger research problem:

Examine whether perceived appraisal fairness influences intention to stay among employees of a specific organization.

This identifies:

  • Independent variable:
  • Perceived appraisal fairness
  • Dependent variable:
  • Intention to stay
  • Population:
  • Employees
  • Context:
  • Organization

3. Moving From Business Issue to Research Problem

Organizations often start with a broad issue.

Example:

Product quality is declining.

This is not yet a research problem.

The researcher must investigate:

  • What changed?
  • What variables may explain the decline?
  • Who is affected?
  • What evidence is required?

Root Cause Analysis

Tools such as:

  • Fishbone diagrams
  • Five Whys analysis

help identify possible causes.

Example:

Problem:

Customer satisfaction is declining.

Investigation:

Why?

→ Customers complain about product quality.

Why?

→ Product quality decreased.

Why?

→ Raw material standards changed.

Research problem:

Examine whether changes in raw material standards are related to product quality and customer satisfaction among current customers.

Important Principle

Research identifies relationships and explanations.

It does not assume responsibility or assign blame.

Incorrect:

Prove that the supplier caused dissatisfaction.

Correct:

Examine whether raw material changes are associated with quality and satisfaction outcomes.

4. Research Questions and Hypotheses

Research Questions

Research questions define what the study wants to understand.

Examples:

  • Does employee motivation influence performance?
  • Does telehealth follow-up reduce readmissions?
  • Does perceived fairness influence retention?

Hypotheses

A hypothesis is a testable statement predicting relationships between variables.

Example:

Perceived appraisal fairness positively influences employees’ intention to stay.

A hypothesis identifies:

  • Predictor variable.
  • Outcome variable.
  • Expected relationship.

5. Theoretical Framework

Meaning

A theoretical framework provides the foundation for explaining relationships between variables.

It uses:

  • Existing theories.
  • Previous research evidence.
  • Established models.

A theoretical framework answers:

Why should one variable influence another?

Example: Technology Adoption Study

A researcher studying digital technology adoption may use an existing adoption model to explain:

  • Perceived usefulness.
  • Ease of use.
  • Adoption intention.

The theory helps organize:

  • Variables.
  • Relationships.
  • Hypotheses.

6. Basis for Cause-and-Effect Relationships

A proposed relationship between variables should be based on:

Theory and Research Evidence

Strong relationships come from:

  • Established theories.
  • Previous empirical studies.
  • Existing research findings.

Weak Foundations

Practitioner Experience Alone

Managers may provide useful insights but are not sufficient evidence.

Single Correlation Study

A single observed relationship does not establish strong theoretical support.

Correlation does not automatically prove causation.

Single Theory Alone

Using theory without supporting evidence may overlook:

  • Context differences.
  • Contradictory findings.
  • Practical limitations.

7. Literature Review Functions

A literature review is not simply collecting articles. It serves multiple purposes.

7.1 Building a Theoretical Framework

Purpose:

To identify and apply existing theories.

Example:

Using a technology adoption model to explain digital adoption behavior.

7.2 Identifying Research Gaps

Purpose:

To find what previous studies have not adequately answered.

Examples:

  • Missing populations.
  • Missing contexts.
  • Contradictory findings.
  • Limited variables.

7.3 Methodological Review

Purpose:

To examine how previous researchers conducted studies.

It compares:

  • Research designs.
  • Data collection methods.
  • Analysis techniques.

7.4 Annotated Bibliography

Purpose:

Summarizes individual sources.

Usually includes:

  • Source summary.
  • Evaluation.
  • Relevance.

It does not build a complete theoretical explanation.

8. Measurement of Unobservable Concepts

Many business concepts cannot be directly observed.

Examples:

  • Job satisfaction.
  • Employee motivation.
  • Trust.
  • Perceived fairness.
  • Brand loyalty.

These are called:

Latent Variables

A latent variable is an underlying concept measured indirectly through observable indicators.

Example: Employee Satisfaction

Cannot directly observe:

Satisfaction

Instead measure through survey statements:

  • “I am satisfied with my workplace.”
  • “I feel valued by my organization.”
  • “I enjoy my work.”

Responses are measured using:

  • Likert scales.
  • Multiple questionnaire items.

9. Questionnaire Design

A questionnaire should be designed based on:

  • Research objectives.
  • Variables.
  • Analysis requirements.

Importance of Planning Before Data Collection

Researchers must decide:

  • What variables will be measured.
  • How they will be measured.
  • What response format will be used.
  • How data will be analyzed.

Why Analysis Planning Comes Early

The intended analysis influences:

  • Question format.
  • Scale selection.
  • Coding approach.
  • Sample requirements.

Example

If factor analysis is planned:

Questionnaire design should consider:

  • Multiple items measuring each construct.
  • Appropriate response scales.
  • Sufficient variation among responses.

10. Consequences of Poor Analysis Planning

If analysis is chosen after data collection:

Problems may include:

  • Variables measured incorrectly.
  • Missing required information.
  • Incompatible data formats.
  • Need for recoding.
  • Weak hypothesis testing.

Correct Sequence

Before collecting data:

  1. Define research objectives.
  2. Identify variables.
  3. Select analysis approach.
  4. Design measurement instruments.
  5. Collect data.

11. Quantitative Research Design

Purpose

Quantitative research measures relationships using numerical data.

Common methods:

  • Surveys.
  • Experiments.
  • Existing datasets.

Structured Questionnaire Surveys

Best suited for:

  • Measuring attitudes.
  • Testing hypotheses.
  • Comparing groups.

Advantages:

  • Standardized data.
  • Large samples.
  • Statistical analysis.

12. Choosing Appropriate Data Sources

Primary Data

Collected specifically for the current study.

Examples:

  • Surveys.
  • Interviews.
  • Experiments.

Useful when:

  • Existing data does not measure required variables.

Secondary Data

Previously collected information.

Examples:

  • Government reports.
  • Company records.
  • Industry databases.

Useful for:

  • Background understanding.
  • Trend analysis.

Key Principle

The data source must match the research objective.

Using existing data that does not measure the required concept creates a mismatch.

13. Structural Equation Modeling (SEM)

Purpose

SEM is used when researchers want to analyze:

  1. Measurement relationships.
  2. Relationships among multiple variables.

It is especially useful for:

  • Latent variables.
  • Complex theoretical models.

Example

Research model:

Perceived fairness → Trust → Intention to stay

Variables:

  • Fairness:
  • Measured by multiple survey items.
  • Trust:
  • Measured by multiple survey items.
  • Intention to stay:
  • Measured by multiple survey items.

SEM evaluates:

  • Whether measurement items represent the concepts correctly.
  • Whether relationships between concepts are supported.

14. SEM vs Multiple Regression

Multiple Regression

Purpose:

Tests relationships between variables.

Example:

Does satisfaction predict intention to stay?

Works well when:

  • Variables are already measured as scores.
  • The model is relatively simple.

Structural Equation Modeling

Purpose:

Tests complex networks involving latent variables.

Advantages:

  • Handles multiple relationships simultaneously.
  • Tests measurement models.
  • Works with constructs measured by multiple indicators.

Comparison

Feature Regression SEM
Main purpose Predict outcomes Test theoretical models
Variables Usually observed scores Latent constructs
Multiple relationships Limited Strong capability
Measurement model Not included Included

15. Factor Analysis

Purpose

Factor analysis identifies underlying dimensions within a set of questionnaire items.

Example:

A satisfaction questionnaire has 20 questions.

Factor analysis may discover:

Factor 1:

  • Work environment.

Factor 2:

  • Management support.

Factor 3:

  • Compensation satisfaction.

Planning Requirements

Before data collection:

Researchers should decide:

  • Factor analysis will be used.
  • Items should measure related concepts.
  • Response scales should be appropriate.

16. Descriptive vs Inferential Analysis

Descriptive Statistics

Describe what the data shows.

Examples:

  • Mean.
  • Percentage.
  • Frequency.

Question answered:

What is happening?

Inferential Statistics

Test relationships and hypotheses.

Examples:

  • Regression.
  • SEM.
  • Factor analysis.

Question answered:

What relationships exist?

17. Common Research Planning Mistakes

Mistake 1:

Writing survey questions before defining the problem.

Problem:

The survey may collect irrelevant information.

Correct approach:

Problem → Information needs → Questions.

Mistake 2:

Choosing analysis after collecting data.

Problem:

The collected data may not support the intended analysis.

Correct approach:

Plan analysis before data collection.

Mistake 3:

Using only manager opinions.

Problem:

Opinions are not equivalent to evidence.

Correct approach:

Combine experience with systematic data.

Mistake 4:

Using secondary data that does not match the research question.

Problem:

Data availability does not guarantee suitability.

Mistake 5:

Assuming correlation proves causation.

Problem:

Relationships require theoretical justification and appropriate research design.

Used to explain:

  • Why variables should relate.

  • Decide variables.

Data Collection Choices

Final Principle

Strong business research does not begin with collecting data. It begins with a clear research problem, a theoretical foundation, appropriate measurement, and a planned analysis strategy.

The quality of the final decision depends on how well the research design connects:

Research problem → Theory → Measurement → Data → Analysis → Evidence-based conclusion.

Case Study

DeltaTech, a mid-sized software firm, has noticed two alarming symptoms: “Declining sales or market share – detected through sales reports and CRM data.” and “Falling employee engagement or productivity – measured via HR surveys, absenteeism rates, and performance reviews.” CTO Priya and HR Director Marcus convene a meeting to define the core research problem. They recognize that a gap exists between current performance and strategic goals, triggering the Planning Stage of their study.Priya leads the team in constructing a Theoretical Framework that links leadership communication, organizational culture, and customer satisfaction. They draft two Hypotheses: H1 asserts that low engagement among customer-facing staff drives reduced customer retention; H2 proposes that unclear product roadmaps harm market share. Marketing Manager Elena questions whether external market trends might confound internal factors, illustrating the need to clearly specify variables and avoid scope creep. Each stakeholder agrees that rigorous definition of constructs—such as “employee engagement” and “customer loyalty”—is critical before moving forward.At the Decision Point, the team evaluates Research Design options. Option A is a cross-sectional survey targeting 200 employees and 500 customers; Option B is a mixed-method design combining focus-group interviews with a smaller quantitative sample. CFO Javier warns of budget constraints limiting sample size, while Operations Lead Claire stresses timely insights. If they choose the survey, they gain statistical power but risk missing nuanced feedback; if they opt for mixed-method, they achieve depth but face smaller sample generalizability. Students must weigh stakeholder priorities, resource limits, and validity trade-offs to recommend the most suitable design.

1. Case Overview

DeltaTech is a mid-sized software company experiencing two major warning signs:

  1. Declining sales or market share
    * Detected through:
    • Sales reports
    • CRM data
    • Market performance indicators
  2. Falling employee engagement or productivity
    * Measured through:
    • HR surveys
    • Absenteeism rates
    • Performance reviews

The CTO, Priya, and HR Director, Marcus, recognize that these symptoms indicate a gap between:

Current organizational performance → Desired strategic goals

This gap triggers the need for a structured business research process.

The case focuses on:

  • Defining a research problem.
  • Developing a theoretical framework.
  • Creating hypotheses.
  • Defining constructs.
  • Selecting an appropriate research design.
  • Balancing validity, resources, and stakeholder needs.

2. From Symptoms to Research Problem

Symptoms vs Research Problem

A symptom is an observable indication that something may be wrong.

Examples:

  • Sales are declining.
  • Employee engagement is falling.
  • Customer retention is decreasing.

However, symptoms do not explain the underlying causes.

Example

Observed symptom:

Customer retention is decreasing.

Possible causes:

  • Poor customer service.
  • Product limitations.
  • Weak employee engagement.
  • Unclear product direction.
  • Competitive pressure.

The research problem should investigate the relationships between possible causes and outcomes.

3. Defining the Research Problem

A strong research problem identifies:

  1. The issue.
  2. The variables involved.
  3. The target population.
  4. The context.
  5. The relationships to be studied.

DeltaTech Research Problem Example

A suitable research problem:

Determine how internal organizational factors, such as employee engagement and leadership communication, influence customer satisfaction, retention, and market performance.

This connects:

Internal factors:

  • Leadership communication.
  • Organizational culture.
  • Employee engagement.

With external outcomes:

  • Customer satisfaction.
  • Customer loyalty.
  • Market share.

4. Planning Stage of Business Research

The planning stage begins after identifying a performance gap.

It establishes the foundation for the study.

Key Activities in Planning

1. Define Research Objectives

Objectives clarify what the research should discover.

Examples:

  • Identify factors influencing employee engagement.
  • Determine whether employee engagement affects customer retention.
  • Understand whether product roadmap clarity influences market performance.

2. Identify Variables

Variables represent measurable concepts involved in the study.

Examples:

Independent variables:

  • Leadership communication.
  • Organizational culture.
  • Product roadmap clarity.

Dependent variables:

  • Customer satisfaction.
  • Customer loyalty.
  • Market share.
  • Employee productivity.

3. Develop Research Questions

Examples:

  • Does employee engagement influence customer retention?
  • Does leadership communication affect employee commitment?
  • Does product roadmap clarity influence market performance?

5. Theoretical Framework

Meaning

A theoretical framework provides the logical foundation explaining why relationships between variables should exist.

It connects:

Existing theories + Research evidence → Proposed relationships

DeltaTech’s Framework

The team proposes relationships among:

Leadership Communication

↓

Influences

Employee Engagement

↓

Influences

Customer Satisfaction and Loyalty

↓

Influences

Market Performance

Why Theoretical Frameworks Matter

A theoretical framework helps:

  • Explain cause-and-effect relationships.
  • Guide hypothesis development.
  • Identify relevant variables.
  • Prevent random data collection.

Without a theoretical framework, research becomes a collection of unrelated measurements.

6. Developing Hypotheses

Meaning of a Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about the relationship between variables.

A good hypothesis:

  • Identifies variables.
  • Predicts a relationship.
  • Can be tested using data.

Hypothesis 1 (H1)

Low engagement among customer-facing staff drives reduced customer retention.

Variables:

Independent variable:

  • Employee engagement.

Dependent variable:

  • Customer retention.

Expected relationship:

Higher engagement → Better customer retention.

Logic Behind H1

Customer-facing employees influence:

  • Customer interactions.
  • Service quality.
  • Problem resolution.
  • Customer experience.

Low engagement may lead to:

  • Poor service.
  • Lower satisfaction.
  • Customer churn.

Hypothesis 2 (H2)

Unclear product roadmaps harm market share.

Variables:

Independent variable:

  • Product roadmap clarity.

Dependent variable:

  • Market share.

Expected relationship:

Clear roadmap → Better market alignment.

Logic Behind H2

A weak product roadmap may cause:

  • Slow innovation.
  • Poor customer confidence.
  • Misaligned products.
  • Competitive disadvantage.

7. Importance of Construct Definition

What Are Constructs?

Constructs are abstract concepts that cannot be directly observed.

Examples:

  • Employee engagement.
  • Customer loyalty.
  • Organizational culture.
  • Leadership effectiveness.

Because they cannot be directly measured, researchers must define measurable indicators.

8. Defining Key Constructs

Employee Engagement

A possible definition:

The level of emotional commitment, involvement, and motivation employees have toward their work and organization.

Possible measures:

  • Job enthusiasm.
  • Commitment.
  • Willingness to contribute.
  • Satisfaction levels.

Customer Loyalty

A possible definition:

The likelihood that customers continue purchasing, recommend the company, and maintain a long-term relationship.

Possible measures:

  • Repeat purchases.
  • Renewal intention.
  • Recommendation likelihood.
  • Switching intention.

Leadership Communication

Possible measures:

  • Clarity of communication.
  • Frequency of updates.
  • Transparency.
  • Employee understanding of goals.

Organizational Culture

Possible measures:

  • Collaboration.
  • Innovation support.
  • Trust.
  • Shared values.

9. Avoiding Scope Creep

Elena’s Concern

Marketing Manager Elena raises an important issue:

Could external market trends influence outcomes?

This introduces possible external variables.

Examples:

  • Economic conditions.
  • Competitor actions.
  • Industry changes.
  • Customer preferences.

Confounding Variables

A confounding variable is a factor that affects both the cause and outcome, making relationships harder to interpret.

Example:

Research finding:

Employee engagement decreases, and sales decline.

Possible explanation:

Employee engagement caused sales decline.

But another possibility:

A market recession caused both lower engagement and lower sales.

Importance of Scope Definition

Researchers must decide:

  • Which variables are included.
  • Which factors are controlled.
  • Which external influences are outside the study.

10. Research Design Decision

DeltaTech considers two options:

Option A: Cross-Sectional Survey

Participants:

  • 200 employees.
  • 500 customers.

Option B: Mixed-Method Research

Combination:

  • Focus-group interviews.
  • Smaller quantitative sample.

The decision requires balancing:

  • Statistical strength.
  • Depth of understanding.
  • Cost.
  • Time.
  • Validity.

11. Cross-Sectional Survey Design

Meaning

A cross-sectional survey collects data from participants at one point in time.

Advantages

1. Statistical Power

A larger sample provides:

  • More reliable estimates.
  • Better ability to detect relationships.
  • Stronger statistical analysis.

2. Generalizability

A larger sample allows findings to better represent:

  • Employees.
  • Customers.
  • Target populations.

3. Efficiency

Surveys are:

  • Faster.
  • Easier to distribute.
  • Easier to analyze.

Limitations

Lack of Depth

Surveys may identify:

Employee engagement is low.

But may not explain:

Why employees feel disengaged.

Limited Context

May miss:

  • Personal experiences.
  • Organizational politics.
  • Cultural issues.

12. Mixed-Method Research Design

Meaning

Mixed-method research combines:

  • Quantitative measurement.
  • Qualitative exploration.

Qualitative Component

Focus groups explore:

  • Employee experiences.
  • Customer perceptions.
  • Cultural issues.
  • Hidden problems.

Quantitative Component

A smaller survey measures:

  • Strength of relationships.
  • Prevalence of opinions.
  • Statistical patterns.

Advantages

1. Greater Depth

Focus groups reveal:

  • Why problems exist.
  • How stakeholders experience issues.

2. Better Construct Understanding

Useful for abstract concepts:

  • Engagement.
  • Loyalty.
  • Culture.

Limitations

Smaller Sample Size

Problems:

  • Lower statistical power.
  • Reduced generalizability.

Higher Resource Requirements

Requires:

  • More time.
  • Skilled moderators.
  • More complex analysis.

13. Survey vs Mixed Methods Comparison

Factor Cross-sectional Survey Mixed Methods
Main approach Quantitative Quantitative + Qualitative
Sample size Larger Smaller
Statistical power Higher Moderate
Depth Lower Higher
Cost Lower Higher
Time Faster Slower
Context understanding Limited Strong
Best for Measuring relationships Understanding causes

14. Validity Trade-Offs

Research design involves balancing different types of validity.

Statistical Conclusion Validity

Concern:

Are relationships detected accurately?

Improved by:

  • Larger samples.
  • Appropriate statistical methods.

Surveys perform well here.

Construct Validity

Concern:

Are concepts measured correctly?

Improved by:

  • Multiple measurement items.
  • Qualitative understanding.
  • Strong theoretical definitions.

Mixed methods often performs better.

External Validity

Concern:

Can findings apply broadly?

Improved by:

  • Larger samples.
  • Representative participants.

Internal Validity

Concern:

Are observed relationships truly caused by the proposed factors?

Improved by:

  • Controlling confounding variables.
  • Strong research design.

Exam Study Guide Appendix

Week 2 — The Business Research Process: Planning, Frameworks, Measurement, Design, and Proposal Preparation

How to use these notes

Week 2 explains how a visible business symptom becomes a researchable problem and then a complete research plan. For exam answers, do not merely list steps: show the logic linking symptom → causes → research problem → concepts/variables → hypotheses/framework → measurement → population/sample → research design → data → analysis → decision.

1. The complete research process

The course presents four broad stages:

  1. Planning: decide why, what, where/context, who, how, and when the research will be conducted.
  2. Execution: collect the required data according to the plan.
  3. Analysis and reporting: handle the data, perform suitable analysis, extract relevant information/insights, and report it.
  4. Decision-making: use the insights to solve or reduce the original business problem.

Planning must be sequential and systematic. Its major elements are:

  • business problem and research problem;
  • research context;
  • background work/literature review and earlier solution attempts;
  • research gap—whether existing knowledge is adequate and applicable, or the issue remains unaddressed;
  • objectives, questions, and scope;
  • concepts and variables relevant to the problem;
  • stakeholders whose views/data are critical;
  • assumptions/hypotheses about relationships and group differences;
  • theoretical framework;
  • measurement strategy;
  • population, sampling, time frame, intervention, study setting, and collection mode;
  • expected data quality and proposed analysis.

Research is ultimately an attempt to identify or understand an effect useful for solving a business problem. Planning must therefore anticipate analysis before data collection; otherwise the collected data may not answer the question.

2. From business symptom to research problem

2.1 Symptoms and their sources

A research project normally begins by noticing a symptom, not by receiving a perfectly formulated research problem. Symptoms are deviations from expected performance, usually detected through key performance indicators/key success factors, such as:

  • sales or growth below target;
  • high employee turnover or low engagement/productivity;
  • poor advertising effectiveness;
  • deteriorating financial performance;
  • weak channel-partner engagement;
  • changes in consumer behaviour;
  • product-quality loss.

Potential sources include financial statements, sales reports, HR/engagement and performance reports, marketing metrics, channel reports, consumer trends, organizational databases, and industry reports. Companies already collect substantial internal secondary data; comparing these metrics with targets can expose a problem. Industry developments also reveal present or future problems—for example AI integration difficulties, changing environmental norms, digitalization, policy changes, technology disruption, or consumer-mindset shifts. Consulting reports can sensitize a researcher to likely future issues.

Distinction: a symptom is observable evidence (for example, declining sales); the research problem identifies the specific knowledge deficit or relationship that must be investigated to address it.

2.2 Cause–effect mapping

To transform a broad business problem into a research problem, map causes and effects:

  • Effect: the undesirable outcome the organization wants to change.
  • Causes: possible reasons producing that outcome.

For declining sales, possible causes include poor customer satisfaction, product-quality challenges, weak sales-team performance, pricing, packaging, and market conditions. Cause–effect mapping helps identify the most disturbing/urgent effect and the cause about which knowledge is inadequate or which can address several parts of the problem.

The Five Whys method goes progressively deeper by repeatedly asking why. Example: sales declined → consumer behaviour changed → why did behaviour change? → sociodemographic composition changed → why? Continue until reaching a phenomenon requiring detailed investigation. The transcript calls this the “5 ways” method; context clearly indicates the Five Whys.

When uncertain, the researcher can use three supports:

  1. Empirical evidence/literature: previously reported and tested findings about similar symptoms and causes.
  2. Experts: interviews or focus groups yielding experienced and intuitive knowledge.
  3. Direct exposure/observation: observe the marketplace or setting—for example, listen to consumer deliberations before purchase.

Do not launch fresh research immediately. First determine whether existing evidence can solve the present case. Conduct new research only when evidence is inadequate, not transferable to the current context, or leaves a relevant gap.

2.3 Worked example: declining sales

Suppose declining sales are observed. Cause analysis identifies customer satisfaction as a critical, overarching and insufficiently understood cause. The study then focuses on customer satisfaction and the factors that build or hinder it.

An illustrative problem statement is:

Periodic evaluation of customer satisfaction helps an organization understand changes in customer preferences and manage customer relationships more effectively. Inadequate knowledge of the factors that create customer satisfaction can produce adverse consequences such as sales decline and customer defection.

The reasoning is that customer satisfaction drives favourable consumer behaviour; understanding it can therefore help resolve sales decline.

Practical use case: A retailer sees revenue decline despite stable footfall. A Five Whys exercise shows repeat purchase is falling, complaints concern checkout delays, and staffing patterns changed. The research problem can become: “To examine whether checkout service quality influences satisfaction and repeat-purchase intention among customers of the retailer’s South Indian stores.”

3. Specifying the research area: context, concepts, variables, and stakeholders

The area of research has two dimensions:

  • Context: where, when, industry/product/organization, policy and environmental circumstances.
  • Concept(s): the phenomena to be studied.

In the sales example, customer satisfaction is the core concept. Literature may reveal a large “capital list” of associated factors; the researcher selects variables relevant to the present knowledge deficit.

3.1 Variables in the customer-satisfaction example

  • Product attributes: quality, price-related aspects, packaging, and other product features needing improvement.
  • Store attributes: convenience, layout, in-store promotion, salesperson effectiveness, and the salesperson’s role in informing customers.
  • Price sensitivity: strong competition and lower-priced alternatives may cause switching even when quality improves.
  • Variety seeking: customers may switch because they desire novelty.
  • Customer value: whether benefits received exceed the effort/cost of purchasing.
  • Customer satisfaction: the desired outcome.

Product and store attributes are especially useful because the business can influence them. Price sensitivity and variety seeking introduce complexity because their level may strengthen or weaken the formation of satisfaction.

3.2 Stakeholders

A stakeholder is a person/group whose problem is addressed or whose viewpoint is essential to solving it. Possible stakeholders include customers, marketers, employees, managers, suppliers, channel partners, logistics partners, retailers, the organization, industry, society, or environment.

Select the critical stakeholder rather than collecting everyone’s view. In the running example, customers are best qualified to judge product/store attributes, value, and satisfaction. Once the variables and critical stakeholder are known, research questions, objectives, and scope can be written clearly.

3.3 Questions and objectives

Illustrative research questions:

  • What is the level of customer satisfaction with Product X?
  • What factors contribute to satisfaction with Product X in Context Y?
  • Does satisfaction differ by gender?
  • Does it vary across age groups?

Corresponding objectives use action verbs:

  • to assess/estimate the level of customer satisfaction;
  • to determine the relative importance of product attributes, store attributes, price sensitivity, variety seeking, and customer value;
  • to compare satisfaction across demographic groups.

3.4 Scope and limitations

Scope states what the research includes and cannot explain. It may be defined by:

  • variables: only the named determinants are studied;
  • stakeholders: customers, not every associated entity;
  • geography: e.g., South India, so findings may not apply elsewhere;
  • time: findings apply to the period studied; a budget, GST, or other policy change may alter relationships;
  • organization/product/industry.

Clear exclusions prevent scope creep and overclaiming. A retail-satisfaction study may include retail attributes and staff service quality while explicitly excluding other possible determinants.

4. Literature review, research gap, and theory

The literature review:

  • shows what previous work says about the topic, context, industry, definitions, measures, relationships, and methods;
  • identifies theories that explain the phenomenon;
  • reveals variables and established cause–effect relationships;
  • helps distinguish symptom from cause;
  • provides measurement scales and methodological precedents;
  • identifies gaps: missing variables/context/population, conflicting results, outdated conditions, or imperfect/non-transferable prior studies;
  • supports hypotheses and avoids unsupported assumptions.

A theory explains a phenomenon through relationships among variables. A theoretical framework selects and organizes the relationships relevant to the current problem. Prior research is a foundation, not an automatic answer: the present context may generate a different relationship. If a new contextual variable (for example, a distinctive South Indian cultural trait) is absent from literature, use expert opinion and transparent logical reasoning, then test the assumption.

Weak foundations include practitioner experience alone, a single correlation, or uncritical dependence on one theory. Strong justification triangulates theory, multiple empirical findings, context, logic, and expert knowledge.

5. Definitions and operationalization

5.1 Theoretical versus operational definitions

  • A theoretical/conceptual definition gives the general meaning in literature. Customer satisfaction may be defined as pleasure/happiness after consumption or as the outcome when perceived benefit exceeds expectation.
  • An operational definition states what the construct means and how it applies in this study. A simple method is to add the context and stakeholder to the theoretical definition.

Example: “the feeling of pleasure or happiness experienced by a customer in South India after purchasing Mobile Brand X.”

Operational definitions are necessary because readers and decision-makers must know exactly what each variable means in the case and how it can be measured/applied.

5.2 Constructs and latent variables

Many marketing, HR, and behavioural-finance variables—beliefs, attitudes, feelings, satisfaction, pain, happiness, engagement, loyalty—are latent variables (the transcript sometimes transcribes “Latin”). They cannot be directly observed with a single physical instrument.

By contrast, temperature, attendance, customer time in a store, footfall, sales and absenteeism can be directly measured, subject to privacy and access.

Latent-variable measurement generally requires:

  1. identify observable indicators/items;
  2. ask respondents to rate those indicators through a scale/questionnaire;
  3. aggregate item responses into a construct score;
  4. assess whether the scale validly and reliably represents the construct.

Without measurement, the level of a phenomenon and its relationship with other variables cannot be established.

Example: Employee engagement might be operationalized through enthusiasm, meaningfulness, willingness to exert extra effort, and intent to contribute. Customer loyalty might use repurchase intention, recommendation, and resistance to switching.

6. Classification of variables

  1. Independent variable (IV): causal/predictor variable capable of producing an effect. Example: product attributes or study effort.
  2. Dependent variable (DV): the outcome/effect influenced by other factors. Example: customer satisfaction or marks.
  3. Intervening variable: comes between a cause and effect and alters the process. - Mediator: explains how/why the IV produces the DV. In the study-effort example, IQ was offered as a reason facilitating results. In the marketing model, product/store attributes create customer value, which then creates satisfaction. - Moderator: changes the strength or direction of the IV→DV relationship depending on whether it is low/high. Student interest can strengthen or weaken effort→marks. Price sensitivity and variety seeking can change the ability of product/store attributes to produce value/satisfaction.
  4. Control variable: a factor whose effect is statistically/design-wise held constant so the main relationship can be estimated reliably. Examples: age, gender, education, and income.

The lecture used a gender/product-knowledge illustration to explain control, reflecting a claimed prior-evidence scenario rather than a universal fact. Exam answers should state the methodological point without treating gender stereotypes as inherently true.

Running framework

  • IVs: product attributes, store attributes
  • Mediator: customer value
  • DV: customer satisfaction
  • Moderators: price sensitivity, variety seeking
  • Controls: age, gender, education, income

The logic is: controllable product/store attributes generate perceived value; value generates satisfaction; customer traits can alter the strength of these paths, while demographics are controlled.

7. Hypotheses and theoretical framework

A hypothesis is a logical assumption made at the beginning about a possible relationship or outcome. It is grounded in literature, experience, expert insight, and logic but remains unproven until relevant data and statistical analysis support or reject it.

Two families highlighted in the lecture are:

  1. relationships among variables;
  2. differences in a variable across population groups/subgroups.

Examples:

  • H1: Product attributes significantly predict customer value among users of Product X in Context Y.
  • H2: Store attributes significantly predict customer value among users of Product X in Context Y.
  • H3: Customer value significantly predicts customer satisfaction with Product X in Context Y.
  • H4: Variety seeking significantly changes the ability of product attributes to predict customer value.
  • H5: Satisfaction differs significantly across age groups/genders.

Each hypothesis represents a relationship. Combining them produces a theoretical framework/model. In a diagram, controls may be drawn as predictors of an outcome or listed at the bottom as controls.

Exam distinction: a research question asks what is happening; a hypothesis predicts an answer that is testable.

8. Measurement, respondents, population, and sampling

8.1 Respondents and instruments

For behavioural constructs, the researcher seeks the critical stakeholder’s responses. The data-collection instrument is commonly a questionnaire, and those providing responses are respondents/sample units.

8.2 Population types

The population is the full set of potential respondents:

  • finite and manageable: number/location known and everyone can be reached → census is possible;
  • finite but not manageable: size known but too large for available time/resources → sample;
  • infinite/unknown: no reliable estimate of the relevant population → sample.

A census studies every unit; a sample study/survey examines a subset and uses statistics/probability to infer whether findings likely hold in the population.

Generalizability asks whether results from one sample would recur in the population or another sample. Example: if 500 of a population of 1,000,000 are studied, the researcher must quantify confidence that sample relationships represent the population.

Sampling decisions include:

  • sampling method: how units are chosen;
  • sample size: how many;
  • sample frame: the list/source from which they are selected.

Probability sampling is generally more robust for statistical population estimation than non-probability sampling because selection probabilities support inference.

8.3 Qualitative sample adequacy

Qualitative research uses idea/data saturation, not an arbitrary fixed count: continue interviewing until new participants no longer produce fresh themes. In the career-re-entry example, if an eleventh interview repeats the earlier ten, saturation may have been reached. Quantitative theory testing instead needs a statistically justified sample size.

Eligibility criteria must be explicit. Example: a career-re-entry respondent may need at least five years’ prior work, a three-year break, intention/attempts to return, or successful re-entry. Successful returners and those struggling to return can be separate comparison groups.

9. Research design

Research design is the plan for how the study will progress. It integrates the nature/measurement of variables with the stakeholder population and practical access.

Core decisions include:

  • purpose and type of study;
  • sampling method, size, and frame;
  • time frame and why it is suitable;
  • scale/instrument development;
  • data source and collection mode;
  • researcher intervention and bias control;
  • study setting and manipulation;
  • expected data quality and analysis strategy.

9.1 Intervention and setting

Researchers may carry planning-stage prejudice into collection and unintentionally lead respondents. Responses should be independent; one respondent should not hear and copy another.

  • No/low intervention: respondents express opinions freely.
  • Controlled intervention: provide a standardized stimulus, such as showing an advertisement before measuring reactions.
  • Manipulated/laboratory setting: set conditions such as temperature or pressure in a biological/technical experiment.

Behavioural and attitude surveys often require no setting manipulation, but intervention and bias must still be controlled.

Collection may be online, telephone-based, or face-to-face; suitability depends on respondents, access, question sensitivity, and data-quality needs.

10. Primary and secondary data

  • Primary data: collected for the first time for the current inquiry, using an instrument developed/selected by the researcher—e.g., questionnaire, interview, observation.
  • Secondary data: already collected or held by an organization/public source—e.g., annual reports, HRMS records, CRM data, web analytics, existing scraped reviews.

Classification can depend on the actual collection history. If the researcher newly scrapes public reviews into a dataset, the assembled data may be primary for that project; if an existing scraped dataset is reused, it is secondary.

“Virtual” does not automatically mean secondary, and “real engagement” does not automatically mean primary. Online Google Forms collect primary data. Conversely, confidential secondary HR data may require direct negotiation with an HR representative.

Practical examples

Amazon satisfaction:

  • Primary route: survey Amazon users about layout, discounts, payments, product quality, and returns using ratings such as very good–very bad or most–least important.
  • Alternative route: mine publicly posted customer reviews to identify sources of satisfaction/dissatisfaction. Return-related complaints can support a focused study of rising return intention.

Digital marketing and purchase: combine website traffic, click-through rate, impressions and conversion data (analytics/secondary) with survey-based purchase intention (primary). Conversion is observed behaviour; intention may need a survey.

Brand equity: theories commonly include awareness, loyalty, perceived quality, and brand associations. Measure through a primary survey or use accessible brand-audit scores.

HR engagement/productivity: use engagement scores, KPI/performance data, absenteeism and tenure from HRMS/appraisal records, supplemented with a survey where necessary.

Turnover: analyze attrition, age, tenure, salary, exit reasons and exit interviews; investigate dissatisfaction, supervision, and organizational culture as possible causes.

The research problem determines what data are needed and where they can be obtained—not the convenience of a favorite technique.

11. Planning analysis before collection

The questionnaire and dataset must contain variables required by the intended analysis. Correct sequence:

Problem → objectives/questions/hypotheses → framework → operational definitions → analysis plan → instrument/data specification → collection → analysis → interpretation.

Descriptive statistics summarize the observed data; inferential methods evaluate relationships/differences and generalize beyond a sample.

Techniques mentioned include regression, time-series analysis, trend analysis, gap analysis, factor analysis, and structural equation modeling (SEM), with software such as Excel or SPSS. Use the technique because it answers the research question, and state both method and tool in the proposal.

  • Multiple regression estimates several predictors of one outcome.
  • SEM can assess multiple linked relationships and latent constructs in a single theoretical model, including mediation.
  • Factor analysis examines whether observed items cluster into the underlying construct(s), helping evaluate measurement structure.

Poor advance planning can yield omitted variables, unusable scales, inadequate sample size, or data incapable of testing the proposed model.

12. Operations and other domains

The same process is not restricted to marketing. For operational quality loss, treat loss as an effect and investigate reasons such as machine temperature, equipment condition, process variables, or operator/service quality. Hypothesize each cause’s relationship and strength. HR, marketing and behavioural finance often share latent attitudinal variables; operations may use more directly measurable variables.

13. Research proposal: required content

A proposal is the plan for the intended research. It should answer:

  1. What? Business problem, how it was detected, research problem/questions, context and objectives.
  2. Why/importance? Urgency, contribution and likely impact on business, industry, society, environment or stakeholders.
  3. What exists? Brief literature review of the topic, concepts, industry/context, prior findings and research gap.
  4. Theory? Variables, expected relationships, assumptions/hypotheses and theoretical framework.
  5. How? Methodology: required data, primary/secondary source, population/sample, instrument, collection approach, analysis method and software.
  6. Outcome? Expected findings and how they may guide a solution.
  7. Beneficiaries/impact? Customers, employees, organization, industry, society, environment, etc.
  8. Constraints? Scope, exclusions and limitations.

The lecturer suggested approximately 1,500–1,600 words for the proposal and emphasized specificity rather than excessive writing. Follow the supplied template, although a sensible logical order containing all essentials was described as acceptable. The proposal deadline stated in that live session was May 15; treat this as session-specific administrative information, not a permanent rule.

Proposal self-test

A topic is likely researchable if about 60–80% of these can be answered convincingly:

  • What problem will it help solve?
  • What contribution will it make?
  • What relevant background work exists?
  • What theory/variables explain it?
  • What data are needed?
  • Are the data accessible, and from what source?
  • Can the scope be completed with available time/resources?

14. Live-session topic guidance and Q&A

14.1 Choosing and refining topics

  • A project may be in marketing, HR, analytics, operations, finance, or AI; the domain is less important than problem relevance, contribution, theory, feasible data and a controlled scope.
  • An industry-wide issue, a specific business issue, or an issue concerning customers/channel/logistics partners is acceptable. State what is included.
  • Broad topics should be narrowed. “Impact of technology on operational efficiency” becomes stronger as “Impact of AI on recruitment/logistics planning/product design efficiency.”
  • A dream application/coding project is permissible if the report clearly defines the problem, explains why another tool is needed, and demonstrates how the application benefits its users. Reverse-map the intended solution to a genuine problem and the proposal template.

Examples discussed:

  • AI integration: an exploratory study of hurdles in creating an AI-enabled workplace can be justified through anticipated efficiency benefits and implementation challenges.
  • ESG scores and HDFC Bank performance: feasible with public annual-report data, but explain benefit to the bank/other banks’ ESG policy; confirm access if non-public data are needed.
  • Women’s career re-entry: strong because it has psychological, HR and social dimensions and publication potential. It may validate an existing framework through a survey or explore new motives/barriers through in-depth interviews and saturation. Motives may include social acceptance, financial stability, self-reliance, engagement and meaningfulness.
  • HR compliance and employee relations: relevant, but specify organization/general context and purpose.
  • Gen Z/young employee resignation in insurance agencies: relevant because turnover increases recruitment/training and retention costs; investigate retention/continuance intention rather than assuming frequent moves guarantee faster growth.
  • Digital-payment adoption and financial behaviour: use technology-adoption/diffusion theories; add contemporary variables such as review quality/volume if simple adoption is already heavily studied.
  • Digital payments, service quality and spending: relevant.
  • Hyperlocal/B2B2C delivery: explore whether customers perceive integrated service quality or distinguish the responsibilities/quality of each business in the chain.
  • Supplier sourcing risk: move beyond cost-only vendor selection by quantifying supply-risk criteria, especially under geopolitical disruption.
  • AI/data analytics influencing voter behaviour: acceptable as exploratory research, but formulate the problematic outcome and contribution—e.g., risks to informed choice/democratic principles—and convert the interesting phenomenon into a problem statement.

15. Quiz-derived exam reinforcement

The quiz material reinforces these principles:

  • A weak problem (“study employee satisfaction”) lacks variables, population, context and relationship. A stronger one asks whether appraisal fairness influences intention to stay among employees of a specified organization.
  • Root-cause tools such as Five Whys and fishbone diagrams help convert a broad issue into a relationship that can be tested.
  • The theoretical framework must align hypotheses, constructs and measures. Adding an attractive hypothesis outside the framework creates an alignment problem.
  • Constructs require precise definitions and operational indicators; otherwise different stakeholders may interpret the same word differently.
  • Confounders offer alternative explanations and must be controlled through scope, design or analysis.

DeltaTech integrated case

The quiz uses a case in which DeltaTech shows multiple symptoms across HR, customers and market performance. The planning challenge is to avoid treating symptoms as the problem, combine initial sources such as sales/CRM reports and HR surveys/performance data, and formulate a bounded research problem.

An illustrative framework links:

leadership communication → employee engagement → customer satisfaction/loyalty → market performance

Hypotheses must correspond to these paths. “Employee engagement predicts customer retention” fits this framework if retention is properly linked/defined. A hypothesis about product roadmaps and market share requires theory and variables covering product-roadmap quality; otherwise it falls outside the model and causes scope creep.

Important constructs include:

  • leadership communication: clarity, consistency, timeliness and transparency of strategic information;
  • employee engagement: cognitive/emotional involvement and willingness to contribute;
  • customer loyalty: repurchase, recommendation and resistance to switching;
  • organizational culture: shared values/norms shaping behaviour.

Cross-sectional survey versus mixed methods

Cross-sectional survey: structured data collected once from a relatively large sample.

  • Strengths: efficiency, larger sample, statistical power and broader generalizability.
  • Limits: less depth/context; weak ability to establish temporal causality; respondents’ interpretations may remain hidden.

Mixed methods: qualitative exploration plus quantitative testing.

  • Qualitative component: interviews/focus groups explain meanings, mechanisms and missing variables.
  • Quantitative component: survey tests the refined model across a larger sample.
  • Strengths: depth, stronger construct understanding, improved item/framework development and triangulation.
  • Limits: more time/cost/skill; the quantitative sample may be smaller under fixed resources.

A sequential recommendation is:

  1. conduct interviews to clarify constructs, mechanisms and stakeholder perspectives;
  2. refine the model/instrument;
  3. validate it with a quantitative survey.

Validity trade-offs

  • Construct validity: measures truly represent the intended constructs; mixed methods often help by clarifying meaning.
  • Statistical conclusion validity: statistical relationships are estimated correctly; adequate sample/power and sound analysis matter.
  • External validity: findings generalize to relevant populations/settings; representative larger surveys help.
  • Internal validity: alternative causal explanations are ruled out; a one-time survey alone is weak for causal claims.

Stakeholders may prioritize different outcomes: technology leaders want strategic/technical insight, HR leaders employee mechanisms, marketing leaders customer effects, finance leaders costs/returns, and operations leaders feasibility. Research design should respond to decision needs without adding unrelated variables until the study becomes unmanageable.

16. High-yield exam distinctions

Pair Key distinction
Symptom vs research problem Symptom is observed underperformance; research problem is the specific knowledge gap/relationship to investigate.
Business problem vs research question Business problem needs managerial action; research question specifies evidence needed for that action.
Conceptual vs operational definition General theoretical meaning vs context-specific, measurable meaning.
IV vs DV Predictor/cause vs outcome/effect.
Mediator vs moderator Explains how/why a relationship occurs vs changes its strength/direction.
Control vs confounder Control is held constant/adjusted; confounder is an alternative explanatory factor requiring control.
Census vs sample Every population unit vs subset used for inference.
Primary vs secondary data Newly collected for current inquiry vs previously collected/held. Collection mode does not determine the distinction.
Research question vs hypothesis Open inquiry vs testable predicted answer.
Scope vs limitation Deliberate boundary of coverage vs constraint affecting what can be concluded.
Cross-sectional vs mixed methods One-time structured breadth vs integrated qualitative depth and quantitative validation.

17. Exam-answer template

For a “design a research plan” question:

  1. State the symptom and evidence/KPI.
  2. Map plausible causes and identify the knowledge deficit.
  3. Write the research problem and contextual problem statement.
  4. Identify core concept, variables, critical stakeholder and scope.
  5. Summarize relevant literature, theory and gap.
  6. Define and classify every variable.
  7. Write questions/objectives and aligned hypotheses.
  8. Draw/describe the theoretical framework.
  9. Operationalize variables and state data requirements.
  10. Define population, census/sample, method, size and frame.
  11. State setting, intervention, time frame, collection method and ethics/bias controls.
  12. Pre-specify analysis and software.
  13. Explain expected insight, managerial decision, beneficiaries, limitations and generalizability.
