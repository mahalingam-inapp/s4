Week overview

Week 14 links four themes:

  1. solar photovoltaic (PV) systems—the photovoltaic effect, system types, components, operation, panel technologies, advantages, and major Indian solar parks;
  2. India’s renewable-energy ecosystem—MNRE and public institutions, leading private companies, and the roles they play;
  3. pollution—meaning, pollutants, causes, consequences, control measures, and seven major types; and
  4. social sustainability—stakeholder impacts, business benefits, implementation, ESG, CSR, and ISO 26000.

The instructor repeatedly stresses that renewable energy is a practical alternative to exhaustible fossil fuels, pollution affects the whole ecosystem rather than humans alone, and sustainability must address people and society as seriously as it addresses the environment.

1. Solar Photovoltaic (PV) Systems

1.1 Meaning and basic principle

  • A photovoltaic (PV) system converts light energy into electrical energy.
  • Sunlight consists of photons, described in the lecture as “packets of energy.” Semiconductor material captures this light and produces electric current through the photovoltaic effect (PV effect).
  • A solar cell is essentially a semiconductor p–n junction diode. The conventional material is silicon.
  • PV systems combine multiple components to use solar energy and generate usable electricity.
  • Scale ranges from portable devices and small home/office rooftops to industrial installations and massive utility-scale plants. Domestic examples mentioned are 3 kW and 5 kW systems; large projects extend through MW and toward GW scale.
  • Governments, including India’s, seek to increase renewable-energy capacity, with solar power as a major contributor.
  • Research seeks more efficient PV panels using newer materials. The transcript appears to refer to perovskite and other emerging material technologies, but the wording is unclear.

Key units

Unit Meaning
kW $10^3$ watts
MW $10^6$ watts
GW $10^9$ watts

1.2 Types of PV system

System Grid connection Storage Main idea/use
Grid-connected/on-grid Yes Not essential Integrated with the conventional residential/industrial electricity system. Powers a home or business; surplus generation is fed to the common grid. At night or during a deficit, power can come from the grid. A utility meter records household use and export. State schemes may support domestic rooftop installations, reducing household electricity bills while supplying excess power to the grid.
Hybrid Yes Yes A modified grid-connected system with a battery pack; combines grid support with stored solar power.
Off-grid/stand-alone No Yes Independent system for a particular home or load. Uses a PV array, battery, charge controller, and inverter and is useful where grid connection is absent or intentionally avoided.

High-value distinction: on-grid is connected to the utility; off-grid is stand-alone; hybrid combines grid connection and battery storage.

1.3 PV hierarchy and components

The lecture gives the aggregation sequence:

PV cells → module → array → panel/system

Many cells form a module; multiple modules form an array; arrays are combined into the larger panel/system arrangement. Terminology is sometimes used differently in industry, but reproduce the lecture sequence if specifically examined.

Component Function
PV cell/module/array Semiconductor elements capture photons and produce direct current (DC) through the PV effect. Linking panels/arrays increases output.
Battery bank Stores surplus solar electricity for future, emergency, or non-sunlight use. Essential in off-grid systems and present in hybrids.
Charge controller Located between the panel and battery; regulates charging and prevents dangerous overcharging and possible battery damage/explosion.
Inverter Converts solar DC into alternating current (AC) used by the utility grid and common electrical appliances.
Utility/power meter Measures household electricity requirement and the amount imported from or exported to the grid.
Grid Receives surplus electricity in an on-grid system and supplies electricity when solar generation is unavailable or inadequate, including at night.

1.4 Working of a PV system

  1. Sunlight/photons fall on semiconductor solar cells.
  2. The photovoltaic effect creates electric current.
  3. Connected cells/modules/arrays aggregate the output.
  4. The electricity produced by the panel is DC.
  5. A charge controller safely directs energy to a battery where storage is used.
  6. The inverter changes DC to AC.
  7. AC powers appliances; a grid-connected system exports surplus through the meter and imports power during shortfall/night.

Phones and laptops internally use DC, but ordinary premises and the utility grid supply AC; hence the inverter is a crucial interface.

One-line answer: solar energy is the input, electricity is the output, and the conversion mechanism is the photovoltaic effect.

1.5 Advantages and significance

  • Renewable and inexhaustible: sunlight is abundant and does not face the depletion problem of fossil fuels.
  • Clean and eco-friendly: operation produces no direct air, water, or noise pollution, helping address global warming, climate change, and damage to humans, flora, fauna, and the wider environment.
  • Accessible and distributed: panels can be installed in many locations—homes, offices, industries, airports, and remote sites—making solar suitable for distributed generation and intelligent energy networks.
  • Economical over operation: sunlight itself is free; users can reduce purchased grid electricity and export surplus. Economic feasibility remains an important project consideration.
  • Low maintenance cost compared with many other energy sources.
  • Noiseless operation, unlike many mechanical generation technologies.
  • Highly scalable: from kW rooftop installations to MW and GW capacity.
  • Strategic value: renewable capacity contributes to national energy needs while reducing fossil-fuel pollution.

The instructor’s strongest emphasis is that clean energy and absence of pollution are solar PV’s central advantage.

1.8 Practical use cases

  • Urban home: a 3–5 kW on-grid rooftop system lowers the bill and exports unused daytime power.
  • Remote home/farm: an off-grid array plus battery, charge controller, and inverter supplies power where the utility is unavailable; wind energy can also support farming applications.
  • Reliability-sensitive site: a hybrid system uses both grid supply and stored energy.
  • Factory/office/airport: larger arrays supply clean power, reduce operating emissions, and support sustainability goals.
  • Utility: MW-scale solar parks add substantial renewable capacity to the grid.

2. Renewable-Energy Institutions and Companies in India

2.1 Policy context and MNRE

India seeks to expand renewable-energy capacity through new projects, research, finance, promotion, and coordination. Solar is presented as a leading source, but the ecosystem also includes wind, small hydro, biogas/biomass, and hybrid systems.

The Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE) is the Government of India ministry responsible for:

  • research and development;
  • international cooperation and coordination; and
  • promotion of renewable sources including wind, small hydro, biogas, and solar power.

3. Pollution: Concept, Pollutants, Impacts, and Control

3.1 Definitions

  • A pollutant is a harmful material or form of energy released into the environment.
  • Environmental pollution is the addition of a solid, liquid, gas, or form of energy—such as heat, sound, or radioactivity—to the environment faster than it can be dispersed, diluted, decomposed, recycled, or stored in a harmless form.
  • Pollution can originate naturally (for example volcanic ash/eruptions, floods, storms, or algal blooms) but human activity—factories, transport, fuel combustion, waste, mining, sewage, and chemicals—is emphasized as the major contributor.
  • Pollutants degrade air, water, and land. Because all life depends on these resources, pollution threatens humans, plants, animals, birds, wildlife habitats, ecosystems, and society.
  • Pollution is often more intense in cities, but it is not geographically confined: it can spread to remote places with little or no human settlement.

3.2 Forms introduced

The lecture mentions air, water, land/soil, noise/sound, thermal, radioactive, light/photo, and plastic pollution. Plastic cuts across land and water rather than being treated only as an isolated category.

Plastic as a cross-media problem

  • Bags, bottles, and other plastic materials persist on land and enter water bodies.
  • They cause land/soil pollution and marine/water pollution.
  • Aquatic organisms and marine animals may ingest plastic, leading to injury or death.
  • The instructor calls for reduced plastic consumption, bans/restrictions where appropriate, and substitution with sustainable materials.
  • A single location can experience air, land, and soil pollution together; pollution types interact.

3.3 Major air pollutants discussed

Pollutant Features and major sources Significance
Particulate matter (PM) Common proxy indicator for air pollution. Components include sulfates, nitrates, ammonia, sodium chloride, black carbon, mineral dust, and water; industry is a major source. Strong evidence links PM exposure with adverse health effects.
Carbon monoxide (CO) Colourless, odourless, tasteless toxic gas from incomplete combustion of carbon-rich fuels such as wood, petrol, charcoal, natural gas, and kerosene. Automobiles are highlighted as a major source. Toxic and potentially deadly.
Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) Released by fuel combustion in transport and industry. Major industrial/transport contaminant; nitrogen oxides also contribute to acid rain.
Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) Colourless gas with a sharp odour, produced by burning coal and oil. Fossil-fuel pollutant; contributes to acid rain.
Ground-level ozone (O₃) Forms through atmospheric gas reactions in sunlight and is a major constituent of photochemical smog. Harmful pollutant; distinguish it from protective stratospheric ozone.
CFCs/halons Associated particularly with older refrigeration and air-conditioning uses. Break down ozone molecules and contribute to ozone-layer depletion.

Smog: The lecture intends smoke + fog and associates photochemical smog with sunlight/UV acting on nitrogen oxides and other atmospheric pollutants.

3.4 Overall negative impacts

Environmental and ecological damage

  • Degrades land, air, water, and neighbouring areas.
  • Damages habitats and wildlife; animals and birds can die or lose suitable habitat.
  • Contaminates fresh water with bacteria, chemicals, particulates, oil, sewage, and plastics.
  • Reduces natural environmental protection; deforestation worsens degradation, while afforestation/reforestation is needed.

Human health

  • Lung and respiratory disease, including lung cancer.
  • Heart/cardiovascular problems.
  • Skin cancer and other cancers.
  • Kidney, liver, brain, eye, skin, stress, and hearing effects depending on pollutant/type.
  • Workers near sources such as coal mines can be especially exposed.

Global warming and climate effects

  • The lecture connects industrial emissions and fossil-fuel combustion with greenhouse gases and global warming.
  • Global warming is the increase in Earth’s average surface/climatic temperature.
  • Consequences include melting polar ice caps and glaciers and rising sea levels.
  • Carbon dioxide and methane are central greenhouse gases. The lecture also lists sulfur dioxide in this context; scientifically, SO₂ is primarily an air pollutant and aerosol precursor rather than a principal long-lived greenhouse gas—retain the lecture association cautiously.

Acid rain

  • Acidic rain/precipitation with elevated hydrogen-ion concentration.
  • Mainly linked to sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides.
  • Harms trees, soil, water, wildlife, and built heritage.
  • Case/example: the Taj Mahal’s change from white toward yellow is attributed in the lecture to acid rain/pollution.

Ozone depletion

  • The stratospheric ozone layer shields Earth from harmful ultraviolet radiation.
  • CFCs and halons cause chemical reactions that break down ozone molecules, thinning the layer/creating an “ozone hole.”
  • Increased UV exposure raises risks of skin cancer and eye/skin-cell injury.

3.5 Pollution-control framework by sector

Sector Measures discussed
Industry Adopt clean technologies; install advanced filters/emission-control systems; reduce harmful releases; improve urban and agricultural waste management; capture methane from waste sites rather than relying on incineration.
Household energy Ensure affordable clean energy for cooking, heating, and lighting; promote solar cookers and other low-pollution household solutions.
Transport Shift to cleaner modes and progressively reduce fossil-fuel vehicles; promote electric cars/two-wheelers, CNG vehicles, hydrogen cars, and other clean-energy vehicles. The lecture envisages a long transition of roughly 30–60 years.
Urban planning/buildings Improve building energy efficiency; create greener, more compact cities; use green-building practices.
Power Use low-emission fuels and renewable, combustion-free sources; expand solar and other renewables; use cogeneration (combined production of heat and power from one system) and distributed generation such as rooftop solar on homes, offices, and factories.
Healthcare Reduce environmental health risks for patients, health workers, and facilities; align health services with pollution prevention.
Municipal/agriculture/waste Prevent and reduce waste; reuse and recycle; use anaerobic digestion and biogas plants; improve overall waste-management strategy.

Instructor’s preferred strategic answer: replace fossil-fuel dependence with clean, renewable, energy-efficient systems across transport, homes, offices, industry, and power generation, supported by waste prevention and pollution-control technology.

4.2 Air pollution

Causes

  • Combustion of coal, petroleum, natural gas, petrol, and diesel releases carbon dioxide, smoke, and other contaminants.
  • Underground/surface mining and coal mining produce gases and particulates.
  • Factories and motor vehicles produce CO, SO₂, NOₓ, smoke, and related pollution.
  • Human industrial activity is emphasized as the dominant cause.

Effects and examples

  • Long-term heart, lung, and respiratory diseases; damage to kidneys, liver, brain, and other organs.
  • Coal-mine workers are given as a high-risk example for lung disease/cancer.
  • Greenhouse-gas accumulation and global warming.
  • Acid rain from SO₂ and NOₓ; trees can die and wildlife is harmed.
  • Ozone depletion when CFCs/halons chemically destroy ozone.
  • Both people and wildlife/habitats suffer; do not write a human-only answer.

4.3 Water pollution

  • All water bodies—rivers, lakes, seas, and oceans—can receive toxic matter, particulates, and unwanted contaminants.
  • Human sources: oil spills, shipping, industrial waste/effluent, sewage, plastics, and chemicals. Industries located near rivers are a recurring example.
  • Natural sources mentioned: algal blooms, volcanoes, floods, and storms/animal-related contamination (last phrase is unclear in transcript).
  • Fresh, clean water is essential to humans, birds, and animals.
  • Effects: aquatic ecosystem imbalance, harm/death to marine organisms, waterborne disease, mercury/chemical contamination, and plastic ingestion.
  • Visual anecdote from slide: a lake near an industry is shown polluted, illustrating proximity between industry and contaminated water.

4.4 Soil/land pollution

  • The lecture uses soil pollution and land pollution interchangeably.
  • It is degradation by chemicals or changes to the natural soil environment.
  • Sources: industrial activity, agricultural chemicals, improper corporate/municipal waste disposal, heavy metals, and deforestation.
  • Effects:
  • nutrient depletion and loss of fertility, making land unfit for agriculture;
  • increased soil salinity/salt content and degraded vegetation;
  • damage to soil flora, fauna, microbes, and small living species;
  • toxic silica dust causing respiratory/lung problems;
  • chemical exposure associated in the lecture with neuromuscular effects, eye irritation, skin rashes, and depression.
  • Soil is a prerequisite for plant growth and agriculture; reforestation is relevant to restoring degraded land.

4.5 Noise pollution

  • Sound is measured in decibels (dB).
  • Noise pollution means excessive environmental sound that disrupts natural balance.
  • Man-made sources include heavy machinery, factories, transport, construction, boring/drilling, firecrackers, loudspeakers, and household appliances such as mixers; volcanoes are a natural example.
  • It irritates humans, animals, and birds.
  • Prolonged exposure causes sleep loss, fatigue, hearing loss, hypertension, stress, communication difficulty, and cardiovascular problems.
  • It can reduce workplace productivity. The instructor defines productivity here as the rate at which a worker performs work; reduced productivity lowers factory output and also affects office work.

4.6 Thermal pollution

  • Also called thermal enrichment.
  • A process that changes ambient water temperature and thereby degrades water quality; the change may be a rise or a drop.
  • Unlike chemical pollution, it changes water’s physical properties.
  • Major cause: power plants and industrial manufacturers using water as a coolant and returning it at a different temperature.
  • Example: releasing very cold water from the bottom of a reservoir into a warmer river can also create thermal pollution.

4.7 Radioactive pollution

  • Pollution caused by harmful radiation; living organisms normally encounter radiation, but levels above a limit become harmful.
  • Lecture classification:
  • Non-ionising radiation: ultraviolet radiation is cited; effects include eye damage and injury to skin cells.
  • Ionising radiation: X-rays, cosmic rays, and atomic/nuclear radiation.
  • Effects include cancer and serious damage to living organisms.

Concept caveat: In standard physics, ultraviolet spans non-ionising and ionising ranges depending on energy, and the lecture’s broad classification is simplified. For a transcript-based exam, reproduce the categories stated while explaining the central distinction: ionising radiation has enough energy to ionise matter and is generally more directly damaging to cells/DNA.

4.8 Light pollution

  • Also called photo pollution: excessive artificial light arising from urbanization and industrialization.
  • Sources: streetlights, domestic and commercial lighting, vehicle lights, and security lights.
  • Their combined effect creates a dome-like shield/glow over illuminated cities.
  • It is a human-caused environmental pollution alongside plastics, greenhouse gases, and sewage and has serious implications for the environment.

5. Social Sustainability

5.1 Meaning and relation to ESG/CSR

  • In ESG, the “S” is social. Social sustainability is therefore not secondary to environmental sustainability; the instructor calls it equally important.
  • It identifies and manages a business’s positive and negative impacts on people.
  • Corporate sustainability and CSR concern society, so the quality of company–stakeholder relationships and engagement is critical.
  • Businesses directly or indirectly affect employees, workers throughout the value chain/supply chain, customers, and local communities. These impacts should be managed proactively, not only after conflict or harm occurs.

Core definition

Social sustainability occurs when formal and informal processes, systems, structures, and relationships actively support the capacity of present and future generations to create healthy and liveable communities.

It is also a process of creating sustainable and successful places that promote wellbeing by understanding what people need from where they live and work.

Link to sustainable development

  • Sustainable development means meeting present needs without compromising future generations’ ability to meet their needs.
  • Social sustainability applies this intergenerational principle to community health, quality of life, participation, relationships, and opportunity.
  • Socially sustainable communities are diverse, connected, democratic, healthy/liveable, and capable of providing a good quality of life.
  • Main objective: reduce negative impacts on society and the environment while serving people.

5.2 Physical and social design

Social sustainability combines:

  • design of the physical realm—infrastructure, amenities, and usable public/social spaces; and
  • design of the social world—relationships, social and cultural life, citizen-engagement systems, and opportunities/places for people to meet and develop.

Organizations must understand people’s needs and wants, the way they live, and the services and spaces required for wellbeing. A good society depends on sound relationships among people and between companies and communities.

5.3 Business benefits (UN Global Compact framing cited)

Aiming for social sustainability can:

  • unlock new markets;
  • attract and retain business partners;
  • become a source of innovation and new product/service lines;
  • increase customer acceptance and enhance company goodwill/reputation when new products succeed;
  • improve internal morale and employee engagement;
  • improve risk management; and
  • reduce or better manage company–community conflict.

Thus social sustainability is not merely charitable expenditure; it can support innovation, market growth, human resources, relationships, and enterprise resilience.

5.4 Stakeholder framework

Stakeholder group Examples from lecture Core responsibility
Internal Employees, workers, employer/management, senior officials, board of directors, shareholders Fair work, safety, inclusion, dignity, engagement, and morale
Value/supply chain Workers and suppliers connected to the firm’s operations Extend responsible labour and safety expectations beyond direct employees
External Customers, local communities, society, government, competitors, business partners Good-neighbour relations, responsible influence, social service, engagement, and impact management

5.5 How an organization can achieve social sustainability

A. Act with internal stakeholders and through the supply chain

  • Apply fair labour practices.
  • Ensure equal pay and good working conditions.
  • Protect freedom of association.
  • Prevent workplace harassment.
  • Treat legal compliance as the baseline, not the complete ambition.
  • Provide effective, comfortable, appropriate health-and-safety equipment.
  • Prioritize industrial safety and worker protection.
  • Promote equality, diversity, inclusion, and equal employment opportunity for all groups without prejudice.
  • Improve morale and engagement; the instructor repeatedly states that employees are indispensable—without them, there is no organization.
  • Integrate the human-resource dimension (fairness and inclusion) with the technical dimension (industrial health and safety).

B. Act with external stakeholders and communities

  • Be a good neighbour and maintain constructive company–society relations.
  • Recognize that firms can exercise political influence and help shape laws and regulations; this influence must be handled responsibly.
  • Where government capacity is weak, companies may act like “private governments” by helping meet welfare needs such as schooling and medical care.
  • CSR spending can support education, schooling, healthcare, community growth, and service to people.
  • Public- and private-sector firms may spend lakhs/crores on CSR because business ultimately serves society and customers.

Practical application framework

  1. Map positive and negative impacts across employees, value-chain workers, customers, and communities.
  2. Identify internal and external stakeholders.
  3. Establish legal compliance and fair-labour fundamentals.
  4. Improve safety, equality, diversity, inclusion, pay, and working conditions.
  5. Engage communities to discover actual needs rather than assuming them.
  6. Design physical amenities and social/cultural participation together.
  7. Invest in relevant education, health, and welfare initiatives.
  8. Monitor morale, conflict, risk, wellbeing, reputation, and stakeholder trust.
  9. Manage impacts proactively and align them with ESG, CSR, sustainable development, and ISO 26000.

5.6 ISO 26000

  • ISO 26000 was issued in 2010 by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO).
  • It is linked to social responsibility/social sustainability.
  • Its broad goal is to contribute to global sustainable development by encouraging businesses and other organizations to practise social responsibility and improve their impacts on workers, the natural environment, and communities.
  • The lecture connects it with the UN Sustainable Development Goals and with reducing environmental harm while serving society.

Common confusion: ISO 26000 provides guidance on social responsibility; it is not a certifiable management-system standard in the same sense as many other ISO standards. The transcript casually calls it an “ISO certification,” but an exam answer gains accuracy by calling it a guidance standard unless the teacher explicitly expects the transcript wording.

5.7 Instructor emphasis

  • “Everything we do is for society/the customer.”
  • Industry exists to serve people; therefore relationships with society are crucial.
  • Environmental action alone is insufficient: sustainability must include wellbeing, dignity, services, relationships, inclusion, and citizen engagement.
  • Proactive impact management and stakeholder engagement are central.
  • Social sustainability is essential to CSR and to the “S” in ESG.

6. Integrated Understanding Across the Week

The five lectures form one argument:

  1. Fossil-fuel activity and poorly managed industrialization create air, water, land, thermal, noise, and other pollution.
  2. Pollution damages human health, workers, communities, biodiversity, habitats, and future generations.
  3. Solar, wind, biogas, and hybrid systems provide cleaner alternatives; PV converts free sunlight into electricity and scales from homes to solar parks.
  4. Government institutions (MNRE, SECI, IREDA, NIWE, NISE) and private firms supply policy coordination, research, finance, manufacturing, EPC, and power generation.
  5. Technology alone does not create sustainability. Firms must also protect workers, include stakeholders, serve communities, and responsibly manage social impacts.
  6. Therefore, a complete corporate-sustainability answer should integrate environmental technology + pollution prevention + social responsibility + governance/stakeholder engagement.

Mini case: a socially sustainable solar project

A company building a solar park should not argue only that solar produces clean electricity. It should also:

  • assess land, water, biodiversity, and construction impacts;
  • ensure fair pay, safe work, freedom from harassment, and proper protective equipment;
  • consult local residents and understand livelihood/community needs;
  • provide appropriate infrastructure, training, education, or health support;
  • manage suppliers and value-chain labour responsibly;
  • communicate with government and communities transparently; and
  • track clean-energy output, avoided pollution, worker outcomes, community conflict, and shared benefits.

This converts a renewable-energy installation into a broader ESG and social-sustainability initiative.

7. Exam Preparation

7.1 Likely short-answer questions and model points

1. Define the photovoltaic effect.

  • Conversion of light energy/photons into electric current in semiconductor material.
  • Occurs in a solar cell/p–n junction.
  • Initial output is DC.

2. Differentiate on-grid, off-grid, and hybrid PV systems.

  • On-grid: utility connected; surplus exported, deficit imported.
  • Off-grid: stand-alone; battery, controller, inverter required.
  • Hybrid: grid connection plus battery storage.

3. State the functions of a charge controller and inverter.

  • Charge controller regulates battery charging and prevents overcharge/damage.
  • Inverter converts DC solar output to AC for premises/grid use.

4. Compare monocrystalline and polycrystalline panels.

  • Mono: single pure silicon crystal, higher efficiency, higher cost.
  • Poly: multiple crystals, lower efficiency, more affordable.

5. What is thin-film PV?

  • Thin photovoltaic layers such as amorphous silicon or CIGS.
  • Lightweight, flexible, and relatively easy to install.

6. Define pollution and pollutant.

  • Pollutant: harmful material/form of energy in the environment.
  • Pollution: addition faster than natural systems can safely disperse, dilute, decompose, recycle, or store it.
  • Can be solid, liquid, gas, heat, sound, or radiation.

7. What is particulate matter?

  • Proxy indicator for air pollution.
  • Mixture can include sulfate, nitrate, ammonia, sodium chloride, black carbon, mineral dust, and water.
  • Strongly associated with adverse health effects.

8. Define thermal pollution.

  • Human-caused change (rise or fall) in natural water temperature that degrades water quality.
  • Commonly caused by power/industrial cooling-water discharge.

9. Define social sustainability.

  • Formal/informal systems, structures, processes, and relationships supporting present and future generations’ capacity to create healthy, liveable communities.
  • Manages business impacts on people and promotes wellbeing.

10. What is ISO 26000?

  • ISO guidance standard on social responsibility, issued in 2010.
  • Encourages organizations to improve impacts on workers, communities, and the environment and contribute to sustainable development.
  • Not a certifiable management-system standard.

7.2 Likely long-answer questions and answer skeletons

Q1. Explain the construction, components, and working of a solar PV system.

Model structure:

  1. Define PV and photovoltaic effect.
  2. Explain silicon semiconductor/p–n junction and photon-to-DC conversion.
  3. Give hierarchy: cell → module → array → panel/system.
  4. Explain PV array, battery, charge controller, inverter, meter, and grid.
  5. Present stepwise energy flow from sunlight to AC/load/grid.
  6. Compare on-grid, off-grid, and hybrid configurations.
  7. Conclude with scalability and clean-energy benefits.

Q2. Discuss types of solar panels and the advantages of PV systems.

Model structure:

  • Table comparing mono, poly, and thin film by structure, efficiency/cost, flexibility.
  • Advantages: renewable, clean, accessible, distributed, noiseless, low-maintenance, economical operation, scalable.
  • Applications: rooftop, remote, industry, airport, utility park.
  • Conclude with the instructor’s clean-energy/climate emphasis.

Q3. Describe India’s institutional ecosystem for renewable energy.

Model structure:

  • India’s capacity-expansion objective and MNRE’s coordinating role.
  • SECI—solar mission implementation.
  • IREDA—promotion, development, finance, efficiency/conservation.
  • NIWE—wind R&D/solutions.
  • NISE—solar PV and thermal R&D/resource centre.
  • Private firms—manufacturing, EPC, IPP, wind turbines, solar glass, hybrid projects.
  • Examples: Tata Power Solar, Suzlon, ReNew, AGEL, Borosil, Sterling and Wilson.
  • Finish with public–private complementarity and environmental purpose.

Q4. Define pollution and discuss its main impacts and remedies.

Model structure:

  • Precise definition and natural/human sources.
  • Pollutants: PM, CO, NO₂, SO₂, ozone, CFCs.
  • Effects on health, ecosystem, climate/global warming, acid rain, ozone layer, and wildlife.
  • Taj Mahal/coal-worker/plastic-ingestion examples.
  • Remedies sector-wise: clean technology, filters, renewable energy, clean vehicles, green buildings, cogeneration, distributed solar, recycling, anaerobic digestion, biogas.
  • Conclude that the issue affects all living beings and needs coordinated action.

Q5. Explain the seven types of pollution.

For each type write definition → causes → effects → example/control:

  1. air;
  2. water;
  3. soil/land;
  4. noise;
  5. thermal;
  6. radioactive; and
  7. light.

Use a comparative table first, then expand. Mention plastic as a cross-cutting land/water issue.

Q6. Explain social sustainability, its benefits, and methods of achievement.

Model structure:

  1. Define it and connect it to sustainable development and ESG’s “S.”
  2. Identify people affected: employees, value-chain workers, customers, communities.
  3. Describe sustainable communities: diverse, connected, democratic, liveable, good quality of life.
  4. Explain physical plus social design and citizen engagement.
  5. Give business benefits: markets, partners, innovation, reputation, morale, risk, conflict.
  6. Internal action: fair labour, equal pay, conditions, association, anti-harassment, safety, inclusion.
  7. External action: good-neighbour conduct, community engagement, education, healthcare, responsible influence.
  8. Link ISO 26000/CSR/SDGs and conclude with proactive impact management.

Q7. “Renewable energy is necessary but not sufficient for sustainability.” Discuss.

  • Renewable energy lowers fossil-fuel depletion and operating pollution.
  • But projects may still affect land, workers, suppliers, and communities.
  • Apply environmental controls plus stakeholder mapping, fair labour, safety, inclusion, consultation, and community wellbeing.
  • Use ESG: environmental performance, social responsibility, and responsible governance together.
  • A socially sustainable solar project is an ideal applied example.

7.4 Common confusions and corrections

Confusion Correct exam understanding
PV produces AC directly Cells initially produce DC; an inverter supplies AC.
Charge controller and inverter do the same job Controller protects/regulates battery charging; inverter converts DC to AC.
On-grid and hybrid are identical Hybrid adds battery storage; a basic on-grid system need not have it.
Mono is cheaper because it is one crystal Mono is generally more efficient but costlier; poly is more affordable.
Ozone is always beneficial Stratospheric ozone protects from UV; ground-level ozone is an air pollutant/smog component.
Air pollution affects only lungs It can affect heart, brain, kidneys, liver, other organs, climate, wildlife, and habitats.
Soil and land pollution must always be separate The lecture uses the terms interchangeably, though broader treatments may distinguish them.
Thermal pollution only means hot water It can be an abnormal increase or decrease in water temperature.
Pollution comes only from people Natural sources exist, but the lectures emphasize human industry/transport/waste as major sources.
Social sustainability means charity alone It covers impacts, fair work, safety, inclusion, participation, community relations, physical/social design, and long-term wellbeing.
Stakeholders are only shareholders Stakeholders include employees, managers, shareholders, value-chain workers, customers, communities, government, competitors, and partners.
Legal compliance completes social sustainability Compliance is the baseline; proactive wellbeing and impact management go further.
ISO 26000 is a certifiable ISO system It is a guidance standard on social responsibility.
Company capacities/dates in the lecture are current Several are source-dated or garbled; label them “as stated in the lecture” unless independently verified.
