Exam-Oriented Notes: Green Marketing, Public Participation, BIS and ISO Certification, ISO 9000, ISO 14000, and ISO 26000

Central idea of the week: Organizations must translate awareness of environmental and social problems into action. Green marketing communicates genuine environmental value; public participation brings affected interests into decisions; and standards, management systems, audits, and certification help organizations plan, implement, verify, improve, and build trust.

1. Week 10 at a glance

The week connects six main topics:

  1. Green marketing
  2. Public participation in environmental decision-making
  3. BIS and ISO certification
  4. ISO 9000 family for quality management
  5. ISO 14000 family for environmental management
  6. ISO 26000 guidance for social responsibility

The instructor repeatedly links these topics to the three broad CSR domains:

  • Ethics: doing what is right and behaving responsibly.
  • Society: philanthropy, stakeholder welfare, community development, human rights, labour practices, and consumer interests.
  • Environment: conservation, pollution prevention, sustainable resource use, and reduction of negative environmental impact.

Integrated sustainability logic

Identify environmental/social impacts → select the suitable framework → implement genuine improvements → monitor and audit → obtain independent assurance where applicable → communicate honestly → engage stakeholders → continually improve.

Important instructor emphasis: sustainability is not equivalent only to environmental sustainability. It covers all stakeholders and therefore includes quality, social responsibility, society, government, employees, customers, suppliers, and the environment.

2. Green marketing

2.1 Definition

Green marketing is a category of marketing in which products and services are promoted on the basis of their environmental benefits. It is associated with marketing eco-friendly products, encouraging sustainable practices, and promoting responsible consumption.

The live-session refinement is important: green marketing is not merely projecting a company as “green,” attaching a green label, or using environmental language in advertising. The product and/or the processes behind it must create genuine environmental value. Marketing communicates and reinforces that value.

2.2 Why it is needed

Human activities are negatively affecting the environment through pollution, deforestation, marine pollution, resource depletion, plastic waste, and other harms. Environmental conservation is therefore a “need of the hour.” Green marketing supports:

  • Environmental protection and conservation
  • Sustainable development
  • Conservation of natural resources
  • Reduction of negative environmental impacts
  • Eco-friendly product choices
  • Responsible consumption and purchasing
  • Ethical and environmental value creation in society

The instructor describes this as a movement from promotion to protection and then to sustaining the improvement.

2.3 Shared responsibility of business and consumers

Green initiatives cannot succeed through business action alone:

  • Businesses must offer products and use processes that reduce environmental harm.
  • Consumers must understand the environmental situation, choose responsible alternatives, and support green initiatives.
  • Marketing can create awareness, but awareness without action is insufficient.

Instructor’s closing message: Awareness plus action creates a better future; awareness alone is not enough.

2.4 Benefits of green marketing

Environmental and social benefits

  • Promotes sustainability and sustainable development.
  • Conserves resources.
  • Reduces pollution and environmental damage.
  • Reduces plastic and other harmful waste.
  • Encourages responsible consumption.
  • Supports a cleaner, healthier environment.
  • Can improve societal welfare by reducing exposure to toxic or hazardous materials.

Business benefits

  • Improves public and brand image.
  • Increases goodwill, credibility, and reputation.
  • Builds customer trust and loyalty.
  • Enhances brand recognition and brand perception.
  • Differentiates a firm in a highly competitive market.
  • Creates competitive advantage.
  • Supports market expansion, including possible foreign markets/clients.
  • Can attract collaborations and new business opportunities.
  • May increase profits and repeat purchases.
  • Can reduce costs through resource efficiency.
  • Supports regulatory compliance.
  • Reduces regulatory, operational, resource, and reputational risks.

The business logic is: real green improvement → responsible brand image → trust → customer preference and loyalty → market expansion and long-term revenue.

2.5 Core example: reusable cloth shopping bags

Supermarkets increasingly replace single-use plastic bags with reusable cloth bags.

  • Cloth bags are reusable and eco-friendly.
  • They provide a direct substitute for plastic bags.
  • They reduce plastic consumption and waste.
  • They encourage customers to participate in sustainability.
  • The action is visible and easy for consumers to understand.

Plastic is particularly problematic because plastic entering oceans harms aquatic organisms and produces long-lasting pollution.

2.6 Credible green marketing versus greenwashing

Credible green marketing Greenwashing
Based on actual environmental improvement Creates a green image without meaningful operational change
Uses specific, evidence-based claims Uses vague or exaggerated claims
Aligns product, production, supply chain, operations, and marketing Treats advertising or labelling as sufficient
Measures and reports results transparently Provides little or no proof
Acknowledges limitations and progress honestly Hides adverse impacts or overstates benefits

Weak claim: “This is an eco-friendly product.”

Stronger claim: “The packaging uses recyclable material and has reduced plastic use by a measured amount.”

Green labelling can carry a useful message, but the label must be substantiated by green materials, safe production, waste reduction, lower emissions, or other genuine benefits.

2.7 Product life-cycle and supply-chain view

Environmental impact should be considered across the product life cycle:

  1. Raw-material extraction
  2. Manufacturing
  3. Distribution
  4. Product use
  5. Disposal, reuse, or recycling

Practical measures include recycled or renewable materials, cleaner production, renewable energy, lower energy and water use, reduced waste, sustainable procurement, supplier assessment, recyclable design, reuse, and end-of-life recovery.

2.8 Solar energy illustration

Marketing solar panels can encourage sustainable consumption because solar power:

  • Produces cleaner energy with fewer emissions.
  • May reduce dependence on conventional electricity purchases.
  • Can reduce pressure for additional conventional power infrastructure.
  • Avoids some environmental impacts associated with other energy projects; even large dams, though a cleaner energy source in one sense, can place pressure on land and ecosystems.

The benefits must be communicated so that ethical and environmental value is created in society.

2.9 Wipro green-computing illustration

The live session refers to Wipro’s environmentally friendly desktops/laptops (described as a circa-2017 example) and RoHS-compliant technology.

  • RoHS compliance limits hazardous substances in electrical/electronic products.
  • Safer materials reduce the harm caused by electronic waste.
  • Ordinary e-waste may release toxic material into soil and cannot be processed like natural/biodegradable waste.
  • Green design connects technology with sustainability.
  • A responsible technology image strengthens brand image and consumer trust.
  • Consumers may feel that buying the product also supports society and the environment.

The instructor notes uncertainty about the exact expansion/technical detail of RoHS during the lecture; the exam-worthy point is its role in limiting hazardous substances.

2.10 Green steel

Green steel refers to steel produced using low- or no-emission inputs rather than conventional high-emission inputs such as coal. Hydrogen is presented as an example of a cleaner input.

Rationale

  • Conventional steelmaking and blast-furnace processes use carbon-intensive materials and release substantial carbon dioxide.
  • Replacing these inputs with cleaner energy or hydrogen can reduce emissions and support a low-carbon economy and net-zero ambitions.

Benefits

  • Lower carbon emissions
  • Support for decarbonization and net-zero goals
  • Cleaner production and more sustainable infrastructure
  • Investment and innovation in green resources

Caveats and challenges

  • High cost
  • Limited availability of clean inputs
  • Technical and infrastructure constraints
  • Difficulty of converting coal-enabled production to hydrogen-enabled processes
  • Uncertain feasibility of full integration at scale
  • Need for collaboration between industry and government

The instructor treats green steel as an extended example and cautions that feasibility remains under debate.

3. GreenLeaf Foods case study

3.1 Case facts

GreenLeaf Foods is a small local snack manufacturer. Its marketing manager, Maria, observes growing customer interest in environmental choices and considers green marketing to improve the firm’s image and expand its market.

The team evaluates three initiatives:

Initiative Timing Reasoning
Sustainable packaging Within six months Visible, affordable, reduces packaging/plastic waste
Reusable cloth shopping bags Within six months Simple substitute for plastic; customers directly participate
Solar-powered operations Deferred Attractive long-term environmental option, but initial cost and technological constraints are high

3.2 Expected benefits

  • Enhanced brand image
  • Greater customer loyalty and repeat purchases
  • Competitive advantage
  • Cost savings through resource efficiency
  • Regulatory compliance
  • Market expansion
  • Reduced environmental, regulatory, and reputational risk

3.3 Challenges

  • High initial costs
  • Consumer scepticism
  • Risk of greenwashing if claims are not genuine
  • Limited consumer awareness
  • Technological constraints
  • Need to coordinate marketing, operations, and finance

3.4 Measurement

Success should be assessed through:

  • Changes in brand perception/customer feedback
  • Repeat purchases and retention
  • Reduction in operating or material costs
  • Compliance with regulatory steps
  • Measurable reductions in waste or environmental impact

3.5 Recommended phased strategy

  1. Establish a baseline for current packaging, plastic use, energy use, and costs.
  2. Introduce sustainable packaging and reusable bags.
  3. Set measurable environmental and business targets.
  4. Coordinate marketing claims with operational evidence and financial feasibility.
  5. Communicate precisely and transparently.
  6. Monitor brand response, cost savings, compliance, and environmental outcomes.
  7. Reassess solar investment when cost and technology conditions improve.

Case conclusion: GreenLeaf’s phased plan is strategically sound because it begins with feasible, visible improvements but does not abandon the longer-term renewable-energy goal. Its credibility depends on evidence and alignment between what the business does and what it claims.

4. Public participation in environmental decision-making

4.1 Meaning and democratic basis

Public participation means involving citizens and affected groups in environmental plans, programmes, policies, legislation, project decisions, and environmental-clearance processes.

Citizens have:

  • A right to receive environmental information.
  • A right to participate in preparing plans, programmes, policies, and legislation that may affect the environment.
  • A reasonable and effective opportunity to present their case when their interests are affected or likely to be affected.

It is a democratic approach: a decision should not be taken without hearing affected members of the public.

4.2 Link with environmental clearance

Public hearing is an important stage in the environmental-clearance protocol. It allows affected communities to understand the proposal, identify likely problems, communicate local knowledge, and present objections or suggestions before approval.

Project developers/authorities should explain:

  • The purpose and nature of the project
  • Likely air, water, land, ecosystem, and social impacts
  • Resource requirements such as land, water, and energy
  • Pollution-control and waste-management plans
  • Proposed mitigation and environmental-protection measures

4.3 Methods of participation

  • Education and awareness
  • Dissemination of environmental information
  • Public hearings
  • Written/oral submissions
  • Consultation through government agencies
  • Media discussion through press, radio, and television

The media highlights pollution and local environmental problems and helps create environmental consciousness among the public.

4.4 Why public knowledge matters

Environmental decisions often concern natural resources and waste, and may affect fragile or sensitive locations differently. The live session uses heavy rain as an illustration: fragile land and sensitive regions respond differently to stronger natural events. Local communities may know existing environmental conditions and vulnerabilities that formal decision-makers overlook.

4.5 Benefits

  • Helps decision-makers identify public-interest concerns.
  • Broadens the perspective used to formulate policy.
  • Reveals prevailing local conditions and practical problems.
  • Enables corrective actions and better solutions.
  • Streamlines activities and energizes environmental decision-making agencies.
  • Improves administration by requiring agencies to consider all dimensions objectively.
  • Produces more reasonable, effective, and higher-quality decisions.
  • Gives all affected or potentially affected interests an opportunity to be heard.
  • Builds confidence, acceptance, and trust between society and the company/government.
  • Supports cleaner human environments and lower pollution.
  • Brings inadequacies in legislation and regulation to light.
  • Encourages shared responsibility between companies, government, agencies, citizens, and society.

4.6 Participation as social audit

The live session characterizes public participation as an implied or indirect social audit. When the public is engaged, decisions are exposed to outside scrutiny, making decision-makers more accountable. Participation therefore supplies information and also performs an oversight function.

4.7 Practical process

Disclose relevant information → educate and invite affected groups → hear concerns and evidence → document issues → assess alternatives and mitigation → explain how input influenced the decision → monitor and provide feedback.

Participation is not meaningful if it is conducted after the decision has effectively been finalized or if information is withheld.

5. Standards and certification: foundational concepts

5.1 Certification

Certification is assurance by an independent certification body that a product, service, system, or organization meets the requirements of a defined standard.

It provides:

  • External verification
  • Evidence of conformity/compliance
  • Customer confidence
  • Market credibility
  • Quality and process assurance
  • A basis for trust and long-term business relationships

5.2 Quality

The instructor stresses that quality is a perception and may vary between people. Nevertheless, customers place high priority on receiving reliable, consistent products. Standards create a documented and tested basis for judging quality and reduce doubts about credibility.

5.3 Standardization and certification as sustainability tools

They help an organization:

  • Monitor, review, and self-assess sustainability efforts.
  • Establish systematic processes.
  • Identify deficiencies.
  • Document and maintain systems.
  • Audit performance.
  • Correct problems and continually improve.
  • Demonstrate responsible performance to stakeholders.

5.4 Broad five-step certification framework

The live session gives a general framework; the exact number and details may vary by standard and organization.

  1. Clarify the need: Understand why the organization wants certification/standardization and which standard is relevant.
  2. Conduct gap analysis: Compare present systems with the standard; identify what exists and what is deficient.
  3. Implement corrections: Establish processes, documents, controls, and activities needed to close the gaps.
  4. Obtain third-party verification/audit: An external body independently assesses conformity.
  5. Submit documents and complete authority verification: If requirements are met, the certificate or quality mark is issued.

An abbreviated quiz sequence is: apply to certification body → independent testing/assessment → certificate issued if compliant.

5.5 Key glossary

Term Meaning and significance
Certification body Independent third party that assesses and certifies an organization’s management system/product against a standard.
Certification audit First external audit of the QMS by the certification body.
Compliance/conformance Adhering to applicable norms, requirements, or standards; e.g., conformity with ISO 9001.
Committee/technical committee Expert group that develops standards for a sector or industry.
Committee chair Neutral leader with overall responsibility who ensures experts’ and delegates’ views are heard.
Consensus General agreement without sustained opposition; aims at harmony among participating interests. The transcript often renders this as “consonance.”
Code of ethics and conduct Principles and guidelines governing people involved in ISO work within an international framework.
ISO Central Secretariat ISO’s central office in Geneva, supporting work such as coordination, IT, and editing.
Planning First stage of a systematic certification/improvement process; establishes strategy and direction.
Continual/continuous improvement Ongoing effort to improve operations, practices, and quality systems through planning, action, tracking, and correction.
Kaizen Japanese philosophy of continuous improvement: small changes can cumulatively transform an organization. Employees must be willing to accept change.
Imperfection/nonconformity Defect or deviation of a system/quality characteristic from intended results or requirements.
Internal audit Intensive review of internal processes and systems by people appointed by the organization; distinct from external certification audit. Financial and energy audits focus on their respective domains.
QMS Formal set of policies, processes, procedures, responsibilities, and documents governing how core business and quality activities are conducted.
Quality management representative Management-appointed person, backed by senior authority, responsible for the QMS from start to finish.
Benchmarking Comparing with best-in-class practices or technology to identify superior methods and performance.
Reliability How consistently and for how long a product or machine performs its intended function.

6. BIS and ISO: comparison

Dimension BIS ISO
Full name Bureau of Indian Standards International Organization for Standardization
Scope India International/global
Nature National standards body of India, established under the BIS Act, 2016 Independent, non-governmental international standards organization composed of national standards bodies
Main coverage Standards for Indian products and services International standards across industries and sectors
Certification Mandatory for certain products in India Generally voluntary and internationally recognized
Core value Standardization, quality assurance, safety, and reliability Global consistency, credible management practices, innovation, solutions to global challenges, stakeholder trust

6.1 BIS activities

  • Formulation of standards
  • Product certification
  • Laboratory testing
  • Hallmarking
  • Hallmarking schemes
  • Sale/publication of Indian standards
  • Training services
  • On-site inspection and sample collection

6.2 BIS benefits

  • Ensures uncompromised/high quality standards.
  • Gives authenticity to products in the market.
  • Builds customer confidence and trust.
  • Provides safe and reliable goods.
  • Minimizes consumer health hazards.
  • Supports national economic welfare.
  • Promotes exports and import substitutes.
  • Minimizes environmental risks by prohibiting certain hazardous materials and chemicals.
  • Uses rigorous lab testing and inspection to standardize quality.

The testing process may be time-consuming, but it adds value. During on-site inspection, officers may take samples for laboratory testing; certification follows successful compliance with the procedure.

6.3 ISO: nature and reach

ISO brings experts together through member national standards bodies to share knowledge and develop voluntary, consensus-based, market-relevant international standards that support innovation and address global challenges.

The transcript gives differing approximate figures in different sections—169 national bodies; 23,000 standards in 164 countries; and a glossary description of around 140 countries. Treat these as lecture-era approximations rather than a conceptual distinction. The examinable idea is that ISO is a large international federation/network.

ISO applies across agriculture, food safety, healthcare, pharmaceuticals, manufacturing, production and operations management, occupational health and safety, risk management, and technology.

6.4 General benefits of ISO certification

  • Standardized and evaluated processes
  • Increased efficiency
  • Lower costs and risks
  • Better visibility and transparency
  • Faster detection and correction of problems
  • Easier training of new employees through documented procedures
  • Greater customer satisfaction
  • Better public perception, credibility, and goodwill
  • Confidence among customers, clients, shareholders, employees, government, and business partners
  • Access to larger companies, collaborations, ventures, and international markets
  • Improved safety, security, and industrial welfare

The instructor’s trust chain is: quality → consistency → customer trust → repeated preference → long-term customers → revenue.

7. ISO 9000 family: quality management

7.1 Definition and purpose

The ISO 9000 family comprises international standards on quality management and quality assurance. It helps organizations document quality-system elements and maintain an efficient QMS.

It helps organizations:

  • Deliver consistent quality in products, services, processes, and performance.
  • Satisfy customers.
  • Meet regulatory requirements.
  • Continually improve.
  • Reduce errors and waste.
  • Increase stakeholder satisfaction.

It is a base or first step for a QMS, is not limited to a specific industry, and applies to organizations of every size and type.

7.2 Importance and benefits

  • Makes the business more marketable.
  • Improves image, goodwill, and brand reputation.
  • Opens new markets and business opportunities.
  • Helps manage growth efficiently.
  • Institutionalizes continuous improvement.
  • Reduces production errors and waste.
  • Improves product/service and process efficiency.
  • Improves customer and stakeholder satisfaction.
  • Provides documented consistency and trust.

Operations-management link: firms convert inputs into outputs using resources; quality management improves this conversion, controls errors, and reduces waste.

7.3 Important standards in the family

Standard Main role Exam point
ISO 9001:2015 Requirements/framework for a Quality Management System Customer satisfaction, consistent quality, and a PDCA-based management framework; described as a “gold standard” for benchmarking QMS.
ISO 9004:2018 Guidance for sustained success and improvement Goes beyond basic QMS requirements; includes self-assessment to identify areas needing improvement and close gaps.
ISO 19011:2018 Guidelines for auditing management systems Covers internal and external audit guidance; audit results can be integrated into business planning.

The transcript reports that ISO 9001 had certified more than one million organizations in over 170 countries; the exam point is its very wide international adoption.

7.4 PDCA/Kaizen

ISO 9001 is linked to the PDCA cycle:

  1. Plan: set objectives, standards, processes, responsibilities, and resources.
  2. Do: implement the planned processes.
  3. Check: measure results, audit, and compare performance with objectives/standards.
  4. Act: correct deficiencies and improve the system.

The instructor also mentions the variant Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA). Both express continual improvement. Kaizen can be used in manufacturing and other organizations, and emphasizes ongoing, often small improvements.

7.5 ISO 9004 and sustained success

Obtaining a high level of quality and sustaining it are different tasks. The instructor uses a rank/trophy analogy: achieving first rank requires effort, but retaining it requires continuing improvement. ISO 9004 supports self-assessment, identifies gaps, and helps an organization sustain and improve existing success.

7.6 ISO 19011 and audit

Auditing is a systematic assessment of current conditions and management processes. It culminates in findings/reporting, supports both internal and external audit activity, and enables an organization to incorporate evidence into future business planning.

8. ISO 14000 family: environmental management

8.1 Definition and purpose

The ISO 14000 family focuses specifically on environmental management. It provides a framework for an environmentally conscious management system and guides organizations in establishing and achieving environmentally friendly objectives.

The family was introduced in 1996 and revised in 2015. It is applicable to any organization regardless of activity or sector.

8.2 Why it matters

Organizations must manage efficiently while minimizing environmental effects. The standards respond to increasing pollution, deforestation, marine pollution, emissions, carbon footprint, waste, and other human-caused harm.

They can assure management, employees, customers, shareholders, clients, and other stakeholders that the organization is managing its environmental responsibilities.

8.3 Environmental Management System (EMS)

An EMS is the structured framework through which an organization identifies environmental aspects, manages risks, sets objectives, operates controls, measures performance, audits compliance, corrects problems, and improves continually.

ISO 14001 sets certifiable criteria/requirements for an EMS. ISO 14004 provides supporting guidance for establishing and improving an EMS.

8.4 Standards mentioned in the transcript

Area Standards mentioned Function
EMS criteria/requirements ISO 14001 (the lecture also groups 14002) Framework and criteria for environmental management
EMS guidance ISO 14004 Guidance/support for establishing and improving an EMS
Environmental auditing ISO 14015, ISO 14016, ISO 14017 (as listed in the lecture) Assessment/audit-related environmental work
Environmental labels ISO 14020, ISO 14021, ISO 14024 Eco-labelling and environmental declarations
Environmental performance evaluation ISO 14030, ISO 14031 Evaluating environmental performance

For exams, prioritize the roles explicitly emphasized: 14001 = EMS criteria; 14004 = EMS guidance; 14020-series = eco-labelling; 14030/14031 = environmental-performance evaluation.

8.5 Eco-labelling

An eco-label identifies products/services with environmental benefits. It is described as voluntary. A label is useful for communicating environmental performance, but certification and process measures—not a mark alone—must substantiate the claim.

8.6 Environmental audit

Environmental auditing evaluates environmental performance and compliance.

Typical sequence:

  1. Review activities and gather environmental data.
  2. Compare actual performance against requirements and objectives.
  3. Identify nonconformities, risks, and improvement opportunities.
  4. Recommend and implement corrective action.
  5. Monitor continual improvement.

8.7 Benefits of ISO 14000

  • Improves company image, goodwill, and credibility.
  • Shows compliance with environmental norms.
  • Helps comply with laws, rules, and regulations.
  • Reduces emissions, carbon footprint, pollution, and environmental risk.
  • Improves cost control and resource efficiency.
  • Enables faster process improvement and more successful implementation of change.
  • Satisfies customers, employees, shareholders, and other stakeholders.
  • May reduce employee turnover and improve retention when employees value the company’s environmental initiatives.
  • Provides systematic protection and conservation of the environment.

9. ISO 26000: social responsibility guidance

9.1 Purpose

ISO 26000 focuses on social responsibility/social sustainability. It assists organizations committed to operating in a socially responsible manner and treats respect for society and the environment as both a critical success factor and the right thing to do.

It helps assess and strengthen an organization’s commitment to sustainability and translate principles into action. It shares internationally relevant best practices.

9.2 Crucial distinction

ISO 26000 provides guidance rather than certifiable requirements. Unlike ISO 9001 and ISO 14001, it is generally not a certification standard. This is one of the most likely exam traps.

9.3 Development and applicability

  • Launched in 2010 after approximately five years of negotiations.
  • Developed through global stakeholder participation.
  • Participants included governments, NGOs, industries, consumer groups, labour organizations, and other communities.
  • Represents international consensus among varied interests.
  • Applies to all types of organizations, regardless of activity, size, or location.

9.4 Core subjects/focus areas

  • Human rights
  • Labour practices
  • Environment
  • Fair operating practices
  • Consumer issues
  • Community involvement and development

These subjects express the connection between business and society: businesses employ people, serve customers, operate in communities, affect the environment, and must consider all these interests.

9.5 Seven principles

  1. Accountability — accept responsibility for decisions and impacts.
  2. Transparency — provide clear, open, understandable information.
  3. Ethical behaviour — act honestly and do what is right.
  4. Respect for stakeholder interests — identify, hear, and balance legitimate interests.
  5. Respect for the rule of law — obey applicable laws, rules, protocols, and guidelines.
  6. Respect for international norms of behaviour — follow relevant international expectations.
  7. Respect for human rights — recognize and protect the rights and dignity of people.

9.6 Operational value

ISO 26000 helps organizations:

  • Make social responsibility operational rather than merely aspirational.
  • Identify and engage stakeholders.
  • Understand different stakeholder viewpoints and develop consensus.
  • Improve cooperation and collaboration.
  • Enhance credibility of social-responsibility reporting.
  • Increase customer/stakeholder confidence and satisfaction.
  • Promote community development and fair labour practices.

9.7 Fair labour practices and hygiene factors

The live session links fair labour practice to workplace “hygiene factors.” Basic facilities and fair conditions—such as adequate washrooms, reasonable leisure/rest time, and fair contractual obligations—may not alone motivate employees, but their absence can strongly demotivate them and harm organizational performance. Social responsibility standards encourage organizations to examine these conditions.

9.8 Separate frameworks, not a package

In response to a class question, the instructor indicates that different standards are generally pursued separately according to organizational needs rather than obtained as one combined package. This was expressed as the instructor’s understanding, so frame it cautiously and always check the applicable certification scheme.

10. Comparing the major ISO frameworks

Framework Primary focus Typical problem addressed Nature
ISO 9000 / ISO 9001 Quality management, consistency, customer satisfaction Complaints, product/service inconsistency, process errors ISO 9001 contains certifiable QMS requirements
ISO 14000 / ISO 14001 Environmental management Pollution, waste, emissions, resource use, environmental risks ISO 14001 contains certifiable EMS requirements
ISO 26000 Social responsibility Ethics, stakeholder impact, labour, human rights, consumers, community Guidance, not certification requirements

Selection rule

  • Quality issue → ISO 9001/QMS
  • Environmental issue → ISO 14001/EMS
  • Social-responsibility issue → ISO 26000 guidance

The frameworks can support one another, but they are not interchangeable.

11. Instructor emphasis, caveats, and classroom applications

High-emphasis points

  • Green marketing is more than green labelling.
  • A green claim must be supported by green products and/or processes.
  • Public participation is a democratic right and an indirect social audit.
  • Certification begins with clarity about organizational need.
  • Gap analysis precedes corrective implementation and external verification.
  • Quality creates consistency; consistency creates trust.
  • ISO 9000 concerns QMS, ISO 14000 environmental management, and ISO 26000 social responsibility.
  • Sustainability includes all stakeholders, not only the environment.
  • ISO 26000 is guidance, not a certifiable requirement standard.
  • Awareness must lead to action.

Review of preceding-week concepts mentioned in the live session

The instructor briefly revises earlier material to connect Week 10 with the broader course:

  • The greenhouse effect is natural, but human/anthropogenic emissions intensify it by increasing atmospheric greenhouse gases.
  • This intensified effect increases Earth’s average temperature, producing global warming.
  • Climate is not the same as day-to-day weather; climate change requires patterns observed over a substantial period.
  • El Niño is cited as an example of a phenomenon known earlier but treated with growing concern in recent decades.
  • Carbon credits are financial instruments; the best classroom answer given is that a credit offsets one tonne of carbon dioxide, although energy accounting may also be related.

These are recap points, not the main Week 10 syllabus, but may appear in integrative questions.

12. Exam preparation

12.1 Likely long-answer questions and model-answer points

Q1. Define green marketing and explain its significance and benefits.

Model points:

  • Define promotion based on environmental benefit.
  • Clarify that genuine product/process improvement is necessary.
  • Link to eco-friendly products, sustainability, conservation, and responsible consumption.
  • Explain business benefits: image, loyalty, differentiation, credibility, markets, profit, compliance, and risk reduction.
  • Explain environmental/social benefits.
  • Use reusable cloth bags and/or solar products as examples.
  • End with the danger of greenwashing and the need for evidence.

Q2. Distinguish green marketing from greenwashing using a case.

Model points:

  • Define both terms.
  • Compare genuine improvement, evidence, transparency, life-cycle alignment, and operational reality.
  • Apply GreenLeaf Foods: sustainable packaging and cloth bags within six months, solar later, measurable outcomes, honest claims.
  • Explain why vague “eco-friendly” advertising without change is greenwashing.

Q3. Explain public participation in environmental decision-making and its benefits.

Model points:

  • Citizen rights to information and participation.
  • Democratic basis and environmental-clearance/public-hearing connection.
  • Methods: information, education, hearing, submissions, media.
  • Benefits: local knowledge, broader perspective, objective decisions, legislative gaps, acceptance, trust, quality, and cleaner environment.
  • Explain participation as an indirect social audit.
  • State that affected and potentially affected interests need a reasonable opportunity to present their case.

Q4. Compare BIS and ISO.

Model points:

  • BIS is India’s national standards body under the BIS Act, 2016; ISO is international and non-governmental.
  • BIS focuses on Indian products/services; ISO covers global sectors.
  • BIS certification is mandatory for certain products; ISO certification is generally voluntary and internationally recognized.
  • BIS activities: standards, product certification, lab testing, hallmarking, training.
  • Shared benefits: quality, safety, reliability, confidence, reputation, lower risk.

Q5. Describe the broad certification process.

Model points:

  1. Clarify need and choose the standard.
  2. Conduct gap analysis.
  3. Implement missing processes/controls and document systems.
  4. Obtain independent third-party audit/testing.
  5. Submit evidence; receive certificate/mark after verification.

Add the caveat that detailed procedures vary by standard, product, and organization.

Q6. Explain the ISO 9000 family and distinguish ISO 9001, ISO 9004, and ISO 19011.

Model points:

  • Family concerns QMS and quality assurance for all organizations.
  • Goals: consistency, customer satisfaction, regulation, continuous improvement.
  • ISO 9001: QMS requirements/framework, PDCA, customer satisfaction.
  • ISO 9004: guidance and self-assessment for sustained success.
  • ISO 19011: auditing-management-system guidance.
  • Benefits: reduced errors/waste, new markets, reputation, trust.

Q7. Explain PDCA and its relation to continuous improvement.

Model points:

  • Define Plan, Do, Check, Act.
  • Link to Kaizen and small continuous changes.
  • Show how audit findings and corrective action restart the cycle.
  • Mention PDSA as a variant noted in the lecture.

Q8. Explain ISO 14000 and the role of an EMS.

Model points:

  • Environmental-management family, introduced in 1996/revised 2015.
  • EMS: identify aspects/risks, set objectives, control operations, monitor, audit, correct, improve.
  • ISO 14001 = criteria/requirements; ISO 14004 = guidance.
  • Mention eco-labelling, environmental audit, and performance evaluation.
  • Benefits: compliance, lower emissions/risks/costs, reputation, stakeholder satisfaction.

Q9. Explain ISO 26000 and its seven principles.

Model points:

  • Social-responsibility guidance, launched in 2010 after five years of multi-stakeholder negotiation.
  • Applicable to organizations of all sizes/types/locations.
  • Guidance rather than requirements; not generally certifiable.
  • List seven principles accurately.
  • Discuss human rights, labour, environment, fair operations, consumers, and community.
  • Apply fair-labour/hygiene-factor example.

Q10. Compare ISO 9001, ISO 14001, and ISO 26000.

Model points:

  • Quality/QMS versus environment/EMS versus social-responsibility guidance.
  • Give a business problem suited to each.
  • State certification distinction clearly.
  • Conclude that sustainability requires integration without confusing their roles.

12.4 Common confusions and corrections

Confusion Correct understanding
Green marketing means using a green label. It requires genuine environmental benefit in product/process/operations; a label only communicates evidence.
Any environmental claim is green marketing. Unsupported or misleading claims are greenwashing.
Sustainability means only environmental protection. It includes environmental, social, ethical, quality, and stakeholder dimensions.
Public consultation is merely a formality. It is a citizen right, decision input, accountability mechanism, and indirect social audit.
BIS and ISO are the same. BIS is India’s national body; ISO is an international standards organization.
All ISO standards certify products. Many relate to management systems; ISO 26000 is guidance and is not normally certifiable.
ISO 9000 and ISO 9001 are identical labels. ISO 9000 is a family/foundation; ISO 9001 contains QMS requirements used for certification.
ISO 14000 and ISO 14001 are interchangeable. ISO 14000 is the environmental family; ISO 14001 contains EMS requirements/criteria.
ISO 9004 is the audit standard. ISO 9004 addresses sustained success; ISO 19011 gives auditing guidelines.
Internal audit and certification audit are the same. Internal audit is organization-led; certification audit is independent third-party assessment.
Certification is achieved immediately after application. Need clarification, gap analysis, implementation, audit/testing, documentation, and verification precede award.
Carbon credit is a right to emit one tonne of oxygen. The classroom’s best answer is an instrument associated with offsetting one tonne of CO₂.

12.5 One-page memory map

  • Market the environmental value: green marketing; avoid greenwashing.
  • Hear affected society: public participation/public hearing; indirect social audit.
  • Assure Indian product standards: BIS.
  • Build global management credibility: ISO.
  • Manage quality: ISO 9001; sustain success: ISO 9004; audit: ISO 19011.
  • Manage environmental impact: ISO 14001; guidance: ISO 14004; labels/audits/performance evaluation within ISO 14000.
  • Guide social responsibility: ISO 26000 and its seven principles.
  • Improve continuously: Plan → Do → Check → Act.
  • Build trust: quality → consistency → trust → loyalty → long-term value.
  • Final lesson: standards, participation, and communication matter only when they produce responsible action.
