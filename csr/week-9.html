1. Week overview

Week 9 connects five core areas:

  1. Global warming and its impacts
  2. The greenhouse effect and greenhouse gases
  3. Climate change
  4. Carbon credits, carbon offsets, carbon footprints and carbon neutrality
  5. Environmental auditing and related glossary terms

The live session adds four important extensions: carbon markets as financial incentives, the difference between climate and weather, Scope 4/avoided emissions, and the carbon handprint. The unifying idea is that human activity has disturbed Earth’s natural heating–cooling balance, and environmental governance must measure impacts, reduce emissions, protect natural sinks, compensate only unavoidable emissions, and continuously improve.

2. Global warming

2.1 Definition and meaning

Global warming is the long-term increase in the Earth’s average surface or near-surface air temperature. The recorded lesson describes it as the warming of the globe over roughly the past one to two centuries, with the pace increasing sharply during the last 100 years.

The live session supplies an essential qualification:

  • The Earth naturally heats and cools even without humans.
  • The present concern is the long-term average temperature increase caused or greatly intensified by unprecedented anthropogenic (human) activity, especially fossil-fuel combustion.
  • Human activity disrupts the natural equilibrium beyond the level that the Earth and its systems can comfortably absorb.

Population growth, industrialization and expanding energy demand have increased the use of coal, petroleum and natural gas. Their combustion releases contaminants and heat-trapping gases into the atmosphere. Global warming is therefore closely connected to the enhanced greenhouse effect and is a major cause or component of wider climate change.

2.2 Main causes stressed in the lessons

  • Fossil-fuel combustion: coal, petroleum/crude oil and natural gas are non-renewable energy sources used for electricity, industry and transport. Their use releases carbon dioxide and other pollutants such as sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and carbon monoxide.
  • Industrialization: factories and industrial processes release greenhouse gases and conventional air pollutants.
  • Transport: petrol- and diesel-powered vehicles are major emission sources.
  • Deforestation: cutting trees removes carbon sinks and may release stored carbon; reduced tree cover means less CO₂ removal through photosynthesis.
  • Agriculture: fertilizers contribute nitrous oxide; rice cultivation and other activities can produce methane.
  • Waste and landfills: accumulated waste releases carbon dioxide and methane.
  • Refrigerants: CFCs and related refrigerants contribute to atmospheric problems, including ozone depletion and warming.

2.3 Negative impacts of global warming

A. More frequent and severe weather

Higher temperatures worsen storms, heat waves, floods and droughts. A warmer atmosphere can collect, retain and release more water, changing rainfall patterns so that wet areas become wetter and dry areas become drier. This disturbs environmental balance and increases exposure to natural disasters.

B. Higher death rates and health emergencies

  • Heat raises the risk of sunstroke and other heat-related illness.
  • As temperature rises, deaths, emergency-room visits and hospital visits also rise.
  • Children and elderly people are especially vulnerable.
  • The instructor emphasized climate change as a major global health threat of the twenty-first century.

C. Dirtier air

  • Rising temperature worsens air pollution and ground-level ozone.
  • Smog (from smoke + fog) is intense air pollution that reduces visibility; automobiles, industry, agricultural fires and forest fires contribute to it.
  • Polluted air reduces access to clean, fresh air and can cause lung and pulmonary disease and even cancer.

D. Wildlife loss and habitat disruption

Global warming threatens humans, animals and plants. Rapid changes on land and in the sea may exceed the ability of species to adapt. Melting ice and glaciers disrupt polar habitats; the lesson uses penguins as an example. The result can be a higher wildlife extinction rate.

E. Ocean acidification

Excess atmospheric emissions put marine systems under pressure. A fall in ocean pH harms organisms such as corals, crabs and molluscs, whose survival depends on appropriate oxygen, salinity and chemical conditions.

F. Sea-level rise

  • Polar ice sheets, glaciers and icebergs are melting.
  • Ocean water also expands as it warms.
  • The Arctic was described as warming about twice as fast as elsewhere.
  • Rising seas cause coastal flooding and erosion and threaten low-lying coastal cities and countries with submergence.
  • The North and South Poles were described as the Earth’s “refrigerators”; loss of ice weakens this climatic function and damages local human, animal and plant habitats.

G. Climate change

Long-term global warming alters weather patterns and growing seasons, contributing to abrupt or persistent climatic shifts and greater risks from storms, floods, droughts, hurricanes, heavy downpours and winter storms.

3. Greenhouse effect and greenhouse gases

3.1 Greenhouse effect: core mechanism

The greenhouse effect is the process by which atmospheric gases absorb and re-radiate heat (infrared radiation), preventing all outgoing heat from escaping to space.

The physical greenhouse analogy is useful:

  • Sunlight enters a glasshouse and warms the plants, surfaces and air.
  • Heat becomes trapped inside and raises the internal temperature.
  • Likewise, incoming solar radiation reaches Earth; Earth later radiates heat outward, but greenhouse gases retain part of it.

The natural greenhouse effect is necessary: it insulates Earth and prevents it from becoming too cold for life. The problem is the enhanced greenhouse effect:

Human activity → more greenhouse gases → a thicker atmospheric “shield” → more infrared heat trapped → higher average temperature → climate impacts.

The instructor repeatedly emphasized that the greenhouse effect itself is natural; the dangerous part is the additional shield created by anthropogenic emissions. In the live-session multiple-choice discussion, the correct explanation was trapping of infrared radiation by gases, not Earth’s rotation, ocean absorption of sunlight or reflection by clouds.

3.2 Causes of the enhanced greenhouse effect

  • Burning fossil fuels for transport, electricity and industry
  • Deforestation and loss of carbon-removal capacity
  • Nitrogen fertilizers
  • Industrial waste and factory emissions
  • Landfills producing CO₂ and CH₄
  • Refrigerants and chemical solvents
  • Agricultural activity, including rice cultivation

3.3 Major greenhouse gases and their sources

Gas Formula Sources/examples highlighted Important point
Carbon dioxide CO₂ Fossil fuels, cement production, transport, industry, agriculture and deforestation; natural sources include volcanoes, hot springs, groundwater and glaciers Central long-lived gas in carbon accounting and the reference gas for CO₂e
Methane CH₄ Wetlands, volcanoes, oceans, animals/insects; landfills, fossil fuels, rice cultivation, agriculture and biogas The glossary states that methane absorbs 84 times more heat than CO₂ (as presented in the transcript); do not assume CO₂ is the only important gas
Nitrous oxide N₂O Industrial manufacturing, fossil-fuel combustion and breakdown/use of agricultural fertilizers Powerful greenhouse gas associated strongly with industry and agriculture
Water vapour H₂O Evaporation; atmospheric concentration rises as temperature rises Most abundant greenhouse gas and an important positive feedback, rather than the main initiating forcing discussed here
Ozone O₃ Stratospheric ozone occurs naturally; tropospheric/ground-level ozone is associated with pollution and human activity Stratospheric ozone protects against UV; ground-level ozone is a main component of smog
CFCs and related refrigerants e.g., chlorofluorocarbons; transcript also mentions CHClF₂-type refrigerant Refrigerators, air conditioners, industrial uses and solvents Contribute to warming and ozone-layer depletion; motivates eco-friendly refrigerants
Sulphur hexafluoride SF₆ Mentioned in the live-session exam question A factual exam point: high warming potential; climate analysis must not focus only on CO₂

The recorded lessons also mention sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and carbon monoxide in discussions of fossil-fuel pollution. In an exam, distinguish greenhouse gases from broader air pollutants rather than treating every pollutant as identical.

3.4 Water-vapour positive feedback

Water vapour behaves differently from CO₂:

Temperature rises → evaporation rises → atmospheric water vapour rises → more heat is trapped → further warming.

This is a positive feedback loop. A climate model that artificially holds water vapour constant will underestimate warming because it misses the additional heat trapping caused by warming-driven evaporation.

3.5 Effects discussed under the greenhouse effect

  • Global warming and consequent climate change
  • Ozone-layer depletion
  • Smog and air pollution
  • Acid rain and acidification of water bodies

Acidification pathway: atmospheric pollutants mix with rainwater → acid rain falls → contaminated runoff enters rivers and other water bodies → water becomes more acidic.

3.6 Ozone-layer distinction

  • The ozone layer lies in the stratosphere and protects Earth from harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation.
  • Depletion permits more UV radiation to reach the surface, increasing risks such as skin cancer and causing wider ecological/climatic harm.
  • CFC refrigerants are a major cause stressed in the lesson.
  • Common confusion: protective stratospheric ozone is not the same as harmful ground-level/tropospheric ozone in smog.

4. Climate, weather and climate change

4.1 Definitions

  • Weather: short-term, day-to-day atmospheric conditions at a location.
  • Climate: the characteristic conditions of a region over a long period, expressed through temperature, wind, precipitation and related patterns.
  • Climate change: a long-term shift in global or regional temperature and weather patterns.

The instructor’s memory aid was:

“Weather is what you see; climate is what you expect or experience.”

One rainy or dry day, month or even isolated year does not by itself prove climate change. The live lecture used roughly 30–50 years as the kind of persistent observation period needed to describe a climatic shift, while acknowledging that scientific definitions and boundaries are debated for scientific and political reasons.

4.2 Types of climate/regions mentioned

  • Dry climates and hot deserts with little rainfall
  • Temperate/coastal climates
  • Cold and polar regions
  • Ice-cap regions
  • Highlands, forests and mountain areas
  • Lowland and terrestrial regions

India was used to show that one country may contain several climatic zones, including deserts, cold/ice-cap areas and coastal regions.

4.3 Causes of climate change

Climate shifts can arise from:

  • Natural changes in solar activity
  • Large volcanic eruptions
  • Long-run natural Earth-system variation
  • Human activities, especially fossil-fuel combustion, factories, vehicles, deforestation, agriculture and other sources of greenhouse gases

The present problem is mainly the scale and speed of anthropogenic influence. Greenhouse gases act like a blanket around Earth, trapping heat and raising average temperature.

4.4 Major impacts

  • Hurricanes, floods, heavy downpours and winter storms
  • Droughts and abrupt shifts in rainfall
  • Melting glaciers, ice caps and ice sheets
  • Rising sea level through ice melt and thermal expansion
  • Coastal flooding and erosion
  • Damage to polar communities, wildlife and plants
  • Changes in local and regional climates and growing seasons
  • Wider threats to health, ecosystems and livelihoods

4.5 El Niño anecdote and lesson

The live session used El Niño to illustrate why climate conclusions require observation over time. It was described as unusual warming in the eastern Pacific near Peru that affects trade winds, monsoonal rain-bearing systems and ocean currents. Scientists observed and studied the recurring pattern before classifying it. The instructor advised consulting authoritative climate sources (and cautioned that Wikipedia is useful for initial reading but not an authoritative final source).

Application: environmental governance and carbon management should be based on long-run evidence, not on interpreting every isolated weather event as climate change.

5. Mitigation, adaptation and climate strategy

5.1 Mitigation versus adaptation

Concept Meaning Examples from the week
Mitigation Actions that reduce greenhouse-gas emissions or strengthen removals Conservation legislation, clean-technology investment, energy efficiency, renewable energy, planting trees, forest protection, carbon capture
Adaptation Adjustment or preparation of natural or human systems for a changing environment, to moderate harm or exploit beneficial opportunities Preparing settlements, infrastructure or ecosystems for changing climatic conditions

5.2 Integrated mitigation logic

An effective strategy must address both sides of the carbon balance:

  • Reduce sources: power generation, transport, industry, buildings, food and waste.
  • Protect or increase sinks: forests, soils and other ecosystems that remove and store carbon.

Replacing coal electricity with clean power is not enough if forests continue to disappear: emissions fall on one side, but CO₂-removal capacity also falls on the other.

5.3 Practical actions

  • Improve energy efficiency and energy management.
  • Use LED/efficient lighting and appliances.
  • Measure machine-level energy use in factories; replace inefficient equipment.
  • Insulate buildings to reduce heat loss.
  • Use smart heating, ventilation and air-conditioning systems.
  • Use transparent, energy-efficient glass and passive/green-building methods.
  • Install solar panels, wind power and hydropower; procure renewable electricity.
  • Prefer public transport, electric vehicles, CNG and biofuel-based transport over conventional fossil-fuel vehicles.
  • Prevent deforestation; undertake afforestation/reforestation.
  • Improve waste management and capture landfill methane.
  • Adopt circular-economy practices.

Energy efficiency produces both environmental and business value: lower demand and emissions, lower operating expenses and improved asset/building performance.

6. Carbon footprint and CO₂ equivalent

6.1 Carbon footprint

A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse-gas emissions associated with a person, activity, product, building, organization or country during a stated period. It includes direct and indirect emissions and is normally expressed in carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e).

Sources include:

  • Manufacturing and industrial processes
  • Heating and building energy
  • Electricity consumption
  • Fossil-fuel transport
  • Food and purchasing choices
  • Waste and landfills
  • Methane, nitrous oxide, CFCs and other gases in addition to CO₂

6.2 CO₂ equivalent (CO₂e)

Different greenhouse gases trap different amounts of heat. CO₂e converts the quantity of each gas into the amount of CO₂ that would create an equivalent warming effect, using global warming potential. This allows emissions such as methane and nitrous oxide to be aggregated and compared using one metric. The transcript mentions reporting in metric tonnes or million metric tonnes; one tonne is 1,000 kg.

6.3 Why footprint measurement matters

It answers:

  • Where do emissions originate?
  • Which sources are largest?
  • Which actions offer the greatest reduction?
  • Where should a limited sustainability budget be invested?
  • What baseline, target and offset quantity are required?

School example: measure electricity, heating, transport, food and waste emissions; compare projects; invest in the option with the highest emission reduction per unit of money.

6.4 Ways to reduce a footprint

  • Improve energy efficiency and change lifestyle/purchasing choices.
  • Conduct home or organizational energy audits.
  • Use public transport and cleaner vehicles/fuels.
  • Adopt renewable energy.
  • Improve building insulation and lighting.
  • Reduce consumption and operational waste.

7. Carbon credits, offsets, markets and neutrality

7.1 Essential distinctions

Term Exam-ready meaning
Carbon credit A tradable permit/allowance representing the right to emit one tonne of CO₂ or its equivalent in other greenhouse gases, or a verified unit created by reduction/removal under the relevant scheme
Carbon offset An action or verified reduction/removal elsewhere that compensates for an emission, measured in CO₂e
Carbon footprint The quantity of greenhouse-gas emissions attributable to an entity/activity
Carbon market/trading A system in which credits, allowances or certificates are bought and sold to reward lower emissions and impose a cost on higher emissions
Carbon neutral Emissions are balanced by equivalent reductions/removals/offsets, giving net emissions of zero
Carbon negative More carbon is removed/avoided than emitted; the recordings cite Bhutan as the only carbon-negative country
Net zero A deeper strategy centered on very large direct reductions and removal of the small unavoidable remainder; broader and more transformation-focused than a simple offset-based neutrality claim

7.2 Three sources/types of carbon credits

  1. Reduced emissions: for example, energy-efficiency measures.
  2. Removed emissions: carbon capture and forest planting.
  3. Avoided emissions: preventing deforestation or avoiding an otherwise expected emission.

Projects mentioned include renewable-energy farms, industrial energy efficiency, forest conservation, afforestation/reforestation, carbon capture and methane capture.

7.3 Cap-and-trade and carbon-market mechanism

A cap-and-trade programme sets an emissions limit that can be tightened periodically.

  • A high emitter that exceeds its allowance must buy extra credits and faces a financial cost.
  • A low emitter or verified reduction/removal project can receive credits and sell surplus units.
  • A central/independent authority or registry verifies activity and facilitates or oversees transfers.
  • Money flows from the higher emitter to the lower-emission/reduction project; the credit flows in the opposite direction.
  • The mechanism therefore penalizes pollution and rewards lower emissions, while giving firms an incentive to improve their own processes.

Illustration used in the live session:

Coal power plant emits 1 tonne → must acquire a credit. A registered solar project avoids/reduces emissions → receives a credit. Coal plant pays; solar project receives finance; the authority verifies the exchange.

Carbon-market instruments mentioned include carbon credits, emissions-trading certificates and perform-achieve-trade-type mechanisms. The live session also referred to the European ETS as an early/important financial mitigation mechanism and stated that India has an official large-scale carbon market.

7.4 Carbon credits are not a licence to pollute

This was the strongest repeated instructor emphasis:

  • Credits are a financial incentive and adjustment mechanism, not permission to increase pollution without restraint.
  • Organizations should inspect and transform operations because purchasing credits costs money.
  • The ultimate purpose of every climate instrument is to reduce harm and conserve Mother Earth.

7.5 Carbon offsets

Because greenhouse gases mix globally, a verified reduction can create climatic benefit regardless of where it occurs. Offsets may be bought, sold or traded as certificates linked to activities that lower or remove emissions.

Advantages:

  • Channel finance to renewable energy, forests, methane capture and cleaner technology.
  • Help finance an expensive transition from high-carbon to low-carbon processes.
  • Operate at organizational, sectoral, regional, national or international scale.
  • Generate environmental co-benefits and community development revenues.

Limitations and risks:

  • An offset-only strategy leaves the buyer’s own emissions unchanged.
  • It may delay energy-efficiency and operational improvements.
  • Dependence on credit availability and price creates risk.
  • Weak-quality projects create credibility and “paper neutrality” risks.
  • Offsets require credible measurement and verification; additionality matters—especially for renewable projects, the reduction should be additional to what would otherwise have happened.

7.6 Correct carbon-neutrality hierarchy

Measure → Avoid/reduce → Replace with clean alternatives → Offset the unavoidable remainder → Monitor and improve

Formula:

Net emissions = total emissions − direct reductions − verified removals/offsets

Measurement comes first because without a baseline an organization cannot set targets, identify priority sources, track progress or calculate the unavoidable remainder.

7.7 Offset project options

Afforestation and reforestation

  • Plant new forest or restore degraded forest.
  • Trees absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis and store carbon in biomass and soil.
  • Co-benefits: biodiversity, habitat restoration, soil and watershed value and community benefits.
  • Caveats: storage develops over time and requires permanence/protection.

Renewable-energy projects

  • Solar, wind or hydropower displaces fossil-fuel electricity.
  • Prevents future emissions and supports the clean-energy transition.
  • Additionality and credible baselines must be assessed.

Methane-capture projects

  • Capture methane from landfills, agriculture or waste treatment before it enters the atmosphere.
  • Particularly valuable because methane is a powerful heat-trapping gas.
  • Captured methane may also be used for fuel or energy, creating an additional benefit.

7.8 Riverview Town Hall case study

Facts: Riverview Town Hall wants carbon-neutral operations. Maya, its Sustainability Coordinator, must choose between:

  1. Improving municipal-building efficiency and adopting renewable energy first, then offsetting unavoidable emissions; or
  2. Buying credits immediately from a distant renewable project while postponing local improvements.

Maya considers afforestation/reforestation, renewable-energy and landfill-methane-capture offsets. She also notes that carbon-market revenue may fund environmental and community-development initiatives.

Recommended answer: choose Option 1.

  • It directly reduces local emissions and fossil-fuel dependence.
  • Efficiency lowers long-run operating costs.
  • Local upgrades strengthen infrastructure and accountability.
  • It avoids dependence on credits and reduces credibility risk.
  • High-quality offsets then balance only the unavoidable remainder.
  • Monitoring should track electricity, fuel, building emissions, energy savings, renewable generation, credit verification and project co-benefits.

Stakeholder value: citizens gain a cleaner environment; government gains climate leadership and policy alignment; communities may gain jobs, infrastructure and development finance; future generations gain lower climate risk and more sustainable resources.

7.9 Vantara live-session illustration

The instructor used Reliance’s Vantara as a hypothetical/potential carbon-market case:

  • Habitat conservation, restoration, biodiversity and extensive tree cover can form a carbon sink.
  • A high-emission business such as a steel or petroleum company may need offsets for difficult-to-eliminate emissions.
  • If Vantara were formally packaged, verified and registered as a carbon-credit project, credits could flow from it to the emitter and money could flow back to support the conservation project.
  • CSR funds could support green-habitat creation, which may generate both CSR value and potential future carbon revenue.

Critical caveat from the instructor: no material evidence was presented that Vantara currently operates as a carbon-credit project. It was explicitly an illustration of potential, not a claim of an existing transaction. Do not write it as an established fact.

8. Emission scopes, Scope 4 and carbon handprint

8.1 Conventional scopes

Scope Meaning given in the live session Example
Scope 1 Direct emissions from the company Fuel burned in an owned facility or vehicle
Scope 2 Indirect emissions from purchased electricity/energy Electricity sourced from a coal plant
Scope 3 Other indirect value-chain/supply-chain emissions Supplier, logistics or downstream emissions

8.2 Scope 4 / avoided emissions

The live session presented Scope 4 as an extended, positive-looking measure of emissions avoided, rather than emissions produced. It may examine:

  • Installed solar or wind capacity
  • Electric vehicles
  • More efficient technology
  • Afforestation/environmental conservation
  • Circular-economy processes

Example: if a solar installation displaces electricity that would have produced one tonne of CO₂, Scope 4/avoided-emissions thinking records that positive avoided impact.

8.3 Carbon footprint versus carbon handprint

  • Carbon footprint: how much the organization emits—its negative impact.
  • Carbon handprint: how much emission the organization helps avoid or reduce—its positive impact.

The handprint is the broader positive-impact framing linked to Scope 4. It complements, but must not be used to conceal, the footprint. A strong answer should report both: reduce the footprint while expanding genuine, verified handprint effects.

9. Environmental audit

9.1 Definition

Auditing means checking or assessing reports, systems and performance. Audits can be financial, energy-related or environmental.

An environmental audit is a systematic, well-defined management assessment that provides information on an organization’s environmental performance to the right people at the right time. It evaluates environmental responsibility, compliance, hazards, risks, policies, procedures and operational practices.

It connects with CSR and sustainability because a company must consider ethics, society and the environment and reduce or eliminate negative environmental impacts.

9.2 Areas examined

  • Environmental policy and procedures
  • Energy use, conservation and energy management
  • Pollution and emissions
  • Waste management
  • Reduce–reuse–recycle practices
  • Resource consumption
  • Potential environmental hazards and risk
  • Health, occupational health and industrial safety
  • Compliance with government rules and internal standards
  • Performance of the environmental management system (EMS)

9.3 Objectives

  • Assess compliance with environmental laws and regulations.
  • Identify whether stated environmental objectives are met.
  • Establish a performance baseline for planning and developing an EMS.
  • Promote good environmental management.
  • Maintain public credibility and goodwill, including the views of nearby communities.
  • Raise awareness and reinforce internal commitment to environmental policies.
  • Minimize exposure to environmental, health and safety risk.
  • Detect weaknesses before they become serious problems.
  • Generate evidence for corrective action and management decisions.

9.4 Auditor’s duties and audit process

  1. Plan the audit: define methodology, scope, objectives and procedures.
  2. Collect information: review activities and records, energy use, waste, emissions and resource consumption.
  3. Inspect operations: conduct site/field visits and check facilities and operational procedures.
  4. Interview stakeholders: meet key personnel, all levels of management, workers and other employees; multiple levels provide a fuller picture.
  5. Test compliance: compare actual performance with laws, government guidelines, company policies, standards and internal targets.
  6. Analyse evidence: examine documentation and data, identify risks, gaps and EMS weaknesses.
  7. Report findings: prepare and present the audit report to officials and other concerned people.
  8. Recommend and follow corrective action: reduce waste, improve efficiency, prevent pollution and address non-compliance.

The final report is crucial in environmental, financial, energy and other audits because it gives decision-makers a coherent view of procedures, performance and needed action.

9.5 Benefits

  • Supplies top management with timely information for crucial decisions.
  • Identifies environmental-responsibility risks and helps reduce them.
  • Verifies legal/standards compliance and triggers corrective action.
  • Reveals EMS weaknesses early.
  • Improves efficiency, resource use, waste control and pollution prevention.
  • Builds environmental awareness and an environmentally responsible organizational culture.
  • Strengthens public credibility and demonstrates social commitment.

Top management must communicate the importance of the process to all officials and employees.

9.6 Important distinctions

Tool Main purpose
Environmental audit Periodic/systematic review of overall performance, compliance, risks and improvement opportunities
Environmental monitoring Repeated measurement of specified parameters over time
Sustainability report External communication of environmental/social/economic information to stakeholders

9.7 ISO note from the live session

The instructor referred to the ISO 14000 family of environmental standards. The basic certification pathway described was:

  1. Select the certification/standard being targeted.
  2. Meet its stated requirements.
  3. Submit the necessary documentation.
  4. Undergo audit and inspection by the relevant officials/body.
  5. Receive certification if requirements are satisfied.

This was only a base explanation; further ISO detail was deferred to a later week.

10. Glossary and connected concepts

Term Concise definition / exam point
Abrupt climate change A large, rapid and widespread change in a major component of the climate system
Anthropogenic Caused by human activity
Biomass Material of biological origin, living or dead, above or below ground—trees, crops, wood, plant waste, animal waste and cow dung
Biogas Gas obtained through anaerobic decomposition of organic slurry in the absence of oxygen; the transcript associates it with methane (CH₄)
Biosphere The part of the Earth system containing all ecosystems and living organisms in the atmosphere, on land and in oceans
Carbon cycle Exchange of carbon among major reservoirs: atmosphere, terrestrial biosphere/freshwater systems, oceans and sediments (including fossil fuels)
Carbon sequestration Capture and long-term storage of carbon; biologically, trees/plants absorb CO₂ and store carbon while releasing oxygen
CCS Carbon capture and sequestration/storage technologies that reduce CO₂ emissions from existing or new coal/gas power plants and industrial processes
Carbon sink A system that absorbs/stores more carbon than it releases, such as a forest or healthy ecosystem
COP Conference of the Parties under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change; regular international meetings involving more than 180 countries, as stated in the lesson
Global warming potential Relative heat-trapping effect used to compare greenhouse gases and calculate CO₂e
Green building A building using sustainable design/operation such as solar power, efficient HVAC and lighting, insulation and energy-efficient glazing
Smog Smoke + fog; intense air pollution that reduces visibility
Sustainable development Meeting present needs while preserving the ability of future generations to meet theirs

Three pillars of sustainability

  • Environmental: climate protection, pollution reduction and resource conservation.
  • Economic: growth, efficiency and long-term prosperity.
  • Social: well-being, equity and community welfare.

11. Integrated frameworks for revision

11.1 Climate cause–effect chain

Population/industrial activity → fossil-fuel use, deforestation, agriculture and waste → more greenhouse gases → enhanced greenhouse effect → global warming → climate-system changes → health, weather, ocean, ice, biodiversity and livelihood impacts

11.2 Organizational climate-action chain

Measure footprint → identify material sources → avoid/reduce emissions → improve efficiency → adopt renewable energy → protect sinks → offset only unavoidable emissions → audit/monitor → report and continuously improve

11.3 Governance logic

Standards and caps → measurement and verification → reward low emitters/removal projects → charge high emitters → finance cleaner technology → reduce total emissions

12. Exam preparation

12.1 High-priority instructor cues

  • Know factual items as well as analytical answers; the instructor said factual questions may be tagged “easy” but must be remembered.
  • SF₆ (sulphur hexafluoride) was explicitly identified as an expected factual answer concerning a high-warming-potential gas.
  • The greenhouse-effect MCQ answer is trapping of infrared radiation by gases.
  • Do not equate one day’s weather with climate change; stress a persistent long period.
  • Do not describe carbon credits as a licence to pollute.
  • Explain carbon credits with the theory, market mechanism and an example, not only “one credit = one tonne.”
  • Remember gases besides CO₂ and understand global warming potential/CO₂e.
  • The live session urged students to study recordings because not every factual point can be repeated in class.

12.2 Likely short-answer questions and model points

1. Define global warming.

  • Long-term rise in average surface/near-surface temperature.
  • Natural heating/cooling exists, but current concern is enhanced anthropogenic warming.
  • Primary driver stressed: fossil-fuel combustion; also deforestation, industry, transport, agriculture and waste.

2. What is the greenhouse effect? Why is it both necessary and dangerous?

  • Atmospheric gases trap/re-radiate outgoing infrared heat.
  • Natural effect keeps Earth warm enough for life.
  • Extra human-caused gases strengthen the shield, trap excess heat and cause warming/climate change.

3. Distinguish global warming, climate change and weather.

  • Weather: short-term conditions.
  • Climate: long-period expected pattern.
  • Global warming: rising average temperature.
  • Climate change: broader long-term changes in rainfall, storms, oceans, ice and ecosystems; warming is one component/driver.

4. List major impacts of global warming.

  • Severe weather; heat deaths and illness; dirtier air/smog; wildlife extinction and habitat loss; ocean acidification; glacier melt; sea-level rise; coastal flooding/erosion; climate change.

5. Explain water-vapour feedback.

  • Warming raises evaporation.
  • More water vapour traps more heat.
  • Further warming follows: positive feedback.
  • Fixed-water-vapour models underestimate warming.

6. Define carbon footprint and CO₂e.

  • Total direct/indirect GHG emissions attributable to an entity/activity.
  • CO₂e converts different gases using global warming potential into a common CO₂ measure.
  • Enables prioritization, targets and offset calculation.

7. Differentiate credit, offset and footprint.

  • Footprint = emissions measured.
  • Credit = tradable unit/allowance, normally one tonne CO₂e.
  • Offset = verified action/reduction/removal used to compensate emissions.

8. What are the three types/sources of carbon credit?

  • Reduced emissions—energy efficiency.
  • Removed emissions—carbon capture/forest planting.
  • Avoided emissions—prevented deforestation or displaced fossil activity.

9. Explain cap-and-trade.

  • Authority sets cap and issues/verifies units.
  • High emitters buy; low emitters/reduction projects sell.
  • Limit may tighten over time.
  • Financial flow rewards low carbon and penalizes high carbon.
  • Aim is behavioral/process change, not a licence to pollute.

10. Define environmental audit and list its objectives.

  • Systematic management tool providing timely environmental-performance information.
  • Tests compliance, risk, policies, procedures, resource/energy use, waste and EMS performance.
  • Supports decisions, corrective action, public credibility and internal awareness.

11. Distinguish mitigation and adaptation.

  • Mitigation tackles causes through reduction/removal.
  • Adaptation prepares for impacts and moderates harm/exploits opportunities.

12. What are Scope 4 and carbon handprint?

  • Scope 4/avoided emissions measures the emissions prevented by positive action.
  • Handprint records positive avoided/reduced impact; footprint records emissions caused.
  • Examples: solar, EVs, efficiency, afforestation, circular economy.

12.3 Likely long-answer questions and answer structures

A. “Explain causes and consequences of global warming and climate change.”

  1. Define global warming and climate change; distinguish them.
  2. Explain natural equilibrium versus anthropogenic enhancement.
  3. Present sources: fossil fuels, industry, vehicles, deforestation, agriculture, waste/refrigerants.
  4. Explain greenhouse mechanism.
  5. Organize impacts under weather, health, air, oceans, biodiversity, cryosphere and coasts.
  6. Conclude with mitigation plus adaptation and environmental governance.

B. “Explain carbon-credit markets and evaluate their sustainability value.”

  1. Define footprint, credit, offset and CO₂e.
  2. Explain cap, central authority/registry and one-tonne unit.
  3. Show high-emitter payment and low-emitter/project reward.
  4. Give coal-plant/solar-project example.
  5. Discuss benefits: finance, incentives, technological transition and co-benefits.
  6. Discuss limitations: additionality, verification, dependency and credibility.
  7. State hierarchy: measure and reduce first, offset remainder.
  8. Conclude: not a licence to pollute.

C. “Discuss the environmental-audit process, objectives and benefits.”

  1. Define environmental audit.
  2. State areas covered and objectives.
  3. Explain planning, data collection, field visits, interviews, compliance testing, analysis, reporting and corrective action.
  4. Explain decision, risk, EMS, culture and credibility benefits.
  5. Distinguish audit from monitoring and reporting.

D. “Advise Riverview Town Hall on carbon neutrality.”

  1. Establish a footprint baseline.
  2. Reject immediate offset-only neutrality.
  3. Prioritize efficiency and renewable energy.
  4. Quantify remaining unavoidable emissions.
  5. Compare forest, renewable and methane projects using additionality, permanence, verification and co-benefits.
  6. Purchase verified offsets only for the remainder.
  7. Monitor performance and improve continuously.

E. “Critically assess Vantara as a potential carbon-credit model.”

  1. Identify conservation, habitat, biodiversity and tree-cover functions.
  2. Explain carbon-sink and potential credit creation.
  3. Model transfer: verified credit to steel/high emitter; finance to conservation project.
  4. Link CSR finance, environmental value and potential revenue.
  5. State the decisive caveat: the lecture offered a hypothetical potential and no evidence of an existing registered credit project.

12.4 Common confusions and corrections

Confusion Correct understanding
Greenhouse effect is entirely harmful Natural greenhouse effect enables life; anthropogenic enhancement is the concern
Global warming and climate change are identical Warming is the temperature trend; climate change is broader
A single unusual day proves climate change Weather is short term; climate change requires persistent long-run evidence
CO₂ is the only relevant gas CH₄, N₂O, water vapour, SF₆, ozone and refrigerants also matter
All air pollutants are greenhouse gases Some discussions overlap, but conventional pollutants and heat-trapping gases are not identical categories
Stratospheric and ground-level ozone are the same problem Stratospheric ozone protects from UV; ground-level ozone is harmful smog pollution
Carbon footprint means CO₂ only It covers all relevant GHGs expressed as CO₂e
Carbon credit and offset are identical A credit is a tradable unit/allowance; an offset is the compensating reduction/removal action or verified unit used for compensation
Buying credits makes continued emissions acceptable Reduce first; offsets cover only the unavoidable remainder
Carbon neutral means zero gross emissions It means net balance equals zero; gross emissions may remain
Handprint replaces footprint It complements footprint by showing avoided emissions; both require transparent reporting
Vantara already sells credits The instructor explicitly presented this only as a potential/hypothetical model

13. Final one-page recall

  • Human activity strengthens a necessary natural greenhouse effect and causes excess warming.
  • Global warming is temperature-focused; climate change covers the wider climate system.
  • Weather is observed now; climate is the long-run expected pattern.
  • Core impacts: severe weather, heat mortality, dirty air, biodiversity loss, acidic oceans, melting ice and rising seas.
  • Water vapour amplifies warming through positive feedback.
  • A footprint measures all GHGs in CO₂e; it must be measured before action is prioritized.
  • Credits and offsets create a financial mechanism linking high emitters to verified reductions/removals.
  • Responsible order: measure → reduce → replace → offset remainder → monitor.
  • Carbon markets reward low emission and penalize high emission; they are not pollution licences.
  • Footprint = harm caused; handprint/Scope 4 = emissions avoided.
  • Environmental auditing systematically tests compliance and performance, reports findings and drives corrective action.
  • The end purpose of every instrument is environmental conservation, genuine sustainability and protection of future generations.
