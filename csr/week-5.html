Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) is the responsibility of a business to operate ethically, benefit society and reduce negative environmental effects while pursuing its business objectives. The recurring framework throughout the week is:

  • Ethics: distinguish right from wrong and conduct business properly.
  • Society/people: create welfare, inclusion and mutual benefit.
  • Environment/planet: conserve resources, prevent pollution and promote sustainability.

The live class links this to the triple bottom line: People, Planet and Profit. A sound CSR strategy should not treat these as isolated goals; it should design operations that satisfy all three.

Instructor emphasis: CSR is relevant to the present and the future. It is increasingly strategic, not merely charitable. When a company gives back to society, society may return value through trust, reputation, talent, loyalty, finance and long-term legitimacy.

2. Foundational distinctions

2.1 CSR and legal compliance

Legal compliance CSR
Obeying laws, regulations and mandatory requirements Responsible action integrated into strategy, often going beyond the legal minimum
Reactive/minimum acceptable standard Proactive and strategic
Examples: legally required wages, pollution limits and reports Examples: community development, cleaner production, ethical sourcing, employee well-being, supplier human-rights programmes and carbon targets above legal limits

CSR complements regulation; it cannot replace it. Regulation applies minimum standards to all firms and can be enforced through inspections, fines and legal penalties. Voluntary CSR alone cannot ensure universal protection of public interests.

Indian caveat: although CSR is often described conceptually as voluntary action beyond compliance, India made CSR provisions mandatory for qualifying companies through the Companies Act, 2013. Thus, legal obligation and strategic responsibility coexist.

2.2 CSR and sustainability

CSR Sustainability
Asks: Are we acting responsibly? Asks: Can present activity continue without harming the future?
Emphasizes responsibility to society, ethical conduct, communities and stakeholders Emphasizes long-term resource use, environmental protection, economic continuity and intergenerational responsibility

Sustainable development means development that meets the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Present needs must be met, but future needs must not be damaged. CSR contributes to sustainability.

2.3 CSR and profit maximisation

  • Traditional view: business primarily maximises shareholder wealth.
  • Modern stakeholder/CSR view: business creates value for shareholders, employees, customers, suppliers, communities, government and the environment.
  • Responsible practices can strengthen rather than weaken long-term profitability by reducing costs and risk, improving trust and attracting talent, customers and finance.

2.4 CSR is more than charity

CSR originated partly from charity, philanthropy and ethical responsiveness, but modern CSR includes:

  • ethical sourcing and responsible supply chains;
  • employee development, welfare and workplace safety;
  • environmental and energy management;
  • governance, transparency and accountability;
  • responsible products, services and operations;
  • community programmes and philanthropy.

Philanthropy means action for people’s goodwill, welfare and benefit. Donations are one form of CSR, but an effective programme is embedded in core operations, strategic planning, performance measures and KPIs.

3. Scope of CSR in diverse sectors

CSR has an enormous scope across business, government and society. Its applications are not confined to the environment; they also cover ethical and social concerns.

Sector CSR priorities, examples and use cases
Energy and utilities Energy management and conservation; reduce energy use and carbon dioxide emissions; shift to renewables such as solar and wind; improve energy efficiency and pursue energy excellence. Utilities may educate customers about conservation and support reliable, environmentally responsible grids.
Finance and banking Ethical investment; socially responsible investment; financial-literacy programmes; economic and financial inclusion; financing that considers ESG risk.
Healthcare and pharmaceuticals Develop medicines and vaccines; invest in medical capacity; respond to diseases such as COVID-19; provide healthcare in underserved regions. Post-COVID, healthcare became an especially visible CSR focus for Indian companies.
Retail and consumer goods Sustainable sourcing (responsible purchase/procurement of materials); eco-friendly practices; reduce packaging waste; responsible waste management; ethical supply chains.
Automotive and transportation Reduce dependence on fossil fuels and emissions; promote electric two-wheelers/four-wheelers and fuel-efficient or hybrid vehicles; use CNG for trucks and buses; extend cleaner mobility from passenger vehicles to goods carriers. This is a rapidly growing sector with major CSR potential.
Transportation and logistics Cleaner fuels and modes; reduce carbon dioxide and other vehicle emissions; use eco-friendly passenger and goods transport; improve logistics efficiency while lowering pollution and global-warming impacts.
Hospitality and tourism Sustainable tourism and ecotourism; preserve culture; support local communities; protect water bodies, forests, surroundings and other natural resources; minimise tourism’s environmental footprint. Hotels and tourism firms can link community support with conservation.
Manufacturing and production Production converts inputs into outputs through a transformation process. CSR therefore focuses on machinery energy use, energy auditing, emissions and greenhouse gases, waste prevention, recycling, worker safety, cleaner production and resource efficiency. R&D that produces a low-waste technology can directly reduce operating costs.
Technology and IT Data privacy, cybersecurity, awareness of cyberattacks and fraud, digital inclusion and literacy; responsible disposal and recycling of electronic waste rather than dumping it into soil. Technology CSR has ethical, social and environmental dimensions.
Education Support schools with corporate resources and advice; improve teaching resources; assist skill development; extend primary education to children in remote and underprivileged areas. Example: the Classmate notebook initiative described in the recording directs ₹1 from a purchase to underprivileged students. Tata, Reliance and other major companies also support education.
Agriculture and food production Sustainable farming, support for local communities and small-scale farmers, food-waste reduction and methods that protect long-term productivity. Agriculture is important to India and contributes to GDP/national income.
Construction and real estate Sustainable building design and green buildings; lower energy consumption; solar panels; energy-efficient lighting and appliances; heat-reducing glass; consider local-community needs and concerns.
Entertainment and media Inclusive and diverse representation; ethical content creation; socially beneficial content. Ethics is not only environmental: media must consider what is right and socially responsible.

Practical sector-selection principle

CSR priorities should reflect an industry’s material impacts. For example:

  • a utility should prioritise conservation, renewables and grid stewardship;
  • a manufacturer should prioritise safety, emissions, waste and resource efficiency;
  • an IT company should prioritise privacy, cybersecurity, digital inclusion and e-waste;
  • a textile company should examine labour, sourcing, energy, water, waste and carbon.

4. Benefits of CSR

The live class’s organising question was: Who benefits? CSR benefits are stakeholder-centred. Beneficiaries may include employees, customers, the company, investors, communities, partners and the environment.

4.1 Employee satisfaction, morale and productivity

  • Employees are integral organisational resources; “without employees nothing can happen.”
  • Respect, support, flexibility and adequate opportunities raise morale and satisfaction.
  • Allowing employees to volunteer—even during working hours or for a few hours weekly—can create pride, motivation and meaning.
  • Motivation and satisfaction can improve productivity.
  • CSR can build a spirited, positive workforce: if employees see their organisation doing good, they may want to act consistently with that culture.

4.2 Employer of choice, recruitment and retention

An employer of choice is an organisation that high-calibre employees actively want to join and remain with. It normally offers:

  • ethical leadership and a responsible culture;
  • quality of work life and work–life balance;
  • flexibility, inclusion and employee well-being;
  • career and skill development;
  • meaningful work and social purpose.

Salary is not the only determinant of employment choice. A credible CSR record can attract people who value purpose and sustainability, retain existing staff and strengthen employer reputation. The live class referred to large firms such as Microsoft as examples of organisations whose sustainability culture can positively affect societal and talent perceptions.

4.3 Organisational identification

Organisational identification occurs when employees:

  • feel that they belong to the organisation;
  • share its values;
  • experience organisational success as their own;
  • become emotionally connected and proud.

Likely outcomes: stronger commitment, engagement, teamwork and discretionary effort; higher loyalty; lower turnover. CSR strengthens identification when employees believe the company authentically reflects their values.

Millennials and Generation Z: the quiz material emphasises that younger employees often value purpose, sustainability, diversity, ethics and social responsibility. CSR can therefore improve recruitment and retention, though genuine value alignment matters more than token volunteering.

4.4 Positive community impact and meaningful relationships

  • CSR should reduce environmental harm and produce social benefit across the value chain.
  • Community activities form relationships among employees, co-workers, the organisation and society.
  • Employees do more than routine desk work; volunteering adds meaning to work life.
  • Companies can provide education, health services, charity, care for poor and elderly people, and support remote communities.
  • CSR can build two-way collaboration and trust among companies, communities, customers, employees and other stakeholders.

4.5 Gratitude and giving back

Gratitude means acknowledging what one has received and giving back. A company draws resources, labour and legitimacy from society and the environment, so it owes commitments in return.

Examples:

  • locate operations in a remote village and create jobs for nearby residents;
  • donate to a chosen charity;
  • volunteer time, effort and resources on weekends;
  • spend time with vulnerable groups;
  • undertake afforestation: plant and nurture trees.

This mutual relationship fosters development inside and outside the organisation and can strengthen the employee–organisation bond and sense of belonging.

4.6 Brand image, reputation and customer loyalty

  • Visible, credible CSR can enhance public image, brand recognition and goodwill.
  • Customers may feel proud to buy from a responsible company.
  • Trust and satisfaction support brand loyalty and may increase sales.
  • Positive media coverage, word-of-mouth and partner relationships reinforce brand value.
  • A good social image can become a strategic organisational asset, but CSR must not be reduced to advertising or image management.

4.7 Operational and financial performance

  • Energy efficiency, waste reduction, recycling and cleaner production can lower operating and production costs.
  • Lower costs can increase profit and competitiveness.
  • CSR-related R&D encourages ideas, prototypes and technologies that reduce waste or resource use.
  • Environmental investments can yield a chain of benefits: lower energy/resource use → lower operating cost → higher efficiency/profitability → stronger competitiveness.
  • Research discussed in the live class supports a relationship between CSR and access to finance.
  • Strong CSR/ESG may improve investor confidence, reduce perceived risk, attract institutional investment and lower the cost of capital.

4.8 Creativity and innovation

  • CSR encourages “thinking outside the box” and new ideas beyond purely technical work.
  • R&D begins with an idea, converts it into models/prototypes and ultimately results.
  • Recognition of good employee ideas raises motivation and morale.
  • Innovation and technology can generate competitive advantage and scalable social/environmental impact.

4.9 Environmental protection and long-term sustainability

  • Promote energy conservation, efficiency and renewable energy.
  • Reduce waste, pollution, greenhouse gases and other negative impacts.
  • Protect resources and consider future generations.
  • Manage environmental risks and opportunities.

4.10 Cross-functional nature

CSR connects with:

  • HR: engagement, satisfaction, attraction and retention;
  • Marketing: image, reputation, loyalty and sales;
  • Operations: energy, waste, efficiency and cost;
  • Finance: performance, capital access and risk;
  • R&D: innovation and cleaner technology;
  • Governance: ethics, disclosure and accountability.

5. Importance and features of CSR

CSR is a continuously evolving concept. It has become globally popular because a business is expected to be ethical, environmentally friendly and socially beneficial.

Major reasons CSR is important

  1. Promotes ethical behaviour: builds principles of right conduct into business decisions.
  2. Creates social value: welfare programmes in education, healthcare, employment and community development benefit society.
  3. Protects the environment: conservation, eco-friendly operations and sustainable development reduce harm.
  4. Builds reputation and goodwill: responsible behaviour improves public and brand image.
  5. Improves cost efficiency: less waste, energy and resource consumption can reduce production cost.
  6. Stimulates innovation: new ideas and technologies help achieve social/environmental goals and business advantage.
  7. Creates competitive advantage: benchmarking against best-in-class organisations and attaining excellence can give a firm an edge.
  8. Strengthens employee engagement and productivity: respected, purposeful employees are more motivated.
  9. Supports long-term sustainability: preserves environmental and social foundations required for continuing operations.
  10. Mitigates risk: anticipates environmental, social, ethical, labour and reputational problems.
  11. Builds stakeholder engagement and trust: strengthens relationships with shareholders, clients, employees, customers and communities.
  12. Improves access to finance: credible ESG performance can attract capital and reduce perceived risk.
  13. Expresses responsibility and gratitude: gives back to society while ensuring sustainable growth.

Benchmarking and competitive advantage

Benchmarking is comparison with a best-in-class or reference company/technology. It helps identify superior practices and pursue excellence. Competitive advantage means having an edge over competitors. CSR awards, reporting and stakeholder expectations can stimulate benchmarking and innovation.

6. Stakeholders

6.1 Definition and effect

A stakeholder is any individual, group or organisation that:

  • has an interest or stake in a company/project;
  • can affect the company’s decisions, performance or outcomes; or
  • is directly or indirectly affected by its success, failure, actions or outcomes.

Stakeholder influence can be positive or negative. Stakeholders are not only beneficiaries: a project or failure may harm them. Because corporate actions create diverse direct and indirect effects, identifying the principal stakeholder requires close attention to scenario clues.

6.2 Stakeholder versus shareholder

Stakeholder Shareholder
Broad category: employees, owners, investors, bondholders, customers, clients, vendors, suppliers, creditors, government, competitors, society, environment, etc. Person/entity that owns shares and has invested capital
Interested in varied economic, social, employment, product or environmental outcomes Primarily concerned with company and stock performance and return on investment
May or may not own shares Is also a stakeholder

Exam rule: All shareholders are stakeholders, but all stakeholders are not shareholders.

6.3 Internal and external stakeholders

Internal stakeholders External stakeholders
Located within or directly connected to the organisation/project Located outside and generally indirectly affected
Employees, workers, owners, board of directors, top/middle management, project managers and internal investors Suppliers, vendors, customers, clients, creditors, competitors, society/community and government

Both categories are important to CSR success.

6.4 Characteristics of stakeholders

  1. Diverse entities: individuals or groups with different identities, interests and degrees of influence.
  2. Varying interests: shareholders may emphasise returns; employees, welfare and working conditions; customers, quality/brand; communities, impacts and contribution.
  3. Expectations: stakeholders expect responsible organisational behaviour, good products/services and positive societal impact.
  4. Power dynamics: power differs. Major investors/shareholders often possess greater financial influence; employees may have less direct power, though their role remains vital. Top management also exercises significant power.
  5. Mutual dependence: organisations depend on stakeholders for labour, capital, resources, customers and legitimacy; stakeholders depend on organisations for jobs, products and societal contribution.
  6. Continuous interaction: communication must be ongoing. Formal channels include meetings and reports; informal channels include one-to-one discussion, social media and public discussion.
  7. Dynamic nature: stakeholder interests and influence shift with external conditions, organisational strategies, competition and public sentiment. Organisations must adapt.
  8. Conflicting interests: different groups may want incompatible outcomes. Interests must be balanced and conflict-resolution mechanisms used so disputes do not damage performance. The recording stresses organisational welfare as a priority while negative conflict is reduced.
  9. Ethical considerations: decisions must be right and responsible; ethics preserves trust and reputation.
  10. Social and environmental impact: stakeholder concerns over society and the environment influence reputation and business prospects.
  11. Legal and regulatory impact: stakeholders, especially government/regulators, shape business operations, labour practices and environmental standards through laws, rules, protocols and guidelines.
  12. Long-term relationships: stability, customer and employee loyalty, and mutual benefit require enduring relationships.

6.5 Stakeholder scenario analysis from the live class

Scenario 1: Permanent work from home to improve work–life balance

  • Directly affected: employees; also management.
  • Other affected groups: families and public transport.
  • Environmental effect: less commuting/fuel use can benefit the environment.
  • Conclusion: both employees/social welfare and the environment are valid major stakeholders. The “largest” effect depends on the exact question wording and evidence.

Scenario 2: Replace office plastic bottles with steel bottles to reduce expenses

  • Owner/company benefits economically through lower recurring expense.
  • Steel manufacturers may benefit; plastic suppliers may be affected.
  • Larger CSR effect: reduced plastic use and a “go green” environmental benefit.
  • The highlighted business reason does not eliminate indirect stakeholder effects.

Scenario 3: Install solar panels mainly to lower electricity bills

  • Company/management benefits from lower cost.
  • Solar manufacturers and electricity utilities/boards (e.g., KSEB) are affected.
  • Major stakeholders/impacts: environment and society. Renewable transition reduces pollution and can free conventional electricity capacity for other users, potentially improving access or controlling cost.

Exam technique for stakeholder cases

  1. Break the scenario into its smallest elements.
  2. Identify the corporate action and stated purpose.
  3. List directly and indirectly affected parties.
  4. Separate economic, social and environmental effects.
  5. Use question cues to choose the most materially affected stakeholder.
  6. Avoid assuming that only benefits count; stakeholders can also experience costs or failure.

7. NGOs, civil society and regulators

NGOs/civil society

They influence companies through investigations, reports, advocacy, campaigns, protests, media attention and public pressure. They generally cannot impose legal penalties, but can reshape public opinion, reputation and corporate conduct.

Regulators

Government agencies use laws, regulations, inspections, enforcement, fines and penalties. Unlike NGOs, they possess legal authority. Therefore, public regulation is indispensable and CSR should complement it.

8. CSR initiatives and policy framework in India

8.1 Companies Act, 2013

  • The Indian government enacted the Companies Act, 2013.
  • It regulates incorporation, responsibilities and dissolution of companies.
  • It introduced CSR as a mandatory provision for qualifying categories of companies; the live class specifically emphasised Section 135.
  • The recording describes India as the only country to have regulated and mandated CSR for covered companies, presenting this as a significant institutional change.
  • Mandatory CSR can push the nation toward the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

8.2 National Voluntary Guidelines (NVGs), 2011

  • Implemented by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs in July 2011.
  • A set of principles/guidelines helping Indian businesses understand and inculcate responsible business conduct.
  • Provides a systematic approach to social, environmental and economic responsibilities; a company should not operate by imposing negative social impacts.

8.3 National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct (NGRBC), 2019

  • The NVGs were updated and released as the NGRBC in March 2019.
  • The update aligns responsible business with UN human rights, the UN Sustainable Development Goals and the Paris Agreement on climate change.
  • NGRBC provides a framework for inclusive and sustainable growth while addressing stakeholder concerns.
  • Illustrative SDGs mentioned: quality education, sustainable cities, economic growth and clean/renewable energy.

Responsible-business principles stressed in the recording

  • Provide goods and services sustainably and safely.
  • Make products eco-friendly and invest in environmental protection/conservation.
  • Respect and promote the well-being of all employees, including people in the value chain.
  • Respect the interests of all stakeholders.
  • Employee value and loyalty can raise productivity; stakeholder interests connect to corporate success.

8.4 National CSR Awards

  • Instituted by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs to recognise companies producing positive societal impact through innovative and sustainable CSR.
  • The awards are described as the apex recognition of Indian CSR initiatives.
  • The first awards were organised in 2019, with the President of India as chief guest.

Objectives

  • appreciate corporate CSR efforts and commitment to society/environment;
  • create competition and excellence among companies;
  • recognise impact, innovation, technology use, sustainability and scalability;
  • channel corporate CSR benefits into diverse sectors and remote areas.

Award categories described

Corporate awards for CSR excellence were grouped by eligible CSR spending, including companies with spend:

  • equal to or above ₹100 crore;
  • ₹10 crore to below ₹100 crore;
  • ₹1 crore to below ₹10 crore;
  • below ₹1 crore.

The transcript’s spoken figures are noisy, but its substantive point is that award categories are based on the amount spent on CSR.

9. Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR)

9.1 Why reporting matters

Theoretical recognition of CSR is not enough; it must be translated into practice and measured. The live class’s recurring principle was:

“What gets measured gets improved.”

A reporting framework is a measuring yardstick. It turns responsibility into disclosed indicators, lets stakeholders evaluate conduct and can nudge companies to improve.

9.2 Evolution from BRR to BRSR

  • Before BRSR, India used Business Responsibility Reporting (BRR) (the live transcript dates the framework to 2013).
  • BRR addressed business responsibility but did not inclusively capture broad/specific sustainability practices.
  • It evolved into Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR), adding the sustainability dimension.
  • The live class states that in 2020, SEBI required the top 1,000 listed companies by market capitalisation to prepare BRSR reports in a prescribed format, with coverage later expanding.
  • It connects to the post-Paris Agreement growth of sustainability reporting and to ESG.

9.3 Purposes

  • transparency;
  • accountability;
  • stakeholder trust;
  • comparable and measurable ESG information;
  • integration of sustainability into decisions;
  • incentive/nudge for companies to undertake sustainable activity;
  • stronger reputation, confidence, efficiency and innovation;
  • support for India’s sustainability efforts.

The instructor compares BRSR to a mirror or camera that captures corporate sustainability efforts and deficiencies. Investors can use it as a first view of ESG performance, while employees and other stakeholders can challenge a false statement (for example, an inaccurate claim of no human-rights violations).

9.4 Mandatory and voluntary content

There is one BRSR report, not two different types of reports. Within it are:

  • mandatory disclosures/items, and
  • voluntary disclosures, commonly called leadership indicators/items.

Advanced companies such as Infosys had reportedly disclosed comparable content even before BRSR requirements.

9.5 Illustrative disclosures

Mandatory/core themes mentioned:

  • energy consumption;
  • emissions, including Scope 1 and Scope 2 (introduced as technical terms for later study);
  • employee welfare, health, training and skills expenditure;
  • governance, ethics and policies;
  • human-rights violations;
  • waste generated, recycling and waste-management measures;
  • water use/conservation;
  • pollution-control arrangements;
  • CSR and community development.

Voluntary/leadership themes mentioned:

  • net-zero and climate targets;
  • advanced ESG achievements;
  • workforce diversity;
  • green innovation;
  • advanced battery/storage technology beyond simply installing solar panels.

The instructor cited a recent news example of Adani Energy installing a large battery system to store renewable energy as an illustration of advanced green innovation.

9.6 Assurance, audit and checks

Reporting is not supposed to be an unverified corporate claim. Independent verifiers/auditors and checking mechanisms validate disclosures. Recorded information can be audited, creating checks and balances and making greenwashing or false human-rights claims contestable.

9.7 Other reporting frameworks named

  • GRI: Global Reporting Initiative (described as operating globally from around 1999).
  • SASB: Sustainability Accounting Standards Board (the transcript renders this unclearly as “SASV”).
  • TCFD: Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures.
  • CSRD: European Union Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive.

BRSR is the principal Indian mechanism discussed for corporate disclosure, but the reporting logic applies to organisations honestly following other credible frameworks too.

10. Glossary

Term Exam-ready meaning
Corporate citizenship (CC) A business taking greater account of its social and environmental responsibilities; closely related to CSR.
Corporate governance (CG) Balancing economic and social goals and individual and communal goals while encouraging efficient resource use. A core feature of responsible companies.
Social reporting Reporting non-financial data, with attention to economic development and stakeholder involvement.
Socially Responsible Investment (SRI) Investment—often through an investment fund—in socially responsible activities or enterprises.
Sustainable development Meeting present needs without compromising future generations’ ability to meet their needs.
Key Performance Indicator (KPI) A quantifiable metric that measures important strategic, operational or financial performance. KPIs allow comparison within the same sector. The lecturer briefly says “KPA,” but the defined concept is KPI.
Public–Private Partnership (PPP) Collaboration between public and private sectors. Examples include some airports. Stated benefits include liability protection, tax concessions and financial/public support.
Reputation assurance Reliance on recognised/global principles or assurance processes to give stakeholders confidence in business conduct and reported reputation. The recording gives only a brief definition.
Developing country In the lecture glossary, a country with poor living standards and limited economic/industrial activity. This is a simplified course definition.
Economic growth drivers Forces behind national economic growth; the lecture highlights technology and agriculture.
Emerge To come out or become prominent/significant.
Corporate Social Investment (CSI) A form/tactic of CSR through which a corporation supports development projects, especially in emerging markets, to improve a community’s social, environmental and economic well-being.
ESG Environmental, Social and Governance criteria/metrics by which stakeholders assess a firm’s commitment to CSR/sustainability and its management of related risks and opportunities. It goes beyond environment alone.
ESG specialist A technology/vendor specialist focusing on a particular ESG area, such as waste management.
Value chain All interlinked value-creating activities involved in delivering a product to the customer; employee and stakeholder well-being should extend across it.
Materiality assessment A process for identifying the ESG issues most important to both the organisation and its stakeholders, so resources can be prioritised.
Creating Shared Value (CSV) Creating economic value for the business while simultaneously solving social or environmental problems.

ESG dimensions

Environmental (E) Social (S) Governance (G)
Carbon/climate, pollution, waste, water, renewable energy and efficiency Employee welfare, human rights, diversity, safety, privacy, product/customer welfare and community engagement Board oversight, ethics, transparency, accountability, risk management and anti-corruption

Typical finance pathway:

Better CSR/ESG → higher investor confidence → more institutional investment → lower perceived risk → lower cost of capital → stronger long-term financing and flexibility.

11. GreenBridge Textiles case study

11.1 Situation and stakeholder pressure

GreenBridge Textiles is a mid-sized apparel/textile manufacturer whose CSR team is led by Priya.

  • Customers increasingly prefer brands with strong CSR records.
  • Retail partners ask for clear sustainability commitments, ethical sourcing and responsible manufacturing.
  • Employees want a stronger culture of social responsibility and meaningful development.
  • Institutional investors screen firms using ESG criteria.

11.2 Strategic options

Option A — Community and employee development Option B — Environmental sustainability
Community programmes, employee development, volunteering, workplace culture and well-being Renewable energy, energy storage, cleaner operations and net-zero carbon commitments
Primarily advances the Social pillar Primarily advances the Environmental pillar
Builds organisational identification, employer brand, engagement, recruitment and retention Reduces emissions and operating costs, improves ESG standing, resilience and investor attraction
Strong focus on employees and communities Strong focus on investors, customers and retail partners, with internal cost savings

11.3 Technical ideas in Option B

  • Renewable energy: naturally replenished sources such as solar, wind, hydro and geothermal; reduces emissions and fossil-fuel dependence.
  • Energy storage: batteries/grid or industrial storage hold electricity for later use, enabling renewable utilisation, supply stability, lower peak cost and resilience.
  • Net zero: balance greenhouse-gas emissions produced with removals/offsets; pursued through efficiency, clean technology, renewable energy, reduction and offsets.

11.4 Shared benefits

Both options can improve:

  • brand reputation and favourable media coverage;
  • customer trust, preference, loyalty and word-of-mouth;
  • retail-partner relationships and contract security;
  • stakeholder confidence and competitive differentiation;
  • long-term business sustainability.

11.5 Materiality and integrated recommendation

The best answer is not automatically “A” or “B.” Conduct a materiality assessment of employees, customers, investors, retail partners, communities and regulators, then allocate resources to the most significant issues.

A strong phased strategy is:

  1. identify material stakeholder priorities;
  2. build an authentic internal foundation through employee/community development;
  3. scale renewable energy, efficiency, storage and net-zero initiatives;
  4. integrate people, planet and profit into business strategy and KPIs;
  5. disclose, measure and verify outcomes.

This embodies Creating Shared Value: environmental investment lowers costs and emissions, improves ESG and investor confidence, and strengthens reputation—benefiting both business and society.

12. Common misconceptions and confusions

  1. “CSR is only charity.” Incorrect. Philanthropy is one element; strategic CSR includes operations, supply chains, people, environment and governance.
  2. “CSR and sustainability are identical.” Related but distinct: responsibility versus long-term continuity/intergenerational needs.
  3. “CSR replaces law.” Incorrect. Regulation establishes enforceable minimum standards; CSR complements it.
  4. “CSR is merely advertising.” Incorrect. Image is a benefit, but credible CSR must be integrated, measurable and genuine.
  5. “CSR is only a cost.” Incorrect. It may require immediate investment but can produce lower costs, less risk, better talent, finance, loyalty and resilience.
  6. “Only environmental activities count.” Incorrect. CSR includes ethics, society and environment; ESG adds governance explicitly.
  7. “Shareholder and stakeholder mean the same.” Incorrect. Shareholders are one stakeholder subset.
  8. “Stakeholders are only beneficiaries.” Incorrect. Anyone affecting or affected—positively or negatively, directly or indirectly—may be a stakeholder.
  9. “Internal stakeholders are more important than external stakeholders.” Both are essential and mutually dependent.
  10. “Power is equal among stakeholders.” It varies with finance, authority, information, legitimacy and organisational position.
  11. “There are two kinds of BRSR reports.” Incorrect. One BRSR contains mandatory and voluntary/leadership items.
  12. “Installing solar panels is only an environmental act.” It also affects cost, utilities, suppliers and society.
  13. “Employee volunteering alone ensures retention.” It helps, but authentic value alignment, leadership, development, flexibility and work–life balance remain important.
  14. “ESG proves performance automatically.” Metrics help stakeholders assess commitments and risk, but disclosures require validation and further analysis.
  15. Terminology noise: the recording sometimes says “ECG”; the intended sustainability concept is ESG. It also says “CESS” while describing a Special Economic Zone (SEZ) where certain taxes/laws may be relaxed. Use the standard terms in answers while noting the lecture’s intended idea.

13. Exam preparation

13.1 High-priority short-answer questions

1. Define CSR and state its three dimensions.

Model points: responsibility beyond profit; ethical conduct; socially beneficial action; reduction of environmental harm; ethics–society–environment; integrated with business and stakeholders.

2. Define sustainable development.

Model points: meet present needs; do not compromise future generations; environmental protection; intergenerational responsibility; present and future needs balanced.

3. Distinguish stakeholder and shareholder.

Model points: stakeholder is broad; affects/is affected; shareholder owns shares and seeks return; shareholder is a stakeholder; not all stakeholders are shareholders; give examples.

4. Distinguish internal and external stakeholders.

Model points: within/direct versus outside/indirect; internal examples—employees, board, managers, owners, investors; external examples—customers, suppliers, creditors, government, competitors and society.

5. What is benchmarking?

Model points: comparison with best-in-class/reference organisation or technology; identifies gaps; promotes excellence; supports competitive advantage.

6. What are KPI, ESG, SRI, CSI and PPP?

Give the full form, a one-sentence definition and one use/example for each.

7. What is organisational identification?

Model points: belonging and value alignment; organisational success felt personally; pride; higher engagement/commitment/effort; lower turnover; CSR can strengthen it.

8. What is materiality assessment?

Model points: identifies ESG issues important to both company and stakeholders; consult major stakeholders; prioritises resources; aligns strategy and long-term value.

1. Explain the scope of CSR across diverse sectors.

Show stakeholder-centred links rather than merely listing.

4. Explain CSR initiatives in India.

5. Explain BRSR and how it translates CSR into practice.

6. “CSR is a strategic investment, not merely a cost.” Discuss.

13.3 Scenario questions and model approach

Work-from-home case

Identify employees as directly affected, then management, family, transport and environment. Explain why employee and environmental answers may both be valid; select the primary one using the exact wording.

13.4 Answer-writing framework

For a 10- or 15-mark answer:

  1. Start with a precise definition.
  2. State the core framework (ethics–society–environment or people–planet–profit).
  3. Organise points into numbered subheadings.
  4. Explain the mechanism: initiative → stakeholder effect → business/societal outcome.
  5. Add one course example (Classmate, remote-village jobs, afforestation, low-waste R&D, solar/storage, GreenBridge or BRSR).
  6. Include a caveat or distinction where relevant.
  7. End with integrated, long-term stakeholder value.

14. One-page revision synthesis

  • CSR integrates ethical conduct, social welfare and environmental care into business.
  • Its strategic objective is stakeholder value and sustainable growth, not charity alone.
  • It applies to every sector, but initiatives should follow the sector’s most material impacts.
  • Benefits flow to employees, customers, communities, investors, the company and environment.
  • Central business benefits: talent, morale, productivity, trust, reputation, loyalty, sales, innovation, lower cost/risk and access to finance.
  • Stakeholders affect or are affected by corporate outcomes; effects may be direct/indirect and positive/negative.
  • All shareholders are stakeholders, but not all stakeholders are shareholders.
  • India’s framework progresses from NVGs (2011), Companies Act/Section 135 (2013) and NGRBC (2019) to awards and sustainability reporting.
  • BRSR operationalises responsibility through measurable, auditable ESG disclosures: what gets measured gets improved.
  • The best CSR strategy integrates People + Planet + Profit, uses materiality to prioritise, measures results with KPIs, discloses them credibly and creates shared value.

Review Week Augmentation: ESG and the GreenWave Decision

  • ESG means non-financial environmental, social and governance factors that affect long-run business performance and decision quality; it is not charity. It complements revenue, profit, assets, cash flow and debt analysis rather than replacing them.
  • Hidden risks include: E—carbon regulation, floods, heat and scarcity; S—strikes, labour/human-rights failures and backlash; G—fraud, weak boards, corruption and compliance failure. ESG extends credit, market, liquidity and operational risk analysis.
  • ESG is not only environmental and does not guarantee returns. It can improve understanding of risks and opportunities and may support confidence, reputation, capital access, preparedness and resilience.
  • Governance converts E and S promises into performance through oversight, controls, responsibility and evidence.

GreenWave integrative case

GreenWave is a mid-sized export garment firm with three water- and energy-intensive dyeing/finishing units, excessive wastewater, above-industry carbon emissions, weak supplier monitoring and inconsistent safety records. European buyers have requested detailed ESG reporting twice. Priya proposes water recycling, partial renewables and governance reform for about ₹6 crore; Ritesh highlights inflation, raw-material costs, liquidity and short-term targets. Buyers hint at multi-year contracts and premium pricing, but neither is guaranteed. Arvind’s real dilemma is the intensity and timing of investment, not whether ESG matters.

Choice Main benefit Main risk
Delay Preserves cash and near-term profit Buyer loss, regulation, reputation and competitiveness risk
Full immediate investment Strong performance signal and faster improvement CapEx, liquidity and implementation pressure
Phased programme Progress, control, flexibility and change management Can become cosmetic delay unless milestones, budgets and reporting are explicit
  • Evaluate value through revenue (retention/new buyers/possible premium), cost (energy/water/waste savings), risk (compliance and fewer disruptions), brand/trust and possible financing effects. Treat outcomes probabilistically.
