1. Week overview

Week 3 connects five core topics:

  1. Ethics in CSR and ethical leadership
  2. Corporate citizenship
  3. Corporate philanthropy
  4. The role of government and society in CSR
  5. Environmental CSR

The live session adds governance, Indian legal context, international CSR regimes, a trust-governance dilemma, and revision of earlier concepts such as Carroll’s pyramid, strategic CSR, data-driven CSR and the Tesla/ESG example.

Central theme: CSR is not a box-ticking or publicity exercise. It is the genuine, accountable and strategic translation of ethical commitments into practices that benefit stakeholders, society and the environment while supporting sustainable business value.

2. Ethics and CSR

2.1 Meaning and relationship

  • Ethics concerns what is right and wrong and the moral principles that guide conduct and decisions.
  • Business ethics comprises the moral principles and values guiding organisational decision-making and business dealings.
  • CSR comprises initiatives and strategies through which an organisation becomes socially accountable. It is broad, varies by company and industry, and focuses on ethics, society and the environment.
  • CSR may be regarded as one facet of a wider system of business ethics.
  • Modern business requires both ethical conduct and CSR. Spending money alone does not make CSR ethical; the purpose, process, accountability and impact matter.
  • An organisation is accountable to many stakeholder groups, not merely its owners.

2.2 Core ethical principles

Principle Meaning in CSR/business practice
Honesty Truthful communication and reporting, even when results are poor
Integrity Consistency between values, words and actions; doing what is right
Fairness/justice Equitable treatment, fair wages and balanced decisions
Transparency Clear disclosure of decisions, spending, results and failures
Accountability Accepting responsibility for decisions, conduct and outcomes
Respect for stakeholders Considering legitimate interests and treating people with dignity
Respect for law Complying with applicable domestic law
Respect for international norms Following accepted standards of behaviour beyond local minimums
Respect for human rights Protecting the dignity and rights of all affected people

Other repeatedly emphasised qualities are clarity, trustworthiness, responsibility, good conduct and the willingness to learn from mistakes and improve continuously.

2.3 Stakeholders

A stakeholder is a person or group linked to, interested in, or affected by the business. The transcript identifies:

  • Shareholders and investors
  • Employees and managers
  • Customers and clients
  • Top-level officials and the board
  • Suppliers and partners
  • Communities and the wider public
  • Government and civil society
  • Future generations and, indirectly, the natural environment

Ethics requires a reasonable balance among stakeholder interests and avoidance or management of conflicts of interest. Ethical practices build stakeholder trust, which strengthens the company.

2.4 ISO 26000 principles of social responsibility

The instructor refers to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), headquartered in Geneva, and its guidance on social responsibility, ISO 26000:2010. Its seven principles are:

  1. Accountability
  2. Transparency
  3. Ethical behaviour
  4. Respect for stakeholder interests
  5. Respect for the rule of law
  6. Respect for international norms of behaviour
  7. Respect for human rights

Exam caution: ISO 26000 is guidance on social responsibility; do not confuse it with a certifiable management-system standard.

2.5 Ethical consciousness and organisational value

Ethical conduct is not only altruistic. It creates a reinforcing cycle:

ethical practice → stakeholder trust and credibility → reputation/goodwill and brand recognition → customer loyalty and investor confidence → stronger performance, innovation and long-term success

Benefits discussed:

  • Enhanced reputation and brand image
  • Customer satisfaction, loyalty and trust; consumers increasingly examine social and environmental impacts
  • Investor confidence and access to support/capital
  • Attraction and retention of talented employees
  • Higher employee morale, engagement, pride and performance
  • Positive work culture
  • Legal and operational risk mitigation; fewer violations and penalties
  • Innovation in products and services prompted by social/environmental needs
  • Competitive differentiation and advantage
  • Better ESG profile and stronger stakeholder relationships
  • Sustainable long-term business success

2.6 Ethical leadership and decision-making

Top management makes important decisions and sets the organisation’s ethical tone. An ethical leader:

  • Demonstrates integrity, honesty, fairness, transparency and trustworthiness
  • Leads by example and serves as a role model
  • Maintains consistency between promises and actions
  • Sets clear expectations and provides guidance
  • Creates open communication channels where employees may seek advice without fear
  • Rewards ethical conduct
  • Accepts accountability for decisions and expects accountability throughout the organisation
  • Learns from mistakes and promotes continuous improvement

Accountability runs both ways: employees are accountable to managers; managers and senior leaders are accountable to the board, shareholders and wider stakeholders.

2.7 Ethics goes beyond compliance

The live-session emphasis was especially strong:

  • Legal compliance is only the floor. Forming a committee, spending an amount and recording it in an annual report may satisfy a rule but does not by itself establish ethical CSR.
  • Ethical CSR must be fair, honest, integrated, genuine, accountable and useful to the stakeholders concerned.
  • Stakeholders must be treated with dignity and respect. Examples include fair wages, safe working conditions and meaningful community engagement.
  • A CSR project should not be imposed merely because a company prefers it; affected people should be heard and their genuine needs understood.
  • Returning unused CSR funds transparently rather than hiding them and protecting the environment for future generations were treated as ethical actions.

3. Ethics versus governance

Ethics and governance are closely related and may overlap; the instructor warned that there is no watertight separation and no true “ethics versus governance” conflict.

Ethics Governance
Concerns good/right conduct and values Concerns structures, processes, oversight, conduct and compliance
Common cues: honesty, fairness, integrity, empathy, justice, responsibility Common cues: law, procedure, committee, board review, audit, disclosure, reporting
Usually extends beyond the legal minimum Often ensures compliance and decisions in stakeholders’ best interests
Asks: “What ought we genuinely to do?” Asks: “Who decides, monitors, reports and is accountable?”

Classroom classification exercise

Situation Primary classification Reason
Acting honestly in CSR reports even when results are poor Ethics Honesty and integrity are the central values
CSR committee meets every quarter to review progress Governance Formal oversight and compliance mechanism
Ensuring community participation before a CSR project Primarily ethics, possibly both Empathy and respect for people; may also be mandated through procedures/SIA
Publishing CSR data in the annual report Governance Formal disclosure and accountability
Transparently returning leftover CSR funds Ethics (with governance implications) Honest handling rather than concealment
Caring for the environment for future generations Ethics Responsibility and intergenerational justice

Social Impact Assessment (SIA) nuance

A participant argued that government may require community participation when a company develops a project near its operations. The instructor agreed that SIA may create a governance/legal dimension. If a company merely performs the mandated SIA, that is good governance; if it goes beyond the requirement to improve community welfare, that additional contribution is ethical. Thus, the same action can carry both dimensions depending on law, purpose and depth.

4. Indian legal context for CSR

The live discussion used Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013 to contrast legal compliance with ethical CSR.

  • Eligible Indian companies have mandatory CSR obligations.
  • The lecture states that the prescribed expenditure is 2% of average annual profits (under the statutory framework, apply the relevant legal calculation and eligibility rules).
  • Eligible companies establish a CSR committee that guides what amount is spent and in which fields/projects.
  • The instructor recalled, with an explicit memory caveat, a minimum of three committee members including one independent director. For an exam, present the lecture point but note that precise composition depends on the applicable statutory provisions and company circumstances.
  • CSR expenditure and activity are reported, reviewed/audited and placed before relevant corporate or regulatory mechanisms.
  • Internal audit/disclosure signals how CSR is practised and may attract investor confidence.
  • The existence of legal rules has not always translated into proportionate social impact; hence genuine ethical commitment remains vital.
  • Committee composition and processes may also depend on company statutes/bylaws and the law of the country.

Exam distinction: “Mandatory CSR in India” does not mean all aspects of CSR everywhere are compulsory. The Indian regime mandates defined duties for eligible companies, while the ethical idea of CSR remains broader than statutory compliance.

5. Corporate citizenship

5.1 Definition and scope

Corporate citizenship is how a company exercises its rights, obligations, privileges and overall responsibility within local and global society and the environment. It is the extent to which a business is socially responsible for its actions and impacts.

In simple terms, the company behaves as a responsible member/citizen of society, translating values and commitments into visible, measurable action. Examples named were Tata, Reliance and Infosys.

Strong corporate citizenship:

  • Goes beyond surface-level sustainability and one-off philanthropy
  • Reduces emissions, waste, pollution and other negative effects
  • Protects employee welfare
  • Embeds ESG and stakeholder considerations into strategy, plans, goals and everyday operations
  • Creates financial success while considering every stakeholder group and the impacts of work
  • Seeks community contribution beyond profit and, at its highest level, transformative social impact

5.2 Corporate citizenship, CSR and ESG

  • The recorded module presents corporate citizenship as the larger umbrella and CSR as the specific activities that advance it.
  • Corporate citizenship concerns the business’s overall societal impact; CSR refers to particular policies, programmes and practices within the business.
  • ESG categorises efforts into environmental, social and governance dimensions and offers metrics through which some of these efforts may be assessed.
  • Corporate citizenship and CSR both seek lower negative impacts and greater welfare, but citizenship is the broader identity/posture of the company.

5.3 Benefits

  • Builds or rebuilds customer trust
  • Differentiates the firm positively in a competitive market
  • Enhances brand equity, public goodwill and loyal customer relationships
  • Improves employee engagement, morale and retention
  • Attracts new/talented employees and improves performance
  • Strengthens reputation and long-term business value
  • Encourages environmental performance—lower emissions and waste—and social welfare

5.4 Five stages of corporate citizenship

These stages measure the level of company involvement with society, forming a continuum from compliance to transformation.

Stage Core idea Typical features
1. Elementary Basic recognition/compliance Focus on obeying existing laws rather than creating positive community impact
2. Engaged Interest and participation Leaders begin attending to societal expectations; mindset shifts toward wider roles and responsibilities
3. Innovative Defined policy and new solutions More programmes are launched; policy becomes clearer; innovative citizenship and philanthropy solutions appear
4. Integrated Embedded in core business Social, environmental and financial performance is continuously monitored; citizenship enters core values, daily work and business lines; social problems are addressed through business solutions
5. Transforming Leadership/system change Company is viewed as a model; its approach produces transformative solutions and changes both society and the business

Instructor examples:

  • Microsoft conserving a large part of the Amazon forest was used as an illustration of a transformative role.
  • Tata’s trust-based ownership/business model was presented as transformative corporate citizenship: major trust shareholding allows a substantial part of value/profits to flow back to society.
  • The point is not to memorise labels alone, but to explain how the depth of involvement increases at each stage.

6. Corporate philanthropy

6.1 Meaning

Corporate philanthropy consists of investments and activities voluntarily undertaken by a company for the welfare/goodwill and development of society and to manage its social impact. It commonly supports local community and nonprofit organisations.

It has grown with higher public scrutiny and dialogue through social media: customers can see, question and respond to corporate social effects, increasing demands for responsibility, accountability and transparency.

6.2 Philanthropy versus CSR

Corporate philanthropy CSR
Primarily voluntary giving/support for social causes Broader responsibility embedded in policies, practices and business functions
Often donation, grant, sponsorship or volunteering oriented Often aligned with business goals, operations, stakeholders and impacts
Can be episodic or programme based Ideally strategic, continuous and integrated
One component of responsible corporate conduct Wider framework including ethical, social, environmental and economic responsibilities

Philanthropy matters, but it should not be mistaken for the whole of CSR or corporate citizenship.

6.3 Forms of corporate philanthropy

Form Explanation/example
Matching gifts Company matches employees’ donations to nonprofit organisations
Volunteer grants Monetary grants go to organisations where employees regularly volunteer
Employee/board grant stipends Employees or board members receive funds to donate to a nonprofit of their choice
Community grants Nonprofits apply under defined eligibility/selection criteria
Volunteer-support initiatives Employees are partnered with nonprofits to provide specialised expertise/support
Corporate sponsorship Financial support is provided and the nonprofit publicly acknowledges the sponsor
Scholarships Support to university students/children so they can continue education
Direct donations/support Money, equipment, services or other assistance to nonprofit/community causes

6.4 Strategic relevance and benefits

  • Greater employee engagement, morale, pride and retention
  • Better ability to attract top talent
  • More positive work environment
  • Improved public image, brand equity and reputation
  • Stronger customer confidence and relationships
  • Better government/community relations
  • Potential increases in sales and profits as people view the firm as responsible and caring, not interested only in money
  • Stronger stakeholder confidence and relationships, enabling deeper engagement with society

Philanthropy can therefore generate long-term strategic value, even when it offers no immediate profit. Its credibility depends on voluntary social purpose, accountability and alignment with a relevant need rather than publicity alone.

7. Governance dilemma: life trustees, fixed terms or hybrid model

The live class discussed an unnamed, real Indian business/trust controversy. The broader question was whether trustees who influence or govern a company should serve for life or through renewable fixed terms.

The governance problem

  • A social-benefit trust must itself earn trust through a sound governance mechanism.
  • Life tenure may allow one person’s preferences to influence the trust/company indefinitely.
  • Agency conflict occurs when the principal/owner and the agent/subordinate have different interests; governance must prevent private interests from displacing the trust’s mission and stakeholder interests.
  • In India, trusts govern or significantly influence several companies, so trustee tenure can affect corporate decisions and philanthropic strategy.
Model Strengths Risks/limitations
Life trusteeship Mission continuity, preservation of founder legacy, stable long-term philanthropy Concentration of power, agency problems, weak renewal/accountability
Fixed renewable terms Transparency, accountability, fresh thinking and periodic renewal Possible loss of continuity, institutional memory or founder mission
Hybrid Life/legacy trustees retained with periodic reviews, checks and accountability Requires carefully designed independent review; less common

The instructor’s research-oriented alternative was a hybrid governance model: preserve founder legacy and continuity, but subject trustees to periodic review and checks so accountability is not sacrificed. Each case should be assessed on its merits and on the best interests of stakeholders.

8. Role of government in CSR

Corporations are integral parts of a country’s economy and society. Government therefore has a critical role in promoting and shaping CSR.

8.1 Main governmental roles

  1. Law and regulation: establish rules, CSR obligations, disclosure duties and environmental/social standards.
  2. Awareness and capacity building: educate companies and stakeholders about CSR and social challenges.
  3. Information provision: maintain websites/resources explaining current issues, CSR’s business/societal role and possible solutions.
  4. Standard and policy setting: provide national/state/industry frameworks and guidelines.
  5. Economic incentives: subsidies or support for firms/business associations adopting socially responsible policies.
  6. Encouraging giving: payroll-giving schemes and other mechanisms through which employees donate to charity/good causes.
  7. Public procurement/partnership leverage: ask suppliers and partners to commit to ethical principles and report regularly on CSR targets.
  8. Monitoring and accountability: require reporting, review and evidence that commitments are performed.

Awareness matters because public attention to business-related social challenges makes solution development more likely. Government information helps private firms identify real problems rather than inventing irrelevant projects.

8.2 Government’s two-pronged approach

  1. Government/public organisations commit their own operations to the triple bottom line.
  2. They require or encourage suppliers and partners to adopt ethical principles and report performance against CSR targets.

8.3 Triple bottom line

P Concern
People Welfare of employees, customers, communities and other stakeholders
Planet Environmental conservation, pollution prevention and resource protection
Profit Economic viability and return/value creation

CSR performance is therefore not measured only by profit. Social and environmental outcomes must be considered, and resources must be protected for future generations.

9. Role and expectations of society

  • Society expects companies to act responsibly, sustainably and transparently.
  • Civil society, communities, customers and other stakeholders identify needs, scrutinise performance, participate in projects and hold firms accountable.
  • Open dialogue and participatory frameworks are required, particularly before interventions affecting a community.
  • The central focal point of CSR is societal benefit: welfare, health, education, poverty reduction and solutions to social challenges.
  • Stakeholder interests must be balanced, and companies should reduce harm to both society and the environment.

9.1 CSR and health

The lecture links CSR policies to the social determinants of health associated with the World Health Organization and the Ottawa Charter context.

Examples and impacts:

  • During peak COVID-19 (2020), companies supported health care through PPE, masks and sanitiser; industries also manufactured needed devices/products.
  • Provide life-saving medicines at affordable prices; cancer medicines were cited because their costs can be very high.
  • Extend health facilities to disadvantaged people, including those below the poverty line.
  • Improve health as a direct route to large societal impact.

9.2 CSR and education/social innovation

  • Companies possess administrative, organisational and project-management expertise that can be applied from project design through execution for the greater good.
  • CSR can improve education in underprivileged areas through school infrastructure, computers, laptops and digital facilities.
  • Education transforms an individual and produces wider positive social effects.
  • Corporations can lead social innovation and support social entrepreneurship because they understand how to make projects work from beginning to end.

10. CSR regimes across countries

Regime means the way CSR is conceptualised and practised under a country’s laws, institutions and culture. It is unsafe to assume one universal model.

Country Lecture’s broad characterisation
India CSR is mandatory for eligible firms under Companies Act Section 135, including 2% of average annual profits under the statutory formula; disclosure and governance mechanisms apply
China Broad emphasis described as “whatever you do, disclose it and be accountable/prove it”; reporting is required in some industrial/ethical-risk areas such as minerals, forced labour and supply chains; approach is evolving
United States No India-style broad mandatory CSR spending rule; relies more on sector-specific laws, especially pollution/environmental rules, fines and risk-oriented regulation

Instructor anecdotes/images used as memory devices:

  • The Great Wall represented China.
  • Tightrope performer Nik Wallenda over Niagara Falls represented the United States/risk-taking orientation.
  • The Western Ghats represented India’s conservation context.

Exam caveat: These are broad classroom comparisons, not exhaustive legal descriptions. Laws and reporting duties vary and evolve.

11. Environmental aspects of CSR

11.1 Meaning and concepts

Environmental CSR seeks to decrease harm caused by corporate activity, improve environmental conditions and implement sustainable practices.

  • Sustainability: meeting/preserving the needs of the present generation without compromising future generations’ ability to meet their needs.
  • Carbon footprint: the quantity (often expressed in tonnes of CO₂ or CO₂-equivalent) emitted through sources such as industrial operations, energy use and transport.
  • Carbon neutrality: net-zero carbon dioxide emissions through deep reduction and balancing of remaining emissions.
  • Renewable energy: replenishing/clean sources such as solar photovoltaic, solar thermal, wind and biomass, contrasted with exhaustible fossil fuels such as coal, petroleum and natural gas.
  • Environmental stewardship: responsible protection and improvement of natural resources and ecosystems.

The triple-bottom-line idea applies here: environmental performance is inseparable from social and financial performance.

11.2 Environmental CSR practices

A. Carbon, energy and materials

  • Reduce company emissions and work toward carbon neutrality.
  • Reduce reliance on non-renewable resources and invest in solar PV, solar thermal, wind and biomass.
  • Limit toxic and non-biodegradable materials, especially plastics that harm humans, animals, birds and aquatic life.
  • Use sustainable materials and substitute recycled plastic, aggregates, glass and other recyclable/less harmful packaging.
  • Treat renewable-energy investment as part of strategy, not an optional add-on; industrialisation depletes resources and generates major emissions.

B. Supply chain and last-mile delivery

  • Supply-chain environmental performance is a multi-tier process involving logistics and warehouse management.
  • Reduce energy use and waste across the chain and increase renewable-energy use.
  • Efficiency can lower the carbon footprint and improve return on investment.
  • Last-mile delivery is the final distribution stage and a significant source of supply-chain emissions in India.
  • Use EVs, CNG vehicles, biofuel/non-fossil-fuel transport, green logistics and green shipping for people and goods.

C. Environmental philanthropy

  • Environmental causes should enter organisational strategic plans.
  • Customers pay attention to charitable contributions.
  • Select a cause aligned with the industry and its material impacts; e.g., a building company might support organisations making renewable energy affordable.

D. Water

  • Measure consumption first; measurement identifies waste and can reduce costs.
  • Conserve water because shortages and freshwater availability are serious concerns.
  • Consider sound office water-filtration systems and prioritise employee health, which can also strengthen engagement.

E. Lighting and buildings

  • Replace inefficient incandescent lamps with LED and other efficient lighting.
  • Green buildings use eco-friendly construction materials and sustainable operation/maintenance.
  • Features cited: solar PV, solar water heating, energy-efficient lighting and good insulation.
  • LEED means Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design; it is third-party verification associated with energy efficiency, lower emissions and improved overall environmental performance.

F. Waste management

  • Reducing trash at source is only one part; handling and disposal are equally important.
  • Establish clear waste-removal objectives.
  • Involve employees personally because participation improves effectiveness.
  • Properly manage plastic and industrial waste.

G. Transportation and work practices

  • Provide/encourage electric vehicles, EV charging, CNG vehicles, electric buses/scooters and other green-fuel options.
  • Teleworking can reduce greenhouse-gas emissions.
  • Sustainable transport can also generate cost savings.

H. Ecological innovation

  • Technology affects every firm regardless of size/industry.
  • Ecological innovation means applying technology to sustainable objectives: finding energy alternatives, improving industrial methods, reducing pollution and advancing EVs and other solutions.
  • Innovation can transform both operations and the business model.

11.3 Environmental best-practice summary

Green energy, sustainable supply chains, conscious water use, sustainable packaging, efficient lighting, green buildings, employee-led waste systems, green transport, innovation, pollution prevention and transparent measurement should work together. An isolated donation cannot compensate for continuing operational harm.

12. Strategic CSR and measurement

Modern CSR differs from traditional charity:

Traditional/limited approach Modern strategic approach
Reactive donations or one-off help Integrated with mission, governance and operations
Input focus: money spent Outcome/impact focus: change created
Shareholder/profit dominated Stakeholder and triple-bottom-line view
Often separate from business Uses business capabilities to solve social/environmental problems
Publicity or compliance orientation Long-term value, accountability, transparency and innovation

A useful responsibility progression from the quiz is:

meet legal obligations → reduce the firm’s environmental impact → support communities through voluntary philanthropy

The ordering matters: a company should make its own operations responsible before treating charity as evidence of responsibility. Modern CSR additionally covers responsible supply chains and products, ethical sourcing and pricing, employee wellbeing, diversity and inclusion, human rights, anti-corruption, honest marketing, responsible risk management, circular-economy/product design and climate responsibility. Philanthropy is voluntary social contribution—not legal compliance, tax avoidance, a replacement for government or merely profit maximisation. It may also include disaster relief, hospitals, shelters, local schools and employee volunteering.

Data is essential because CSR, like other managerial activity, should be evidence based. Useful assessment areas include:

  • Inputs: money, employee time and resources
  • Outputs: people reached, grants, equipment, training, projects
  • Outcomes: improved health, education, emissions, waste, water or livelihoods
  • Long-term impact: durable social/environmental change
  • Governance: committee oversight, audit, disclosures and stakeholder participation
  • Business value: trust, employee indicators, reputation, risk, innovation and ROI

Illustrative metrics named in the quiz material include:

  • Environmental: carbon emissions, water consumption, energy efficiency and waste recycled.
  • Social: employee satisfaction, diversity, community investment, volunteer hours and training hours.
  • Governance: ethics compliance, board diversity, transparency and audit performance.

Revision link: Tesla and interconnected CSR

The live class recalled Tesla as a warning against judging a company on one dimension. It may be an environmental champion, yet alleged/problematic work-related practices can damage the social dimension, create backlash and contribute to exclusion from an ESG index. CSR/sustainability is interconnected; excellence in “E” does not erase weakness in “S” or “G.”

13. GreenLine Textiles case/quiz

13.1 Case logic

The quiz material concerns a textile company whose operations harm the environment/community, with a decision-maker (Lina) choosing between a comprehensive response and a limited/short-term response.

The primary concepts are:

  • Minimising environmental and social harm
  • Individual as well as organisational ethical responsibility
  • Ethical leadership and tone at the top
  • Transparency plus corrective action
  • Resource conservation
  • Community support and stakeholder trust
  • Long-term strategic value versus short-term cost control

13.2 Why Option A is correct

Option A combines four mutually reinforcing actions:

  1. Honest reporting: disclose impacts accurately and acknowledge poor performance.
  2. Install a filter: address pollution at its source rather than merely managing reputation.
  3. Conserve water/resources: improve long-term efficiency, reduce environmental footprint and protect scarce resources.
  4. Support affected communities: address social consequences, repair relationships and rebuild trust.

Together these form a holistic CSR strategy: environmental prevention + operational improvement + ethical governance + stakeholder engagement.

13.3 Why the limited option fails

A response focused only on short-term costs, superficial disclosure, publicity or isolated compensation:

  • Does not eliminate the pollution source
  • Leaves environmental and social harm in place
  • Treats CSR as cost/compliance rather than responsibility
  • Risks greenwashing
  • Weakens trust and increases legal, operational and reputational risk

Transparency alone is insufficient. Honest reporting is necessary, but without meaningful corrective action it does not minimise harm.

13.4 Stakeholders and long-term benefits

Internal stakeholders include Lina/management, employees and owners/investors. External stakeholders include customers, affected communities, regulators, suppliers, future generations and the environment.

Ethical action can produce customer retention, talent attraction, employee retention, reputation, stronger stakeholder relations, reduced risk and sustainable business success. Reputation is strategic capital: hard to build, easy to lose and capable of affecting sales, recruitment, partnerships and legitimacy.

13.5 Greenwashing

Greenwashing is creating a misleading impression of environmental responsibility without corresponding substantive performance. Warning signs include selective disclosure, claims unsupported by data, donations used to distract from core harm and reporting without remediation.

14. High-yield comparisons

Pair Essential distinction
Ethics vs law Ethics asks what is right and may exceed the legal minimum; law states enforceable minimum duties
Ethics vs governance Ethics centres values and right conduct; governance centres structures, oversight, procedure, disclosure and accountability; they overlap
CSR vs philanthropy CSR is broad and embedded; philanthropy is voluntary giving/support and is only one form/component
Corporate citizenship vs CSR Citizenship is the broader responsible identity and overall impact; CSR comprises programmes/practices that advance it
CSR vs ESG CSR is the responsibility/strategy; ESG categorises and measures environmental, social and governance performance
Transparency vs accountability Transparency discloses information; accountability accepts responsibility, scrutiny and consequences/action
Output vs impact Output is immediate activity/result; impact is lasting change
Carbon footprint vs carbon neutrality Footprint is emissions generated; neutrality is the net-zero end-state after reduction/balancing
Charity vs strategic CSR Charity gives help; strategic CSR integrates stakeholder needs, operations, governance, measurement and long-term value
Environmental philanthropy vs environmental responsibility Donation supports a cause; responsibility first prevents/reduces the firm’s own harm

15. Exam preparation

15.1 Likely long-answer questions and model-answer points

Q1. Explain the relationship between business ethics and CSR.

  • Define ethics, business ethics and CSR.
  • Explain CSR’s ethical, social and environmental scope.
  • Present honesty, integrity, fairness, transparency, accountability and stakeholder respect.
  • Include ISO 26000’s seven principles.
  • Explain stakeholder trust and benefits to reputation, employees, customers, investors, risk and innovation.
  • Conclude that ethical CSR is genuine action beyond compliance.

Q2. Distinguish ethics from governance in CSR.

  • Define both and state that they overlap.
  • Ethics: values/right conduct/beyond-law dimension.
  • Governance: structures, committees, oversight, audit, reporting, accountability and stakeholder interest.
  • Apply the classroom examples: honest poor-result reporting, quarterly committee meetings, community participation and annual-report disclosure.
  • Explain the SIA nuance: one act may be governance when mandated and ethical when it exceeds law.

Q3. Define corporate citizenship and explain its five stages.

  • Define company as responsible member of society.
  • Explain overall rights, obligations, privileges and impacts.
  • Relate citizenship to CSR and ESG.
  • Describe elementary, engaged, innovative, integrated and transforming stages as increasing involvement.
  • Add Microsoft/Amazon and Tata Trust examples.
  • State benefits: trust, differentiation, employees, reputation and transformation.

Q4. What is corporate philanthropy? Explain forms and benefits.

  • Define voluntary social contribution and distinguish it from CSR.
  • Explain matching gifts, volunteer grants, employee/board stipends, community grants, skill volunteering, sponsorships, scholarships and donations.
  • Benefits: engagement, retention, talent, morale, brand equity, customer confidence, government/community relations and long-term sales/profit.
  • Add caveat: philanthropy cannot substitute for remedying core operational harm.

Q5. Discuss government’s and society’s role in CSR.

  • Government: regulation, policy, information, awareness, capacity building, incentives, procurement, reporting and monitoring.
  • Two-pronged approach: model triple bottom line internally and require ethical/reporting commitments from partners.
  • Society: expectations, participation, dialogue, scrutiny, need identification and accountability.
  • Apply to health (COVID/PPE, affordable medicines) and education (digital facilities, social innovation).

Q6. Explain the environmental aspects of CSR.

  • Define environmental CSR, sustainability, carbon footprint and neutrality.
  • Use triple bottom line.
  • Cover energy, materials/plastic, supply chain/last mile, packaging, water, lighting, LEED/green buildings, waste, transport, technology and philanthropy.
  • Explain operational and business benefits: lower pollution, resource protection, efficiency, ROI, innovation and legitimacy.

Q7. Compare CSR regimes in India, China and the USA.

  • Define regime and warn that CSR is country-specific.

15.4 Common confusions and examiner traps

  • “CSR = charity.” Incorrect; charity/philanthropy is only one component.
  • “Legal compliance automatically means ethical CSR.” Incorrect; compliance is the minimum.
  • “Ethics and governance are completely separate.” Incorrect; they overlap, but differ in emphasis.
  • “Transparency alone solves harm.” Incorrect; disclosure must be followed by prevention/remediation.
  • “A strong environmental record proves total ESG excellence.” Incorrect; Tesla illustrates interdependence across E, S and G.
  • “Spending proves impact.” Incorrect; distinguish inputs/outputs from outcomes and long-term impact.
  • “Environmental donations offset continuing pollution.” Incorrect; first minimise own operational harm.
  • “Corporate citizenship and CSR are exact synonyms.” In these lectures, citizenship is the broader umbrella/identity.
  • “ISO 26000 is a certification.” Treat it as guidance, not a certifiable standard.
  • “All countries use India’s mandatory 2% model.” Incorrect; regimes differ.
  • “Life trustees or fixed terms are always superior.” Incorrect; evaluate continuity, accountability, power and stakeholder interest; hybrid arrangements may balance them.

15.5 Answer-writing template

For a 10–15 mark answer:

  1. Open with a precise definition.
  2. State the central principle or framework.
  3. Explain dimensions in organised subheadings/table form.
  4. Add at least one lecture example or case.
  5. Present a limitation/caveat or distinction.
  6. Conclude with stakeholder impact and sustainable long-term value.

16. One-page rapid revision

  • CSR joins ethics + society + environment + economic viability.
  • Ethical CSR is honest, fair, transparent, accountable, stakeholder-oriented and beyond compliance.
  • ISO 26000: accountability, transparency, ethical behaviour, stakeholder interests, law, international norms, human rights.
  • Governance provides the structures and oversight that translate commitments into accountable practice.
  • Corporate citizenship is the wider responsible-company identity; stages: elementary → engaged → innovative → integrated → transforming.
  • Philanthropy is voluntary welfare-oriented support; it improves relationships and morale but cannot replace operational responsibility.
  • Government regulates, informs, incentivises, sets standards and monitors; society participates, scrutinises and identifies needs.
  • Triple bottom line = people, planet, profit.
  • Environmental CSR covers carbon, renewable energy, packaging, supply chains, water, buildings, waste, transport and innovation.
  • India uses mandatory CSR duties for eligible firms under Section 135; other national regimes differ.
  • GreenLine best response: report honestly + stop pollution at source + conserve water + support communities.
  • The exam’s recurring logic is: prevent harm, engage stakeholders, govern transparently, measure impact and create long-term value.
