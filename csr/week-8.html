1. Week overview and central theme

Week 8 connects five core areas of CSR and sustainability:

  1. Environment, natural resources and ecosystems
  2. United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
  3. Environmental protection and conservation
  4. Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) and environmental clearance
  5. Environmental and sustainability terminology

The central argument is that CSR is concerned with ethics, society and the environment. Economic or social development cannot be separated from environmental protection. Human beings depend on environmental systems, so environmental conservation is both a practical necessity and a moral obligation.

Instructor emphasis: Understand the concepts so that protection can be designed intelligently: understand → protect → preserve → sustain life. Awareness must result in responsible behaviour, not remain factual knowledge.

Sustainability is the broad idea of meeting present needs without compromising future generations’ ability to meet their needs. ESG is related but is a measurable framework through which environmental, social and governance performance may be assessed. CSR can fund and implement sustainability solutions.

2. Environment

2.1 Definition

The environment is the sum total of living and non-living elements and their effects that influence human life. It can also be described as the complex of physical, chemical and biotic factors—such as climate, soil and living organisms—that act upon an organism or ecological community and determine its form and survival.

The term also includes the aggregate of social and cultural conditions influencing an individual or community. Thus, environment is not merely “nature”; it is a broad, interacting set of natural, physical, chemical, social and cultural surroundings.

2.2 Components

Component Meaning Examples from the lessons
Biotic Living components, visible or microscopic Humans, animals, birds, insects, reptiles, plants, forests, fisheries, marine life, bacteria, viruses and fungi
Abiotic Non-living physical and chemical components Air, clouds, dust, water, water vapour/humidity, land, soil, sand, sunlight, rocks and mountains

Both are indispensable. Environmental functioning arises from their combination and interaction.

2.3 Environment versus ecosystem

Environment Ecosystem
Broad and encompassing—everything around us A specific functional unit within the broader environment
May include natural, physical, chemical, social and cultural surroundings Requires interaction between biotic and abiotic elements in a defined place
Example: Mother Earth Examples: forest, pond, river system, or the space around a tree

Mathematically, an ecosystem may be thought of as a subset of the environment. A forest is an ecosystem; Earth is the broader environment. This distinction is practically important because different ecosystems require different conservation responses. A Himalayan or icy ecosystem cannot be protected with exactly the same programme as a tropical forest.

3. Natural resources

3.1 Meaning

Natural resources are naturally occurring materials or supplies drawn from Earth that exist independently of human action and are useful to human beings. They include food, water, materials for buildings and clothing, fertilizers, metals and geothermal power. Geothermal power is energy obtained from heat within Earth, including geothermally heated water.

3.2 Five major natural resources stressed in the lesson

Air

  • Clean air is essential for plants, animals and human survival.
  • Oxygen is needed by humans; oxygen and carbon dioxide are important environmental gases.
  • Industrial and automobile emissions contaminate the atmosphere.
  • Important pollutants mentioned: sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide.
  • Measures must reduce air pollution and preserve clean air.

Water

  • More than 70% of Earth is covered by water, but the lesson states that only about 2% is freshwater.
  • Access to safe drinking water and clean water is a serious concern.
  • Use must be regulated and water bodies—rivers, lakes, ponds, seas and oceans—preserved.
  • Water pollution, including oil and plastic pollution, is an alarming problem.

Soil

  • Soil consists of particles and nutrients and enables plants to grow.
  • Soil contamination and erosion undermine agriculture, biodiversity and food security.
  • Sustainable soil management includes controlling chemicals, preventing erosion and maintaining fertility.

Iron and other minerals

  • Iron occurs as a mineral and is used in weapons, transport and buildings; steel is produced from iron.
  • Other abiotic mineral resources include copper, gold and silver, which become materials and alloys.
  • Mineral stocks are limited and non-renewable, so efficiency and recycling matter.

Forests

  • Forests supply clean air, preserve ecology and contain extensive biodiversity.
  • Afforestation means planting trees; deforestation means cutting them down.
  • Forest protection conserves species and their habitats and supports broader ecological processes.

3.3 Classification by origin

Biotic resources Abiotic resources
Derived from living/biological components Non-biological environmental components
Forests and forest products, crops, birds, animals, fish and marine life Land, water, air and minerals such as iron, copper, gold and silver
Generally described in the lesson as renewable because they reproduce or replenish, provided use remains within regenerative limits Generally limited and non-renewable because they cannot reproduce; shortages arise when stocks are exhausted

Caveat: “Biotic” does not guarantee practical renewability. Overharvesting can destroy the regenerative capacity of forests, fisheries or other biological resources. Sustainable use requires harvesting no faster than regrowth.

3.4 Sustainable resource-management rule

Consumption or harvesting must not exceed nature’s regenerative capacity.

Forestry example:

  1. Estimate existing forest stock and annual net regrowth.
  2. Set the allowable harvest at or below net regrowth.
  3. Harvest while ensuring regeneration.
  4. Monitor the forest and adjust future limits if ecosystem health declines.

Monitoring is indispensable: it shows resource availability, regeneration and ecosystem health and signals when harvesting must be reduced.

4. Ecosystems

4.1 Definition and functioning

An ecosystem is a community of life forms interacting with non-living components in a specific functional unit. For example, a tree and its surrounding soil, water, organisms, birds, insects and microorganisms form an interactive unit.

4.2 Types

Natural ecosystem Artificial ecosystem
Occurs naturally Created or substantially maintained by humans
Forests, deserts, lakes, ponds, mountains, grasslands, rivers and oceans Aquariums, crop fields, gardens, parks and zoos

National parks generally cover broader protected areas. Wildlife sanctuaries focus especially on animal conservation and may be known for particular animals such as tigers or elephants. Other related conservation concepts include biosphere reserves and biodiversity hotspots.

4.3 Ecosystem services

Ecosystems are “natural infrastructure” and provide:

  • Water purification
  • Flood control
  • Carbon storage and oxygen-related ecological cycling
  • Soil conservation
  • Temperature moderation
  • Support for rainfall cycles
  • Habitat and biodiversity protection

Wetland example: A wetland filters polluted runoff before it reaches a river. Draining it removes natural filtering capacity, so more contaminants reach the river, water quality declines and biodiversity and flood-control benefits are lost.

5. Sustainable development and the United Nations SDGs

5.1 Sustainable development

Sustainable development means meeting the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability or needs of future generations. It balances three dimensions:

  • Economic development
  • Social well-being
  • Environmental protection

This does not mean stopping development. It means assessing impacts, preventing avoidable harm, using resources efficiently and protecting future capacity.

5.2 Origin and purpose of the SDGs

  • The United Nations is an intergovernmental organization headquartered in New York. Its central purposes include international peace and security and friendly relations among nations.
  • The SDGs were adopted by the UN in 2015 as 17 integrated goals intended to create a more sustainable and peaceful world by 2030. The recorded lesson occasionally mistranscribes the target year as 2013; the live session clarifies 2030.
  • They are a global blueprint for dignity, peace and prosperity for people and the planet, now and in the future.
  • Their predecessor was the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), launched in 2000 for achievement by 2015. The instructor said China achieved the MDGs while many countries did not and that design/conceptual weaknesses helped motivate the SDGs.

5.3 Why adopt SDGs?

  • Encourage social mobilization: organize communities to identify problems, act and find solutions.
  • Create constructive peer pressure among political leaders.
  • Turn networks of expertise, knowledge and practice into action.
  • Mobilize stakeholder networks across countries.
  • Coordinate shareholders, customers, clients, communities, governments and other stakeholders around shared goals.
  • Prioritize those furthest behind and end poverty, hunger, AIDS and discrimination against women and girls.

5.4 Design principles

Principle Meaning Illustration
Universal Applies to all countries regardless of income, development status or geography A high-income country must still report on inequality, climate and consumption
Inclusive Benefits must reach vulnerable, marginalized and disadvantaged groups “Leave no one behind”
Integrated Action on one goal affects other goals; social, economic and environmental outcomes are linked Education affects work and inequality; clean water affects health; renewables affect climate and growth
Action-oriented Goals must lead to plans, measurable targets, indicators and monitoring Progress is demonstrated through evidence, not slogans

The live session summarized the orientation as people, planet and prosperity and stressed that this is a classification, not three additional goals.

5.5 Goal–target–indicator framework

Goal → Targets → Indicators

Each goal contains targets; each target has indicators. Indicator performance demonstrates target achievement, and target achievement builds toward the goal. The instructor referred broadly to several thousand indicators (the transcript renders the number unclearly as “3300 or 400”); the exam-worthy point is the hierarchy, not the uncertain number.

5.6 The 17 goals and the lesson’s explanations

  1. No Poverty: Millions live below the poverty line. Aquaculture and fisheries were said to support livelihoods for roughly 250 million people worldwide. Supporting farmers and fishing communities and improving fish health can create more ethical and profitable income.
  2. Zero Hunger: Aquaculture supports nutrition and basic income, particularly in developing countries. Better fish welfare reduces mortality and ecosystem degradation and improves farming effectiveness.
  3. Good Health and Well-being: Fish is a primary protein source for millions in developing countries; environmental quality also directly affects public health.
  4. Quality Education: Many children lack primary education or educational facilities. CSR already focuses on education, but access must be universal and of good quality.
  5. Gender Equality: Empower women; eliminate violence against women and girls and forced marriage; secure equal opportunities for girls.
  6. Clean Water and Sanitation: Access to water is a human right. Clean drinking water, sanitation and effective water management are essential.
  7. Affordable and Clean Energy: Reduce reliance on fossil fuels—coal, petroleum and natural gas—because they pollute and are exhaustible. Promote affordable solar photovoltaic, wind, biomass and hydel energy and reduce energy consumption.
  8. Decent Work and Economic Growth: Development should create decent work while supporting economic progress.
  9. Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure: Functioning, resilient infrastructure and innovation are foundations of successful communities.
  10. Reduced Inequalities: Reduce income inequality; promote social, economic and political inclusion; end discrimination.
  11. Sustainable Cities and Communities: Sustainable consumption and urban/community systems should sustain current and future livelihoods.
  12. Responsible Consumption and Production: Increase welfare while minimizing feed/material demand and waste; use resources efficiently and reduce waste at source.
  13. Climate Action: Address greenhouse gases, global warming, shifting weather patterns, glacier melt and sea-level rise. The instructor warned that some places may be submerged as sea levels rise and stressed public awareness of climate impacts.
  14. Life Below Water: Efficient production helps aquatic systems retain natural balance. Oceans are essential to human existence, major carbon sinks and sources of food, medicines, biofuels and other products. Reduce marine, oil and plastic pollution; plastic is especially harmful to fish and aquatic organisms. Green shipping is preferable to polluting conventional practices.
  15. Life on Land: Conserve terrestrial ecosystems, forests, wildlife and biodiversity; combat desertification and reverse land degradation. Protect national parks, wildlife sanctuaries and biodiversity hotspots. Terrestrial ecosystems sustain life and were described as contributing roughly half of global GDP.
  16. Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions: Promote peace globally and maintain institutions capable of fair, effective action.
  17. Partnerships for the Goals: SDGs require commitment, collaboration and cooperation among countries—developed and developing—and among governments, the private sector and civil society.

5.7 Can companies achieve SDGs faster than governments?

The instructor rejected a simplistic government-versus-company framing:

  • Government supplies policy, laws, regulation, public funds and enabling facilities—for example, establishing a carbon market, renewable-energy policy or emission standards.
  • Companies execute at scale—for example, build solar plants and adopt low-carbon technology.
  • Companies may accelerate SDG achievement, but cannot replace government.

The correct framing is government and company, not government versus company.

6. Environmental protection, conservation and preservation

6.1 Definitions and distinction

  • Environmental protection/conservation: Any measure taken to conserve, maintain or preserve the state of the environment, including reducing pollutants and avoiding overuse of natural resources.
  • Environmental preservation: Practices intended not to alter the environment—to keep it unchanged.
  • Conservation: Wise and efficient use; for non-renewable resources it particularly involves recycling and other efficiency measures.

Protection may therefore combine pollution control, careful resource use and strict preservation in sensitive areas.

6.2 Why it matters

  • Supports human and non-human life and health
  • Protects biodiversity and ecosystem stability
  • Preserves natural resources and livelihoods
  • Reduces economic and legal risk
  • Sustains future generations
  • Reflects gratitude and moral responsibility toward the environment on which humanity depends

Environmental harm produces consequences: “every negative action has a reverse return action.” Pollution and ecosystem damage ultimately return as illness, resource shortages, economic costs, climate risks and lower quality of life.

6.3 Main methods

  • Reduce air, water, land, marine and noise pollution.
  • Control industrial and vehicle emissions, including NOx, SO₂, CO and CO₂.
  • Use clean energy and reduce fossil-fuel dependence.
  • Improve energy and resource efficiency.
  • Apply circular-economy principles: reduce, reuse, recycle and recover value.
  • Prevent pollution at the source through cleaner technologies, processes and materials.
  • Protect forests, wildlife, aquatic organisms, wetlands and water bodies.
  • Afforest and reduce deforestation.
  • Avoid ocean plastic and unsafe plastic burning: marine plastic threatens aquatic organisms, while toxic fumes from burning plastic were linked in the lesson to respiratory illness and cancer risk.
  • Promote the 3Rs—reduce, reuse and recycle—along with waste management, energy-saving devices and rainwater harvesting.
  • Use policy, behavioural change and technology together.
  • Deploy CSR and sustainability funds to support green technology and conservation.

6.4 Prevention versus cleanup

Cleanup/remediation Prevention
Responds after contamination occurs Stops or reduces pollution at source
Often expensive and incomplete Usually reduces risk and waste more efficiently
Damage may already affect health/ecosystems Protects people and ecosystems before harm

Modern environmental management therefore prioritizes prevention while retaining remediation for existing damage.

6.5 Integrated environmental management in business

An integrated programme combines:

  • Energy management: lower consumption, emissions and bills.
  • Waste management: reduce waste; improve segregation, recycling and resource recovery.
  • Pollution prevention: redesign materials and production processes.
  • Green procurement: select environmentally responsible suppliers and sustainable materials.

Benefits include operational efficiency, lower costs, compliance, reduced liabilities, stakeholder trust, community support, reputation and long-term business resilience.

An Environmental Management System (EMS) is the structured organizational system for monitoring and continuously improving energy use, waste, emissions and resource consumption.

7. Technology, nature and environmental protection: live-session cases

7.1 The Bhopal “algae tree”

The instructor discussed a solar-powered air-purification installation in Bhopal, described as an algae tree. Living microalgae absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen, applying the basic photosynthetic idea in a technological system.

Claims and caveats from the discussion:

  • It can purify air and reduce pollution rapidly in urban locations.
  • It may be replicated where CO₂ pollution is severe.
  • A comparison of one installation with roughly 20–25 mature trees was presented only as an average technical statistic; estimates vary among tree species and systems.
  • It can complement trees in polluted urban spaces but cannot reproduce the full ecological functions of a tree.

A natural tree:

  • Supports birds, nests, microorganisms and other organisms.
  • Is itself a small ecosystem.
  • Supports biodiversity, habitat, cooling, soil-erosion prevention and rainfall cycles.
  • Has cultural and social meaning beyond gas exchange.

Core conclusion: Technology may improve air quality, but it cannot replace nature’s broad ecological infrastructure; it can only complement it. Nature is infrastructure for sustainable living.

Financial versus economic feasibility

Pure financial cost-benefit calculation may make an algae installation appear expensive. Wider economic assessment includes avoided respiratory illness, reduced out-of-pocket health expenditure and better urban living standards. The Delhi/Lodhi Park anecdote illustrated that even people exercising in open spaces may suffer respiratory problems under severe pollution. Thus broader social and health benefits can make such technology economically worthwhile even if narrow financial returns are weak.

New technology can also produce unforeseen side effects, compared by the instructor to medicine, so evidence, monitoring and further research are necessary.

7.2 Nuclear energy debate

Question posed: Is nuclear energy clean, environmentally sustainable, or harmful?

  • Nuclear power has low greenhouse-gas emissions and was described by the instructor as a clean/green technology in that limited emissions sense.
  • It becomes environmentally sustainable only if safety can be assured.
  • Catastrophic risk, nuclear waste and inability to guarantee safety complicate the claim.
  • Examples/anecdotes: the Kudankulam debate and benefits to South India; an article by Dr A. P. J. Abdul Kalam; Chernobyl; and the Japanese nuclear accident (the transcript appears to mean Fukushima).
  • India’s three-stage nuclear programme was attributed to Homi J. Bhabha. The instructor said India had advanced to a second-stage breeder approach in which less uranium can generate more plutonium, which can be used for further electricity generation. The detailed nuclear technology was not required; the conceptual point was technological efficiency plus safety.

Instructor’s balanced position: Environmental protection needs technological solutions grounded in realistic conditions. Clean energy, resource efficiency and green technology are necessary, but technology must not create unacceptable environmental harm.

Citizens, businesses and government share responsibility, and CSR/sustainability funds can finance safer innovation and mitigation.

8. Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)

8.1 Definition

Environmental Impact Assessment is a systematic process through which the likely impacts of a proposed project or development are identified, predicted and evaluated before the project is installed or approved.

It considers positive and negative:

  • Environmental impacts
  • Social and socioeconomic impacts
  • Cultural impacts
  • Human-health impacts
  • Economic implications

It is a prerequisite for major projects because prevention and design changes are more effective before construction. A related concept mentioned in the live class is Social Impact Assessment (SIA), which focuses specifically on social consequences.

8.2 Three underlying analytical components

  1. Collect appropriate information to decide whether an EIA is necessary.
  2. Predict changes in environmental parameters under the proposed project and compare them with the likely without-project situation.
  3. Record and analyze actual changes, supporting monitoring, implementation review and audit.

8.3 Full EIA procedure

  1. Screening: Examine project type, investment scale, location and development category to decide whether a full EIA is required.
  2. Scoping: Identify significant impacts, the zone of influence, monitoring needs, data requirements and alternatives. Expert Appraisal Committees or State Expert Appraisal Committees may assist.
  3. Baseline-data collection: Establish existing air, water, land, biodiversity, social and health conditions. Baseline data is the reference against which change is predicted and later measured.
  4. Impact prediction: Forecast both positive and negative consequences.
  5. Alternatives assessment: Compare alternative sites, designs, technologies and the no-project option across environmental attributes.
  6. Mitigation and Environmental Management Plan (EMP): Specify actions to prevent, minimize, correct or compensate for negative impacts.
  7. EIA report: Document data, predictions, alternatives, impacts, mitigation and monitoring arrangements.
  8. Public hearing/consultation: Inform and consult people near the project site; record concerns, local knowledge, health/resource fears and views.
  9. Decision-making/appraisal: The competent authority, experts, consultants and project proponent review the evidence and decide to approve, approve with conditions, or reject.
  10. Review: Assess the EIA report’s adequacy and implications for project implementation.
  11. Monitoring and EMP implementation: Compare actual outcomes against predictions and ensure conditions are followed.
  12. Post-completion environmental audit: Review environmental performance and operations after implementation/completion, analogous to an audit of financial performance.

8.4 Benefits

  • Better project design
  • More informed and environmentally sensitive decisions
  • Better integration with environmental and social settings
  • Reduced environmental damage and legal risk
  • Cost savings and potentially shorter permission/approval time
  • Greater accountability and transparency
  • Positive contribution to sustainability
  • Better achievement of financial and socioeconomic objectives
  • Benefits to both the organization and the environment

8.5 Problems and limitations

  • Difficulty securing adequate, meaningful public participation
  • EIA may not be integrated at the key decision points
  • Inconsistency in deciding which developments require EIA
  • Poor understanding or use of baseline data
  • Inadequate integration of biophysical, social and economic dimensions
  • Weak or faulty data can produce faulty decisions
  • A detailed procedure is useful only when conducted carefully and followed by implementation and monitoring

8.6 EIA, monitoring and audit

EIA Environmental monitoring Environmental audit
Primarily before approval During construction/operation Usually after operations begin or after completion
Predicts future impacts Measures actual air, water, emissions, waste and ecosystem conditions Reviews compliance and environmental performance
Supports project decision/design Tests predictions and management controls Identifies gaps and improvements

9. Environmental clearance

9.1 Meaning and purpose

Environmental clearance is the formal review and authorization process used to assess a project’s likely effect on the environment and habitat and minimize negative impacts before it proceeds. EIA supplies much of the evidence used in clearance.

9.2 Environmentally sensitive locations/projects mentioned

Clearance is especially important for projects affecting or near:

  • Mountain and hilly areas
  • Coastal areas, coral reefs and estuaries (where freshwater rivers/streams meet the ocean)
  • Archaeological and religious monuments
  • Gulf regions
  • Biosphere reserves
  • National parks, wildlife sanctuaries and national wetlands
  • High-seismicity zones
  • Tribal settlements
  • Areas of scientific or geological interest
  • International borderlands
  • Airports and defence establishments
  • Lakes and other ecologically sensitive areas

9.3 Four principal clearance stages

  1. Screening: Confirm that the selected location complies with guidelines and determine the project category.
  2. Scoping: The relevant Expert Appraisal Committee/State Expert Appraisal Committee defines issues and required studies.
  3. Public hearing/consultation: A mandatory step in the lesson’s account. Affected residents express concerns and views about the proposed project; these must be considered.
  4. Environmental appraisal: Once required documents, data and hearing records are received, the project is assessed from the environmental angle. The lesson states appraisal should be completed within 90 days. The ministry/competent government authority then approves, conditionally approves or rejects the proposal.

Environmental appraisal decides the project’s future, so it is the final and crucial stage.

9.4 EIA versus environmental clearance

  • EIA is the study/process that predicts and evaluates impacts and mitigation.
  • Environmental clearance is the administrative/regulatory decision process relying on EIA, expert appraisal and public consultation.
  • They are linked but not interchangeable.

10. Glossary

Term Exam-ready meaning and notes
Atmosphere Gaseous envelope surrounding Earth and held in place by gravity.

Biodiversity Biological diversity—the richness and variation of species and communities.

11. Greenfield Cooperative case study

11.1 Setting and stakeholders

Greenfield Cooperative is a small farming community seeking healthy land, safe food and sustainable livelihoods.

  • Town council: planning, regulation and coordination.
  • Maya, lead farmer: represents farmers and converts plans into workable farming practices.
  • Tomas, soil technician: provides scientific guidance, measures soil/environmental conditions and evaluates results.
  • Workshop owners: must improve industrial-waste handling.

11.2 Problem–impact map

Problem/source Effects
Excessive pesticide use Soil contamination, harm to pollinators/beneficial organisms, biodiversity loss, food contamination and groundwater risk
Heavy chemical-fertilizer dependence Nutrient imbalance, reduced soil quality/fertility, chemical dependency and water contamination
Poor industrial-waste disposal by a nearby workshop Waste accumulation, contamination and risk to fields and water
Plastic litter carried into fields after storms Persistent soil contamination, harm to organisms, food-safety and ecosystem risk

11.3 Four governing principles

  1. Conservation of resources: Protect soil and groundwater and use compost/organic matter to sustain natural nutrient cycles.
  2. Pollution prevention: Reduce pollutants at source rather than clean them later.
  3. Biodiversity protection: Reduce pesticides that harm non-target species, especially pollinators and soil organisms.
  4. Intergenerational equity: Preserve fertile soil, clean water and productive capacity for future farmers.

11.4 Integrated action plan

  • Reduce pesticide use.
  • Replace part of the chemical-fertilizer requirement with compost.
  • Establish a simple waste-collection point so stormwater does not carry plastic into fields.
  • Require the workshop to improve waste storage, handling and disposal.

Compost is both a soil intervention and circular-resource strategy: it recycles organic material, adds organic matter, improves soil structure and nutrient cycling, and reduces industrial-input dependence.

The case uses a systems approach. Farm inputs, workshop waste, stormwater, soil, crops, food safety, biodiversity and groundwater are interconnected; treating only one symptom would not solve the system-wide problem.

11.5 Six-month pilot

  • Conduct the plan on three fields for six months.
  • A pilot limits risk, creates local evidence and permits refinement before expansion.
  • Monitor soil and groundwater, farming practice and contamination events.

Success indicators:

  1. Measurable improvement in soil fertility
  2. No new food-contamination incidents
  3. No signs of polluted groundwater near trial sites

These indicators directly answer the original problems, making them strategically valid. “No new incidents” is a prevention measure, while improved fertility is a positive restoration measure.

11.6 Benefits and lesson

  • Environmental: healthier soil and water, reduced pollution, stronger biodiversity.
  • Social: safer food, improved health and community trust.
  • Economic/agricultural: sustained productivity, lower long-run risk and reduced chemical dependence.
  • Future: continued farming capacity and intergenerational fairness.

Case sequence: Prevent pollution → conserve resources → protect biodiversity/ecosystems → measure results → preserve environmental quality for future generations.

12. Exam preparation

12.1 High-value answer frameworks

“Explain environmental responsibility in business”

Use: Assess → Prevent → Reduce → Transition → Monitor

  • Assess impacts through EIA and baseline data.
  • Prevent pollution at source.
  • Reduce energy, material and waste intensity.
  • Transition to clean energy, green technology and circular practices.
  • Monitor, report and audit results.

Add benefits: efficiency, cost savings, compliance, trust, resilience and sustainability.

“Explain responsible development using EIA”

Use: Study impacts → identify risks → compare alternatives → mitigate → consult public → appraise → approve/reject → monitor/audit.

Example: For a road near a river, do not “build first, repair later.” Establish baseline river conditions, compare routes, design erosion/runoff controls, consult local communities, impose approval conditions and monitor construction.

“Analyze a sustainability case”

Use the chain:

Source/activity → environmental pathway → impact → stakeholder → prevention/mitigation → measurable indicator → long-term benefit.

12.2 Likely long-answer questions and model points

1. Define environment and distinguish it from an ecosystem.

  • Environment: totality of living/non-living and physical, chemical, social and cultural surroundings.
  • Biotic and abiotic components with examples.
  • Ecosystem: specific functional unit involving interaction.
  • Environment is broader; ecosystem is a subset.
  • Forest/tree-space example.
  • Practical importance: ecosystem-specific conservation design.

2. Explain natural resources and their sustainable management.

  • Definition and human uses.
  • Air, water, soil, iron/minerals and forests.
  • Biotic versus abiotic classification.
  • Renewable versus non-renewable caveat.
  • Pollution and depletion threats.
  • Harvest ≤ net regrowth; forestry sequence and monitoring.

3. Discuss the SDGs’ purpose, design and application.

  • UN adoption in 2015; 17 goals; target 2030; MDG predecessor.
  • Integrated social-economic-environmental balance.
  • Universal, inclusive, integrated, action-oriented.
  • Goal–target–indicator hierarchy.
  • People, planet, prosperity orientation.
  • Examples of interconnected goals.
  • Government policy plus company execution and partnership.

4. Explain environmental protection and why prevention is preferable to cleanup.

  • Define protection, conservation and preservation.
  • Moral, ecological, health and economic need.
  • Pollution types and resource overuse.
  • Prevention reduces source, cost, exposure and irreversible harm.
  • Measures: clean energy, efficiency, circular economy, afforestation, biodiversity and water protection.
  • EMS, reporting and monitoring.

5. Define EIA and explain its process, benefits and problems.

  • Pre-decision systematic assessment.
  • Environmental, social, economic, cultural and health dimensions.
  • List the full procedural stages in correct order.
  • Explain baseline and without-project comparison.
  • Benefits: better design, informed decisions, lower harm/legal risk/cost, transparency and sustainability.
  • Problems: weak participation, late integration, inconsistent screening, poor baseline data and fragmented analysis.

6. Explain the environmental-clearance process and its relation to EIA.

  • Clearance as authorization; EIA as evidence-generating assessment.
  • Sensitive locations/examples.
  • Screening, scoping, mandatory public hearing and appraisal.
  • Expert committees, stakeholder views, 90-day appraisal statement.
  • Approve, conditionally approve or reject.

7. Can technology replace nature? Discuss with the algae-tree case.

  • Explain microalgae/photosynthesis and urban air-cleaning value.
  • Scalability and pollution reduction.
  • Statistical tree-equivalence caveat.
  • Trees’ biodiversity, habitat, cooling, soil and rainfall functions.
  • Complement, not substitute.
  • Financial versus wider economic feasibility and health-cost savings.
  • Need monitoring for unintended consequences.

8. Is nuclear power environmentally sustainable?

  • Low operational greenhouse emissions support the “clean” label.
  • Sustainability depends on safety, waste, accident risk and governance.
  • Kudankulam benefits/debate; Chernobyl and Fukushima warnings.
  • Three-stage Indian programme/breeder concept as technological efficiency example.
  • Conclude conditionally: useful clean-energy option only with robust safety and environmental management.

9. Apply sustainability principles to Greenfield Cooperative.

  • Map four sources to impacts.
  • Four principles: conservation, prevention, biodiversity, intergenerational equity.
  • Four actions: pesticide reduction, compost, collection point, workshop handling.
  • Explain systems approach and stakeholder roles.
  • Six-month/three-field pilot and three indicators.

12.5 Common confusions and corrections

Confusion Correct understanding
Environment and ecosystem are synonyms Environment is broad; an ecosystem is a specific interacting unit within it.
All biotic resources are automatically renewable They renew only if exploitation remains within regeneration limits.
Sustainability means no development It means development within social and ecological limits.
SDGs are only for developing countries They are universal and apply to all countries.
SDGs are a list of aspirations only Their action architecture is goal → target → indicator.
Companies or governments can achieve SDGs alone Government enables/regulates; companies execute and accelerate; partnership is essential.
EIA studies only environmental chemistry It integrates environmental, social, cultural, health and economic effects.
EIA and environmental clearance are identical EIA is assessment; clearance is the regulatory decision process.
Public hearing is mere formality It brings affected-community concerns and local knowledge into appraisal.
EIA ends with approval EMP implementation, monitoring and audit follow approval.
Monitoring and audit are the same as EIA EIA predicts before approval; monitoring measures ongoing outcomes; audit reviews performance/compliance.
Technology can replace trees if it absorbs more CO₂ CO₂ exchange is only one service; natural trees support entire ecosystems.
Nuclear is simply clean or simply dirty It is low-carbon but sustainability is conditional on safety, waste and risk governance.
Narrow financial cost equals social value Economic feasibility also includes avoided illness, public welfare and environmental benefits.

12.6 One-minute final revision

  • Environment = broad totality; ecosystem = specific biotic–abiotic interaction.
  • Sustainability = present needs + future capacity.
  • Resources must be used no faster than regeneration; non-renewables demand efficiency and recycling.
  • SDGs: 17, adopted 2015, target 2030; universal, inclusive, integrated, action-oriented; goal → target → indicator.
  • Protection combines conservation, preservation, pollution prevention, clean energy, efficiency and biodiversity protection.
  • Technology complements nature; it does not reproduce all ecosystem services.
  • EIA comes before approval: screen → scope → baseline → predict → alternatives/mitigation/report → public hearing → decision/review → monitor/audit.
  • Clearance: screening → scoping → public hearing → appraisal.
  • Government makes the enabling system; companies execute and accelerate; partnership delivers SDGs.
  • Greenfield: prevent pollution, conserve resources, protect biodiversity, measure outcomes and protect future generations.

Closing instructor message: “Be aware, be responsible, act for the present”—while protecting the life chances of coming generations.
