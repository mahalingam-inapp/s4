Exam-oriented master notes

These notes consolidate all substantive content in the six recorded lessons, the live class, the quiz material, and the GreenLine Tech case. Repeated transcript phrases and platform noise have been removed, while definitions, distinctions, examples, frameworks, questions, caveats, and instructor emphasis have been retained.

Week 1 introduces:

  1. The concept and importance of CSR

  5. Types of CSR

The course moves from theoretical foundations to industrial practice, sectoral insights, practical frameworks, climate change, climate finance, ESG and reporting frameworks (including the Global Reporting Initiative), and other emerging sustainability topics.

The instructor repeatedly stressed that CSR must be understood systematically, not as an unconnected list of terms. The central idea is that business can help society, conserve the environment, create stakeholder value, and remain economically viable.

2. Meaning and concept of CSR

2.1 Core definition

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) is the responsibility or commitment of a business to operate ethically, contribute to economic development, protect the environment, and improve the quality of life of employees, their families, customers, communities, and society.

The World Business Council for Sustainable Development-oriented definition discussed in class presents CSR as the duty of business to contribute to economic development while improving the quality of life of the workforce, their families, and broader society.

In simple terms, CSR means:

  • Being responsible and accountable for business conduct
  • Creating a positive impact on society and the environment
  • Reducing or preventing negative social and environmental effects
  • Considering all stakeholders in business decisions
  • Building lasting, mutually beneficial relationships between business and society

2.2 The three-part balance

CSR balances three interconnected dimensions:

Dimension Main concern Typical examples
Economic Business viability and development Profit, efficiency, growth, capital, return on investment
Social Welfare, equity, and quality of life Employee welfare, community development, education, healthcare, inclusion
Environmental Protection and conservation Lower pollution, renewable energy, efficient resource use, waste reduction

This balance is also expressed through the Triple Bottom Line:

  • People — social welfare and equity
  • Planet — environmental protection
  • Profit — economic viability

2.3 CSR is integrated, not isolated

CSR is a broad management concept, not a silo or a separate charitable activity. A company integrates economic, social, ethical, and environmental considerations into:

  • Business operations
  • Stakeholder interactions
  • Strategy and mission
  • Production and supply chains
  • Human-resource policies
  • Marketing and customer relationships
  • Finance, governance, and reporting

Therefore, CSR applies across every major business function.

2.4 CSR is more than charity

Philanthropy and donations are part of CSR, but they do not exhaust it. Responsible business begins by:

  1. Preventing harm
  2. Acting lawfully and ethically
  3. Treating stakeholders fairly
  4. Reducing environmental damage
  5. Then adding voluntary community initiatives

Illustrations from the quiz:

  • A hospital cannot compensate for violating patient consent merely by holding free medical camps. Genuine CSR includes ethical care, privacy, safety, and outreach.
  • A software company cannot dispose of e-waste irresponsibly and claim CSR only because it conducts coding workshops. It must manage e-waste and data ethically as well.
  • A factory cannot offset bribing inspectors simply by planting trees. Ethical governance and sound environmental operations must accompany philanthropy.

2.5 Voluntary in theory; context-specific in law

The instructor strongly emphasized this distinction:

  • In its theoretical and historical origin, CSR is a voluntary ethical commitment by business—often involving monetary and other resources—to improve society and the environment.
  • Whether CSR is legally mandatory depends on the country or regulatory regime. India and China were cited as contexts where qualifying companies face mandatory requirements.
  • Therefore, do not generalize the Indian legal position into a universal definition of CSR.

2.6 Corporate citizenship

A company is not isolated from society. Like a citizen, it has obligations toward the community and environment in which it operates. Corporate citizenship therefore means acting accountably and conducting business responsibly, with concern for social and environmental welfare.

3. Main focal areas and links with management

3.1 Ethics

Ethics means doing what is right, fair, honest, and responsible. Ethical CSR includes:

  • Fair labour practices
  • Honest and responsible marketing
  • Fair stakeholder treatment
  • Transparency
  • Avoiding bribery and corruption
  • Preventing exploitation
  • Timely, respectful disclosure of operating concerns

3.2 Society and philanthropy

Philanthropy means action for the welfare or goodwill of people. CSR seeks to transform and benefit society through:

  • Charity and donations
  • Community outreach
  • Employee volunteering
  • Education and healthcare programs
  • Local and global community support
  • Rural development, infrastructure, skill development, poverty reduction, and disaster relief

Public- and private-sector companies spend large sums—described in class as lakhs and crores of rupees—on CSR across education, healthcare, finance, IT, and other sectors.

3.3 Environment and sustainability

CSR responds to pollution, climate change, global warming, declining resources, toxic waste, and greenhouse-gas emissions. Relevant practices include:

  • Energy management and energy efficiency
  • Renewable energy, including solar and wind
  • Sustainable sourcing and procurement
  • Waste reduction and recycling
  • Resource conservation
  • Afforestation/tree planting
  • Cleaner production and lower emissions
  • Environmental impact assessment
  • Net-zero and carbon-management efforts

The effects of environmental degradation extend beyond humans to animals, birds, plants, aquatic organisms, ecology, and entire ecosystems.

3.4 Marketing

CSR is linked to marketing because it affects:

  • Brand reputation and goodwill
  • Customer satisfaction and loyalty
  • Customer retention and sales
  • Differentiation from competitors
  • Green marketing and eco-friendly positioning

When comparable products compete in a saturated market, a credible CSR record can make a firm the preferred option. This is a long-term advantage based on trust, unlike a temporary price cut.

3.5 Human-resource management

CSR includes respect for employees, equality, diversity, inclusion, fair pay, safe conditions, meaningful work, training, good incentives, work–life balance, and protection from abuse.

Employees may stay with a company not only because of salary but also because of its public image, purpose, workplace climate, and contribution to society and the environment. Higher morale can produce:

  • Better productivity and efficiency
  • Higher engagement and job performance
  • Lower absenteeism
  • Greater satisfaction and retention
  • Stronger employer branding

The instructor described empathy as placing oneself “in their shoes” and thinking from another person’s perspective.

3.6 Risk management

Social and environmental risks can affect long-term business growth. CSR helps identify, reduce, and manage these risks and is therefore a risk-mitigation strategy, not merely a social expense.

4. Why CSR is important

4.1 Sustainability and environmental protection

Responsible businesses reduce their negative environmental footprint and move toward renewable energy, energy-efficient technology, sound energy management, sustainable procurement, conservation, and recycling.

Sustainable development means meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

4.2 Positive social impact

CSR enables companies to give back to communities, address social needs, support charitable and outreach programs, encourage employee volunteering, and improve individual and family wellbeing.

4.3 Stakeholder trust and loyalty

Continuous engagement builds mutual commitment, trust, loyalty, and productive relationships with employees, shareholders, customers, clients, suppliers, government, competitors, NGOs, and communities.

4.4 Brand reputation and competitive advantage

A socially responsible business can stand out from competitors and build a positive image. Goodwill improves customer confidence, supports customer retention, and can increase sales.

4.5 Employee retention and engagement

People prefer organizations that treat employees well and have a positive social and environmental reputation. Happy, motivated employees are more productive and more likely to remain.

4.6 Sales, revenue, and capital growth

Purpose-led companies may be viewed more positively by customers. Loyalty and a wider customer base can improve sales, profitability, capital growth, and shareholder returns.

4.7 Ethical conduct

CSR encourages doing the right thing, adopting fair labour and marketing practices, and avoiding corruption and bribery.

4.8 Long-term viability

CSR links business prosperity with the long-term health of people and the planet. A firm must remain financially resilient so that its social and environmental commitments can continue.

5. Benefits of CSR — consolidated list

  1. Employee engagement: A strong sense of purpose improves morale and involvement.
  2. Productivity and efficiency: Motivated employees work more effectively; absenteeism may fall.
  3. Revenue and shareholder value: Successful CSR can improve returns and long-run financial results.
  4. Support for local and global communities: CSR provides resources and a platform for social change.
  5. Customer retention and loyalty: Responsible brands earn trust and repeat business.
  6. Talent attraction, development, and retention: CSR strengthens employer branding.
  7. Public trust: Ongoing funding and visible adherence to principles reinforce social legitimacy.
  8. Capital growth: Reputation and customer reach can improve profitability and business growth.
  9. Competitive advantage: Strong initiatives create an edge in saturated markets.
  10. Relationship building: CSR forges mutually beneficial stakeholder relationships.
  11. Risk management: It reduces exposure to social, environmental, governance, and reputational risks.
  12. Greater sustainability: Renewable energy, efficient systems, and sustainable practices conserve ecology and resources.
  13. Internal efficiency: Sound CSR requires good financial management, productivity, planning, and coordination.

Benchmarking

Benchmarking means comparing a company or its practices with best-in-class technology, an industry standard, or a reference company. CSR and ESG performance may be compared against competitors and accepted standards to identify gaps and strengthen competitive position.

6. Basic elements of CSR

The recorded lesson organizes CSR around four closely connected elements, each with internal and external aspects.

6.1 CSR strategy

Strategy is a long-term planning process, usually directed by top management. CSR strategy is the starting point of organizational CSR practice.

Requirements:

  • Clear definition and future-oriented goals
  • Alignment with mission, vision, objectives, and business strategy
  • Support from senior executives and people passionate about CSR
  • Participation across management levels and employees
  • Priorities, budgets, selection criteria, responsibilities, and performance measures

Internal aspect: Build solid organizational support before defining and executing goals.

External aspect: The strategy acts as a positioning document showing stakeholders and the public the company’s responsible-business ambition and goals.

Without a strategy, projects may compete for funds, departments may act independently, employees receive mixed signals, and CSR becomes inconsistent.

6.2 CSR management system

The management system turns strategy into execution.

Internal aspect:

  • Gather feedback on how the strategy has been received
  • Engage more people in the business
  • Form a core team with vision, knowledge, and commitment
  • Pool knowledge and prepare a sound management plan
  • Use teamwork and communication to execute the strategy

External aspect: The system is the company’s vehicle for gaining recognition for its CSR activities. However, confidentiality remains important: a company may communicate useful information without revealing excessive sensitive detail.

6.3 CSR reporting / sustainability reporting

After strategy and execution comes reporting. A CSR report systematically records a company’s initiatives, results, and performance, commonly annually and sometimes quarterly.

A report may cover:

  • Objectives, policies, and projects
  • Environmental performance
  • Employee initiatives
  • Community work
  • Governance practices
  • Stakeholder engagement
  • Performance indicators and future goals

Purposes and benefits:

  • Meaningful disclosure to stakeholders
  • Transparency and accountability
  • Investor and shareholder confidence
  • Public trust
  • Evaluation of whether the team created something worth reporting
  • Support for customer loyalty and financial performance

Internal challenge: collecting complete, signed-off, current numbers and text from multiple people can be difficult and may create friction.

CSR reporting is not simply advertising, internal discussion, or bare legal compliance. Together, strategy, the management system, and reporting become the visible “face” of the organization’s CSR.

6.4 Stakeholder engagement and communication

This element holds all other CSR elements together. Without continuous engagement, building a strategy or report has little value.

Good engagement requires:

  • Communication and rapport
  • Trust and loyalty
  • Transparency
  • Balance among stakeholder interests
  • Avoidance or resolution of conflict
  • Involvement in decisions and implementation

Internal aspect: Be transparent within the company so teams understand one another’s activities.

External aspect: Engage NGOs, local communities, interest groups, customers, clients, government, and other important parties. External engagement is often the hardest part.

6.5 Process sequence

CSR strategy → CSR management system → CSR reporting → continuous stakeholder engagement and communication throughout

7. Stakeholders

A stakeholder is an individual, group, or entity with a direct or indirect interest (“stake”) in a business, who may be affected by its decisions or influence their implementation.

Internal stakeholders External stakeholders
Board of directors Customers and clients
CEO and top managers Suppliers and partners
Managers and supervisors Government and regulators
Employees and workers Competitors
Other company staff NGOs and interest groups
Shareholders/investors (often treated as internal in the lectures) Local community and broader society

Other stakeholder-oriented considerations include the environment and future generations.

Stakeholder expectations

  • Shareholders/investors: Return on investment, financial health, transparent reporting
  • Employees: Fair pay, safe work, meaningful jobs, protection from abuse, development opportunities
  • Customers: Lawful, safe, quality products and honest communication
  • Government: Taxes, compliance, and responsible cooperation
  • Communities: Development, welfare, infrastructure, education, healthcare, and lower pollution
  • Suppliers: Fair and ethical relationships
  • Environment/future generations: Conservation and lower long-term harm

8. Characteristics or features of CSR

8.1 Responsible corporate citizenship

Corporations should behave as responsible citizens, be accountable for their effects on society, and respond to social and environmental problems.

8.2 Public and consultative orientation

Businesses must make their interactions with society positive and productive. They should consult neighbours and citizens affected by corporate operations and maintain transparency.

8.3 Environmental responsiveness

Growing public knowledge about resource depletion, toxic waste, pollution, and global warming pressures companies to improve. CSR responds through conservation, renewable energy, environmental assessment, net-zero practices, and lower harm.

8.4 Protection of clients

Corporate systems should monitor company–client interactions so that no one is cheated or exploited. Client projects rest on mutual trust and responsible completion of obligations.

8.5 Protection of staff and labour

Employees have a right to fair pay, safe conditions, meaningful work, good incentives, and freedom from mistreatment or exploitation. Employee morale is vital to organizational success.

8.6 Integration into mission and charters

CSR and environmental responsibilities should be embedded in the company’s mission, vision, goals, charters, and strategic plans. This institutionalizes safety, good governance, charity, and environmental stewardship.

8.7 Senior-management guidance

Directors and senior executives should guide CSR programs, goal-setting, governance, sustainability reporting, ethical practices, and sustainable product development. Their role must extend beyond an annual review.

8.8 Competitive and strategic value

CSR can function as:

  • A competitive advantage
  • A marketplace driver
  • A risk-mitigation strategy
  • A source of opportunity and long-term viability

Leading companies explain how CSR makes money, reduces risk, supports survival, and creates opportunity. Programs need business plans, goals, success criteria, evaluation, and managerial accountability.

8.9 Long-term view

A company’s impact may outlive current executives. CSR should benefit today’s shareholders and future generations; sustainability is therefore a long-term planning concern.

9. Five types of CSR responsibility

9.1 Economic responsibility

The primary economic responsibility is to be profitable and financially sustainable. Profit enables the business to survive, compete, and participate in legal, ethical, philanthropic, and environmental responsibilities.

Key idea: Profit is not the opposite of CSR; it is a foundation that makes continuing CSR possible.

9.2 Legal responsibility

Businesses must follow laws, regulations, and guidelines that codify fair practice. Examples:

  • Paying income and other taxes
  • Fulfilling corporate legal obligations
  • Protecting employees from abuse and treating them fairly
  • Supplying products and services that meet legal requirements
  • Minimizing environmental damage
  • Observing industrial-relations and labour protections

Human-rights violations, pollution, and failure to meet statutory duties illustrate legal risk.

9.3 Ethical responsibility

Ethical responsibility goes beyond minimum law and requires fair treatment of every stakeholder. Examples:

  • Transparent and honest leadership
  • Fair practices toward investors, employees, suppliers, and customers
  • Benefits beyond minimum legal requirements
  • Diversity training for hiring managers
  • Reduction of environmentally harmful practices
  • Ethical and sustainable supplier selection
  • Timely, honest disclosure of operational concerns
  • No fraud, bribery, corruption, exploitation, or other improper conduct

9.4 Philanthropic responsibility

Philanthropic responsibility aims actively to make society and the world better. Examples:

  • Charity and nonprofit donations
  • Fundraising for local needs
  • Scholarships and grants
  • Disaster relief
  • Educational and healthcare facilities
  • Corporate charitable trusts or foundations
  • Research funding and tree planting
  • Working with partners whose values align with the company’s mission

9.5 Environmental responsibility

Environmental responsibility aims to reduce business harm to nature through:

  • Lower pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions
  • Energy management and lower consumption
  • Renewable sources such as solar and wind
  • Efficient machines and sustainable production
  • Reduce, reuse, and recycle practices
  • Reduced plastic use (plastic harms aquatic life and causes land pollution)
  • Afforestation and research support
  • Conservation of ecology and ecosystems

A useful responsibility hierarchy from the quiz

For scenario questions, the quiz presents this progression from minimum obligation to highest voluntary contribution:

  1. Legal: comply with laws
  2. Ethical: act fairly beyond the law
  3. Environmental: reduce impact beyond minimum requirements
  4. Philanthropic: voluntarily support community causes

Economic viability underpins the ability to sustain all levels.

13. CSR, ESG, and sustainability — do not confuse them

The instructor repeatedly said these concepts overlap but are not always identical. All belong to the larger sustainability debate.

Concept Core meaning Scope/time Typical expression
CSR Ethical, traditionally voluntary business commitment and use of resources for society, environment, community, and stakeholder engagement Often initiative- or program-oriented; legal status depends on context Donations, volunteering, community projects, responsible practices
ESG Measurement/benchmarking of environmental, social, and governance performance Quantified and comparative Scores, standards, peer benchmarks, investor screening
Sustainability Broad transformation of business toward long-term environmental, social, and economic health/value creation Long-term and systemic Business-model change, SDGs, climate action, enduring value

ESG in more detail

Data or government agencies may assess a company against standards and peers, then produce an overall or component score.

  • Environmental: emissions, energy, waste, resource practices
  • Social: employee and community wellbeing, human rights, stakeholder treatment
  • Governance: how the company is directed and controlled—for example, women directors, independent directors, human-rights safeguards, and fair employee treatment

Investors use ESG information to decide where to place funds and to avoid companies with poor practices.

Sustainability in more detail

Sustainability protects people and planet while also including economic/business health. It connects with the UN Sustainable Development Goals and the response to global challenges. CSR can be part of sustainability, but sustainability is broader than spending CSR funds.

IPL question discussed in class

A learner asked whether an IPL “green dot ball” tree-planting initiative is CSR. The instructor’s cautious response was: if it contributes resources to social or environmental development, it can fit CSR, but it also aligns more broadly with sustainability. The instructor had not examined the initiative in detail and therefore avoided an absolute classification.

14. Practical company examples

14.1 Salesforce and the 1–1–1 model

Salesforce was used to demonstrate profit with purpose and the movement from a purely philanthropic model toward broader stakeholder-oriented sustainability.

The model commits:

  • 1% of product
  • 1% of equity
  • 1% of employee time

Employees therefore contribute personally through volunteering, not only through corporate money. The lecture cited more than 5 million volunteer hours, support for 40,000+ NGOs, and benefits to many students as visible evidence of impact.

Exam use: If asked to explain profit with purpose, cite Salesforce—the firm earns profit while deploying equity, products, and employee capability for societal development.

Instructor trivia: the then Salesforce India head was identified through a clue as a former State Bank of India chief (Arundhati Bhattacharya). The point was to make the corporate example memorable.

14.2 Tata Group and CSR materiality

Bombay House was identified as the Tata Group headquarters. Tata was presented as a widely respected Indian example of societal commitment and stakeholder trust.

The lecture’s materiality idea was that products creating positive outcomes can also outperform financially. What matters is not only that profit is made, but how it is made and how capital and the business model create social value.

Key arguments:

  • Tata’s association with trusts and societal uplift creates a strong social impression.
  • The instructor stated that a very large share—described as about two-thirds—supports societal uplift.
  • This creates expectations of honest conduct and lower social/environmental harm.
  • Trust in turn supports products, reputation, revenue, margins, and effective capital deployment.
  • A company needs a strong financial position to continue social and sustainability expenditure.

Thus financial wellbeing and societal commitment can reinforce each other. The instructor acknowledged that students might disagree with the evaluation and invited critical views.

14.3 GreenLine Tech case

Situation

GreenLine Tech, a small electronics manufacturer led by plant manager Aisha, must choose between:

  • Option A: $20,000 machine upgrade; reduces emissions, conserves resources, and saves about $8,000 per year in energy and waste costs.
  • Option B: $12,000 annual employee-wellbeing program; improves fairness and working conditions but gives smaller direct savings.

Evaluation

Criterion Option A: machine Option B: wellbeing
Economic viability Strong; recurring savings Moderate; indirect productivity/retention gains
Environmental protection Strong; lower emissions and resource use Weak/directly limited
Social equity Indirect; stability and future funding Strong and direct
Long-term sustainability Very high Moderate

Option A has an approximate payback period of $20,000 ÷ $8,000 = 2.5 years.

Recommended decision

The quiz recommends Option A because it creates the best combined balance:

  1. Maintains economic viability through recurring savings
  2. Directly reduces environmental impact
  3. Builds financial capacity for future employee and community programs

Option B remains socially valuable, but in this constrained choice it meets fewer pillars directly.

Main lesson

CSR requires balance, not the maximization of only one aim. Profit enables continuing environmental and social programs; environmental efficiency can improve profit; those savings can then support social equity. Every decision in production, finance, marketing, HR, and operations has wider stakeholder consequences.

15. CSR materiality and financial sustainability

CSR materiality

Material CSR issues are those significant enough to influence both stakeholders and business/financial outcomes. Positive-impact products and responsible conduct can build trust, outperform financially, strengthen margins, and justify effective capital deployment.

Financial sustainability

CSR must survive difficult business periods. It should be integrated into long-term planning, supported by sustainable cash flow, and treated as an investment rather than an optional publicity expense.

Poor approaches include:

  • Spending without financial planning
  • Running programs that threaten company survival
  • Depending only on publicity for funding
  • Treating CSR as unrelated one-off donations

Best approach: incorporate CSR into a sustainable business model so profit, impact, and stakeholder value reinforce one another.

16. Nation building through CSR

CSR can contribute to national development through:

  • Education and skill development
  • Healthcare
  • Rural and community development
  • Infrastructure
  • Poverty reduction
  • Environmental protection

Nation-building CSR requires continuing community development, not merely obeying laws or making occasional donations.

National climate context discussed in the live class

The instructor linked CSR and sustainability to India’s nationally determined contributions (NDCs), describing new targets for 2031–35:

  • 47% reduction in emissions intensity
  • 60% installed power capacity target (the lecture context implies a non-fossil/clean-capacity share)
  • 3.5–4.0 billion tonnes of carbon sink through forests

Corporate transition to eco-friendly business, corporate finance, government policy, and citizen awareness were described as a “downward filtration” through which national targets are achieved. The instructor also noted that India had achieved some earlier targets ahead of time and left identification of those targets as a learner exercise.

Even an employee, household member, friend, or colleague can contribute by applying and spreading sustainability awareness.

Source caveat: These figures are preserved as stated in the class transcript. For a current legal or policy answer, verify the latest official wording and units.

17. Indian mandatory-CSR discussion

A learner asked: Is CSR mandatory only for corporate companies? What about educational institutions and government companies?

The instructor answered:

  • Mandatory status is context-specific.
  • In India, qualifying companies registered under the Companies Act, 2013 must undertake CSR.
  • Government or education-related entities may also be covered if they are registered as companies and meet the applicable conditions.
  • Eligible companies must spend 2% of the average net profits of the preceding three years on CSR.
  • The lecture intended to cite the classic thresholds of ₹500 crore net worth, ₹1,000 crore turnover, or ₹5 crore net profit, though parts of the automatic transcript garble “net worth,” “equity,” and some zeroes.
  • The instructor added that changes affecting MSMEs were being discussed/not finalized at the time of the session.

Exam caveat: Distinguish the general concept (traditionally voluntary ethical commitment) from the Indian statutory regime (mandatory for eligible companies). For a law-specific answer, use the exact provision and current official thresholds supplied in the syllabus or statute.

18. Evolution and major theoretical debates

The modern charitable or voluntary corporate-donation tradition was traced to the 1950s. The live class then highlighted three major positions.

18.1 Milton Friedman — shareholder primacy

In a highly influential newspaper article (the instructor placed it around 1972 and mentioned The New York Times with a note of uncertainty), Friedman argued that the social responsibility of business is to serve shareholders by maximizing returns, rather than taking over the social role of governments and NGOs.

Important qualification: profit should be earned in a moral, legal, and non-exploitative way. Proper salaries and compensation benefit employees; taxes and contributions enable government action. Thus the view did not authorize illegal or unethical profit-making—it rejected a separate corporate social role beyond lawful profit maximization.

Environmental concern was rising during this era through Silent Spring (the transcript says “Rachel Gerson,” evidently referring to the author discussed in class), the DDT debate, and international environmental conferences.

18.2 R. Edward Freeman — stakeholder theory

In the 1980s (the lecture suggested around 1982), Freeman argued that every stakeholder matters to business development. A business is not isolated from society or the environment. It must consider everyone with a direct or indirect interest in its activities.

Do not confuse:

  • Milton Friedman → shareholder profit maximization
  • R. Edward Freeman → stakeholder theory

18.3 Michael E. Porter — shared value

The lecture associated Porter with a later shared-value approach: companies should develop products, services, and business models that are economically justifiable and also improve societal wellbeing.

Comparison

View Central question Main answer
Friedman Whose interest should business primarily serve? Shareholders; maximize lawful, ethical returns
Freeman Who matters to business? All direct and indirect stakeholders
Porter/shared value Can commercial and societal value coexist? Yes; design profitable activity that also solves social problems

This progression helps explain the movement from philanthropy and shareholder primacy toward stakeholder engagement, strategic CSR, materiality, and sustainability.

19.2 Likely short-answer questions and model points

1. Define CSR.

  • Ethical and accountable conduct by business
  • Contribution to economic development
  • Improvement in employee, community, and societal wellbeing
  • Environmental protection and reduced harm
  • Stakeholder orientation
  • Balance of economic, social, and environmental dimensions

2. Why is CSR important?

  • Sustainability and environmental conservation
  • Positive social impact
  • Ethical conduct
  • Stakeholder trust
  • Brand reputation and differentiation
  • Employee engagement and retention
  • Customer loyalty and sales
  • Risk management and long-term viability

3. Explain the Triple Bottom Line.

  • People, Planet, Profit
  • Social equity, environmental protection, economic viability
  • All three must be balanced
  • Use GreenLine Tech as an application

4. Differentiate internal and external CSR.

  • Internal: employees and processes inside the firm—wages, safety, training, inclusion, work–life balance
  • External: communities, customers, suppliers, environment—development programs, conservation, safety, ethical sourcing
  • A comprehensive program may combine both

5. Distinguish CSR, ESG, and sustainability.

  • CSR: ethical/voluntary commitment and responsible initiatives; legal mandate is context-specific
  • ESG: measurable and benchmarked E/S/G performance, useful to investors
  • Sustainability: broader, long-term transformation of economic, social, and environmental health
  • They overlap but are not synonyms

6. What is CSR reporting?

  • Systematic disclosure of objectives, projects, environmental/social/governance performance, indicators, and future goals
  • Usually annual; may also be quarterly
  • Promotes transparency, accountability, investor confidence, and public trust
  • Not merely advertising or compliance

7. What is stakeholder engagement?

  • Continuous involvement of affected or influential parties in decisions and implementation
  • Requires communication, transparency, trust, and balance
  • Essential to strategy, execution, and reporting

8. Explain corporate citizenship.

  • Company is a member of society, not an isolated entity
  • Has obligations similar to a citizen
  • Must be accountable for social and environmental effects

9. What is profit with purpose?

  • Earning profit while using business resources and capabilities for societal benefit
  • Salesforce 1–1–1 model is the principal example

10. Explain CSR materiality.

  • Identifies social/environmental issues important to stakeholders and financial performance
  • Positive outcomes, trust, revenues, margins, and capital use may reinforce one another
  • Tata example

  2. Legal—obey laws and regulations
  3. Ethical—fair conduct beyond law

  5. Environmental—reduce ecological harm
  6. Explain interdependence and give examples

D. “Explain CSR as a source of competitive advantage.”

  • Trust and brand reputation
  • Customer loyalty and retention

  • Investor confidence
  • Stakeholder relationships
  • Risk reduction
  • Benchmarking and differentiation
  • Credibility requires integration into operations, not publicity alone

E. “Trace the evolution of CSR thought.”

  • Philanthropic roots from the 1950s
  • Friedman: lawful shareholder-return maximization
  • Freeman: stakeholder theory
  • Porter: shared value
  • Modern movement toward CSR, ESG metrics, materiality, and broad sustainability

19.4 Scenario-answer method

For case questions:

  1. Identify affected stakeholders.
  2. Test legal compliance first.
  3. Test ethical fairness beyond law.
  4. Assess social and environmental effects.
  5. Check economic viability and long-term funding.
  6. Prefer an integrated solution over publicity or a one-off donation.
  7. Link the recommendation to People, Planet, and Profit.
  8. State measurable outcomes, reporting, and management accountability.

19.5 Common confusions and corrections

Confusion Correct understanding
CSR means charity Charity is only the philanthropic component; CSR begins with responsible core operations
CSR is always mandatory Its theoretical origin is voluntary; legal mandate depends on jurisdiction
CSR and sustainability are identical CSR is narrower/program-oriented; sustainability is broader and long-term
ESG is another name for CSR ESG is principally a measurement and benchmarking framework
Profit conflicts with CSR Profit provides the financial foundation for continuing CSR
Legal compliance alone is enough Law is the minimum; ethical conduct goes beyond it
Environmental responsibility belongs to one department It must shape design, production, procurement, investment, logistics, and waste decisions
CSR reporting is advertising It is systematic, accountable disclosure of performance
Shareholders are the only relevant group Stakeholder theory includes all groups directly or indirectly affected
Friedman endorsed profit at any cost His argument required morally and legally permissible, non-exploitative profit-making
Freeman and Friedman are the same theorist Milton Friedman = shareholder primacy; R. Edward Freeman = stakeholder theory
A high-impact program need not be affordable Financial viability is necessary for sustained impact

20. Final integrated takeaway

CSR is a long-term approach to responsible business, not a stand-alone donation program. It requires lawful and ethical conduct, fair stakeholder treatment, economic resilience, environmental conservation, social welfare, strategic planning, execution, transparent reporting, and continuous engagement. The best CSR creates mutual value: society and the environment benefit, while the company gains trust, capable employees, loyal customers, lower risk, competitive strength, and lasting viability.

Review Week Augmentation

  • Treat CSR as stakeholder responsibility integrated into strategy, not as stand-alone philanthropy. Its value mechanisms include trust, stakeholder stability, social licence to operate and resilience, but these are possible long-run effects rather than guaranteed profits.
  • CSR cannot compensate for weak operations, innovation, investment or management. It complements competent execution.
  • Keep the functional chain clear: CSR shapes responsible conduct; ESG measures and discloses performance; sustainable finance uses ESG information in capital allocation; green and climate finance narrow the use of capital further. This is an explanatory hierarchy, not a rigid legal taxonomy.
  • CSR stakeholders include employees, customers, communities, investors, suppliers, government and the natural environment.
