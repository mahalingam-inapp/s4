1. Week 1 at a glance

Week 1 establishes two foundations:

  1. How to learn and work effectively: time management, prioritization, reading, note-taking, memory, test preparation, focus, SMART goals, and active learning.
  2. How a business works: managerial roles, the business environment, organizational structure, production and service processes, marketing/sales, customer value, cross-functional coordination, entrepreneurship, and innovation.

The instructor’s recurring message is that a degree, idea, or busy schedule is not enough. Competence becomes visible through understanding, execution, customer value, and outcomes.

2. Managing time and priorities

2.1 Time management is a learned professional skill

  • An MBA normally represents a full-time workload. Trying to combine full-time work and a full-time MBA without reducing or restructuring something is likely to damage both.
  • Do not assume that four weekend hours are enough. Decide deliberately whether the programme is full-time or part-time and negotiate the required time with family and work.
  • A credential without ability is compared to holding a degree that says one is a musician: sooner or later people discover whether the skill is real.
  • Professionalism means doing the necessary work in front of you, then taking leisure. Responsibilities give leisure meaning; a life with no responsibility can itself become boring.

Illustrative weekly arrangement: reserve three weekday evenings (for example, 7:30–10:30 p.m.) for study, protect two weekday evenings for family, and allocate agreed weekend periods. The exact schedule is personal; the principle is visible, negotiated time blocks.

2.2 Planning, scheduling, and estimation

Time management has two parts:

  1. Draw the time out: put specific study periods into a calendar (Outlook or another calendar can be colour-coded by course/topic).
  2. Manage the reserved time: enter it with a task list, remove distractions, and execute rather than postponing.

Convert a block into concrete work. Example:

  • Read Chapter 3: estimated 60 minutes.
  • Take notes/answer questions: estimated 45 minutes.
  • Use the remainder of a two-hour block to review.

Writing the plan makes it psychologically real and primes the mind to act. Estimation improves through feedback: if a task estimated at 45 minutes takes longer, schedule more next time; with practice and fluency, it may later fall to 30 minutes. Also recognize dependencies—one task may need to be completed before another becomes easy.

2.3 Procrastination: causes and remedies

The basic cause is the preference for immediate pleasure over effort. At first, unfamiliar learning may feel unpleasant, especially when the motivation is only the eventual salary or credential.

Remedies emphasized:

  • Make the personal goal emotionally important: promotion, responsibility, income, capability, or becoming an entrepreneur.
  • Replace negative self-talk (“I am not good”) with trained, constructive self-belief (“I am good; I can learn this”).
  • Organize a specific weekly schedule and topic plan.
  • Remove distractions before beginning.
  • Rest when the real problem is fatigue. The instructor’s example was a 15–20 minute afternoon nap that restored productive energy until about 6:30 p.m.
  • Reward yourself after completion.
  • Tell an accountability person whose opinion matters—not merely someone who will approve anything. A respected colleague can make a commitment more binding.

2.4 Distraction control and deep focus

  • The laptop is not necessarily the problem; WhatsApp, email alerts, sounds, calls, and task switching on it are.
  • Put the phone on silent or turn it off. Treat study as urgent enough to deserve two uninterrupted hours.
  • Disable alerts and, where needed, use headphones to block environmental noise.
  • Nothing important is normally lost by temporarily disconnecting from low-priority communication.
  • Notes taken during focused study prevent the useless last-minute cycle of rereading the book or replaying videos.

The live session sharpened this point: distractions are expensive. After an interruption, the brain may require roughly 20–23 minutes to regain full focus. If attention shifts every 10–15 minutes while building a pitch, product, plan, or idea, the learner/entrepreneur repeatedly touches fragments without developing a complete, strong result.

Focus improves:

  • Productivity: thought and work move faster.
  • Decision quality: pricing, features, service consistency, and customer strategy become clearer.
  • Quality of work: deeper thinking reveals small details; quality applies to products, services, communication, and customer experience.

2.5 Prioritization and self-management

Education must be treated as an important priority, not as whatever is done after all other activities. A valid reason might be wanting promotion, more money, greater responsibility, or a more interesting life. In early semesters, progress may begin in small pieces and accelerate as habits develop.

Distinguish:

  • Urgent from important.
  • Activity from progress.
  • Filling time from placing time where it produces an outcome or impact.

Being busy all day can produce no forward movement. A neat calendar and checklist may be only activity management. Entrepreneurial time management asks whether activity moves the venture in the right direction.

2.6 Time as an entrepreneurial “unfair advantage”

Money can be raised, skills learned, and teams hired; nobody can buy more than the same 24 hours. Two founders may have similar ideas, resources, and markets, yet the one who uses time better can win.

Time affects three stages:

  1. Decisions: avoid disguising indefinite delay as careful thought. Speed is not blind rushing; it is the habit of deciding at the right time.
  2. Execution: ideas can occur anywhere (even in the shower), but the first real step distinguishes imagination from entrepreneurship.
  3. Outcomes: the world generally rewards results, not effort alone. Balance speed and quality—moving too slowly loses opportunity, while moving recklessly harms quality.

Feature-choice example:

  • Option A: spend two weeks analysing two features and collecting opinions.
  • Option B: decide in two days, launch, and learn from feedback.
  • Neither is automatically wrong. The danger is that two weeks becomes four and four becomes eight. Use enough analysis for a responsible decision, then execute and learn.

2.7 Allocation–Efficiency–Consistency–Momentum framework

Element Meaning Diagnostic question/example
Allocation Put scarce time where it creates impact. With five startup hours today, what deserves them? Four hours perfecting a logo and none speaking to a customer may decorate an idea rather than build a business.
Efficiency Reduce friction so work moves smoothly. Unnecessary meetings, notifications, switching tasks, and spending 30 minutes repeatedly revising a simple email all consume effort without proportional progress.
Consistency Show up systematically and continue every day. The startup participant Ratish was discussed as someone whose progress required regular effort, meetings, follow-ups, planning, and decisions—not one day of work followed by several idle days.
Momentum The movement created when all three operate together. Allocation directs energy; efficiency keeps it moving smoothly; consistency keeps it running.

Consistency is the toughest part and is essentially discipline. The gym analogy: enthusiasm often survives days one to three and disappears on day four. Entrepreneurship requires maintaining the energy of the start.

3. Goals, reading, notes, memory, and exams

3.1 SMART goals

Letter Requirement Entrepreneurship examples and cautions
S — Specific State exactly what will improve; avoid “improve my business.” Identify the particular sales, product, service, or customer-experience change.
M — Measurable Make progress trackable. Increase sales from 100 units to 200 units.
A — Achievable Fit the goal to time, resources, skills, and stage. A venture beginning with 33 children cannot realistically begin at 10,000; 200 customers may be a credible nearer target.
R — Relevant Align with the venture’s broader purpose and real conditions. A sustainability goal should fit the organization, stakeholders, society, and long-term growth—not merely look attractive today.
T — Time-bound Fix a deadline. Raise a specified amount of capital by August/September 2026.

Complete example: Increase the cafe’s monthly sales by 20% over the next three months through combo offers and improved customer experience. It is clear, measurable, realistic, aligned, and time-limited. A clear goal creates focus; focus drives action; action drives results.

SMART goals organize work but do not by themselves remove distractions, ensure reflection, or prevent cramming.

3.2 Reading as an active process

  • Read with a purpose and questions rather than moving the eyes across text.
  • Preview headings and structure; identify what the chapter is trying to teach.
  • Relate new material to prior knowledge and practical business situations.
  • Pause, paraphrase, question, and test understanding.
  • Identify main ideas and supporting evidence instead of highlighting everything.
  • Rereading alone creates familiarity, which can be mistaken for mastery.

3.3 Note-taking and the Cornell method

Taking notes is part of understanding, not clerical copying. Handwriting encourages processing and summarization; typing can tempt verbatim copying.

Cornell page structure:

Area Position/function Contents
Title/topic Top Clear identity of the lecture/chapter.
Notes column Large right-hand area Main lecture notes, explanations, details, and examples.
Cue column Narrow left-hand area Keywords, concepts, questions, and retrieval prompts added during or after class.
Summary Bottom Brief synthesis of the page in the learner’s own words.

Use the cue column later to cover the notes and self-test. Benefits include organization, active recall, easier revision, comprehension, and long-term retention.

3.4 Active versus passive learning

Passive/surface learning Active/deep learning
Repeated reading, copying, memorizing, highlighting without thought, last-minute video replay Solving cases, applying theory, explaining to another person, questioning, retrieving without looking, reflecting, and comparing alternatives
Initially easy and fast Requires effort and deliberate practice
Familiarity and short-term recall Durable understanding, analysis, transfer, and decision ability

Reflection turns experience into knowledge:

  1. What happened?
  2. Why did it happen?
  3. Which concept/theory explains it?
  4. What should be done differently next time?

3.5 Chunking, memory, and revision

  • Break a large task into manageable focused units (the case uses 30-minute research blocks) with short breaks.
  • Chunking reduces cognitive overload because working memory is limited, makes progress visible, and reduces the feeling of being overwhelmed.
  • It is different from cramming: distributed sessions improve retention; an intense final burst may briefly increase recall but produces stress, weak understanding, and poor application.
  • Chunking alone is insufficient without spaced review, active recall, reflection, and long-term scheduling.
  • Test preparation should be continuous: create usable notes as the course proceeds, retrieve ideas without looking, practice applying concepts, and avoid relying on last-minute exposure.

4. What managers do

Management work can be grouped into three broad categories covering most managerial activity.

4.1 Informational roles

  • Monitor/seek information: learn what is happening in operations, customers, competitors, journals, markets, seasons, and society.
  • Disseminate information: tell colleagues what they need to know.
  • Spokesperson/information provider: answer questions such as a product’s price or communicate results.

Education develops the ability to describe situations well and, importantly, to know what questions to ask.

4.2 Interpersonal roles

  • Figurehead/formal role: people expect a manager to behave appropriately and authentically.
  • Leader: sometimes push, more often encourage, and make it easier for subordinates and colleagues to succeed.
  • Liaison/networker: maintain links across people so a call or request can get work done.

4.3 Decisional roles

  • Entrepreneur/change initiator: start projects, redesign older work, and pursue customer-focused improvements with minimal disruption.
  • Disturbance handler: solve unexpected problems—for example, replace an absent employee or correct a label-printing machine that is slightly misaligned.
  • Resource allocator: compare solution cost, time, and consequences.
  • Negotiator: resolve statements such as “I cannot do all of this, but I can do some.”

Examples ranged from assigning Rakesh’s work when he is ill to a senior leader deciding how to respond to Jio’s new university initiative. Managers are hired substantially to solve problems; MBA knowledge and experience improve this capacity.

5. Business environment and organizational structure

5.1 Macro environment: broad forces

Force Transcript examples/implications
Economic National performance, inflation/prices, buying sentiment, employment, currency and exchange rates. An exchange movement can make shipment to the USA twice as expensive. Demand for genuinely trained MBAs may raise salaries.
Technological AI/ChatGPT and large language models, image AI, automation, and robotic factories. Firms that fail to adapt when rivals do may disappear.
Sociocultural/demographic Ageing Japan versus India’s young population; gaps in vocational training for electricians, carpenters, and builders; greater visibility of gender equality and the need to avoid discrimination.
Environmental/human-induced Heat in factories, traffic, pollution, stress, and highly polluted Indian urban areas affect health, retention, and operations.
Political/government/legal Government policies, administrative delays, and encouragement of entrepreneurship affect firms. Policy can support job generation and national development.

The environment is dynamic. AI, post-pandemic demand for online learning, and government priorities can change quickly. The instructor cited Byju’s: reduced post-pandemic interest in online learning compounded its other serious ethical/business problems.

5.2 Micro/internal and immediate environment

  • Owners/shareholders: their preferences and rights strongly influence the company.
  • Managers: may demand financial strengthening, operational changes, or return-to-office policies.
  • Employees: may require climate control, resist long polluted commutes, and have work and welfare needs.
  • Customers: judge whether value justifies price and can leave for better solutions.
  • Competitors: lower-cost or different offerings require understanding and adaptation.
  • Suppliers: cross-border supply (for example, China to India) may become difficult.
  • Intermediaries: distributors, stores, legal actors, and quality-control entities enable the route to market.

Exam distinction: competitor action is a micro influence because it directly affects the firm’s market, not a macro force. Spend most attention on the company itself and immediate environment while continuing to scan broader forces.

5.3 Organizational hierarchy and the C-suite

In a listed company, shareholders vote for directors; the board represents shareholders and appoints/oversees the top executive. Senior leaders appoint other heads, who in turn appoint/manage lower levels.

Hierarchy:

  1. Senior management/C-suite — enterprise direction.
  2. Middle managers — regional managers, production heads/directors, sales managers.
  3. First-line managers — supervisors, team leaders, foremen.

Hierarchy creates coordination rather than necessarily rigid control. People should work with whoever has the needed information and decision rights while respecting structure enough to avoid chaos.

Role Core responsibility
CEO/CMD/President Overall direction, major decisions, performance and stakeholder reporting (sales, expenses, profit).
COO Day-to-day company operations and staff management; often the number-two executive.
CIO Information, computer, telephone, and related systems that support production, sales, and accurate data.
CHRO Recruit, manage, develop, pay, and when necessary release people; oversees recruitment, HR, training/L&D.
CMO Describe and position the offering, price it, choose where it is sold, and manage marketing/channel activity.
CRO Own sales/revenue targets; example: commit to and manage a 20% sales increase.
CTO Technology, intellectual property, technical superiority, and relevant equipment/capability.

6. Production of products and services

6.1 Core conversion model

A company fundamentally (1) makes something customers will pay for and (2) gets/sells/delivers it to them.

Inputs/factors of production → conversion/transformation process → outputs → customer

Inputs include:

  • Natural resources and raw materials.
  • Human resources with suitable skills/training.
  • Capital/money.
  • Equipment, facilities, information, energy, and supporting supplies.

6.2 Product example: bottled water

The business must source water, purify it with additives/filters, form the plastic bottle, make the label, fill, stamp, seal, test, package (including cardboard), and distribute. Each resource and operation forms part of production.

6.3 Service example: Subway restaurant

  • Trained people receive ingredients such as tomatoes, onions, sauces, and frozen supplies.
  • The store uses space, water, electricity, refrigerators, equipment, and money.
  • Workers thaw, cut, combine, prepare, wrap, and hand the sandwich to the customer.
  • The combination creates greater value than the separate components.
  • A manufactured product may leave a factory in volume and move through an Amazon warehouse; a service output may be produced and delivered directly in the store.

6.4 Facility layout and process choice

Layout follows the nature and flow of work:

  • One person versus a team of ten.
  • Assembly-line/assembly-machine flow, as in cars, where each station adds a part.
  • Project or fixed-position collaboration, as with a handicraft/window installation.

The layout should support sequence, movement, volume, technology, people, and customer/service requirements.

6.5 Operations concerns

  • Match capacity and output to demand; demand is uncertain and the business must be able to meet it.
  • Maintain quality and consistency in both products and services.
  • Coordinate sourcing, inventory, equipment, labour, production, packaging, distribution, and feedback.
  • Improve the process for speed, lower cost, fewer errors, and reliable delivery without sacrificing customer value.

7. Entrepreneurship, innovation, customers, and cross-functional work

7.1 Entrepreneurship versus innovation

Entrepreneurship Innovation
Identifies an opportunity/problem, organizes resources, accepts uncertainty/risk, creates and captures value, and builds a sustainable venture. Introduces a new or improved product, process, service, experience, pricing/delivery approach, or business model that creates value.

Innovation does not require advanced technology, a new invention, or a large R&D budget. It can be:

  • Product innovation: new product or important feature.
  • Process innovation: faster, cheaper, or better production/logistics/delivery.
  • Service innovation: easier onboarding, support, purchase, or interaction.
  • Business-model innovation: subscription, usage-based pricing, platform, or another way to create/capture value.

7.2 Customer-centred sequence

  1. Understand the customer and the real problem.
  2. Understand competitors and the environment.
  3. Identify the opportunity.
  4. Design a proposed solution.
  5. Build a prototype/early version.
  6. Test with customers.
  7. Learn and revise.
  8. Align production/operations, marketing, sales, and finance.
  9. Launch/scale.
  10. Continue feedback and improvement.

Prototyping reduces uncertainty and development cost, validates assumptions, improves usability, and catches problems before scaling. The modern approach is early learning and concurrent feedback, not “build everything, launch, then fix.”

7.3 Five basics of every business (live-session framework)

Step Key question Examples/emphasis
Design Who is the real customer, what problem needs solving, and what should be built around that need? A college cafe designs quick, affordable, satisfying food; a phone might target premium users, ease of use, a clean interface, or smooth experience.
Produce How is the idea converted into a real, reliable product/service? Focus on quality, consistency, and ability to meet uncertain demand.
Deliver How does it reach the customer correctly and on time? In a cafe, serve the correct order on time.
Sell How is value communicated? Even the best product fails if the entrepreneur cannot communicate it through presentation, pricing, and customer interaction.
Satisfy Did the whole experience make the customer want to return? Satisfaction after use supports loyalty and continuity.

7.4 Customer value and selling orientation

Customer value is determined by customer priorities, not the company’s feature list. If reliable delivery matters more than new features, improving delivery creates more value. Common drivers: reliability, convenience, trust, simplicity, speed, transparent pricing, usability, and service quality.

Feedback sources include interviews, surveys, reviews, complaints, support tickets, and usage data. Feedback is required before and after launch.

Transactional selling Relationship/consultative selling
Short-term, product-centred, pressure for an immediate transaction Ask questions, diagnose need, recommend the appropriate solution, support use, build trust and long-term success

Relationship selling can create repeat purchases, loyalty, referrals, satisfaction, and higher customer lifetime value.

7.5 Cross-functional organization

An organization joins leadership with marketing, operations, product/engineering, sales, finance, and other functions. Leadership is the “brain” that sets direction, but cannot perform without the rest.

  • Marketing understands customers and communicates positioning.
  • Product/engineering turns the imagined solution into reality.
  • Operations ensures it is produced consistently and as intended.
  • Sales communicates value and closes/maintains relationships.
  • Finance supplies discipline and resources.

Traditional “throw over the wall” development (marketing → engineering → production → sales → customer) creates late changes, delay, rework, cost, and mismatched promises. Concurrent collaboration identifies conflicts early and keeps the customer visible throughout.

8. Integrated case: Sophia Martinez

8.1 Facts

Sophia, a third-year marketing student at Crestview University, interns at BrightWave Solutions while handling coursework and a live client project. She faces phone notifications, impromptu calls, a shared apartment with little quiet space, and last-minute cramming before client presentations.

She uses:

  • A specific two-day research goal with measurable checkpoints that is achievable, relevant to the pitch, and time-bound.
  • Thirty-minute chunks.
  • Handwritten Cornell notes (left cue column, right details, bottom summary).
  • Malik as a peer accountability partner.

The continuing cramming shows a key distinction: good micro-level techniques can still sit inside poor macro-level planning.

8.2 Options

Criterion A: Daily phone-free sessions B: Weekly milestones C: Peer supervisor per milestone
Main problem addressed Interruptions/environment Long-term pacing and priorities Enforcement/accountability
Strengths Deep work, concentration, active learning, fewer switching costs Prevents cramming, shows progress, sustains project momentum Visibility, responsibility, deadline adherence
Costs/risks Reduced flexibility and delayed urgent responses Planning/monitoring overhead Micromanagement, resentment, reduced autonomy, relationship strain
Overall Best direct response to distraction Most balanced systemic/root-cause response Use carefully; convert supervision into supportive accountability

Best integrated answer: combine A and B, and adapt C so Malik/peers are supportive accountability partners rather than hierarchical supervisors. Protect focus daily, review weekly milestones, and use collaborative check-ins.

8.3 Root-cause logic

  • Symptoms: stress, cramming, superficial learning.
  • Immediate cause: interruptions and attention switching.
  • Systemic causes: inadequate long-term schedule, lack of milestones, and uncontrolled environment.
  • Lesson: SMART goals, chunking, and notes are necessary but insufficient; sustained success also needs macro planning, focus protection, review, and healthy team dynamics.

9. Likely exam angles and answer frames

Short-definition questions

  • Define time management, prioritization, procrastination, active learning, Cornell notes, SMART goals, managerial roles, macro/micro environment, production, entrepreneurship, innovation, customer value, and cross-functional team.
  • Distinguish activity management from entrepreneurial time management.
  • Distinguish efficiency from consistency; explain how both combine with allocation to create momentum.
  • Distinguish product and service production; transactional and relationship selling; entrepreneurship and innovation; micro and macro environment.

Framework questions

  • Explain SMART with one measurable venture example.
  • Explain Allocation–Efficiency–Consistency → Momentum.
  • Explain Inputs → Conversion → Outputs, using bottled water or Subway.
  • Explain managerial roles: informational, interpersonal, decisional.
  • Explain business basics: Design → Produce → Deliver → Sell → Satisfy.
  • Explain customer-centred innovation from insight through prototype, feedback, alignment, launch, and continuous improvement.

Applied/case questions

  • A founder spends most available time perfecting a logo: diagnose poor allocation and recommend customer interviews/prototyping.
  • Two founders face a feature decision: discuss the balance between analysis, decision speed, launch, feedback, and quality.
  • A factory becomes hotter and employees leave: combine environmental, human-resource, operations, and managerial analysis.
  • A competitor launches a cheaper product: classify it as micro environment, gather information, assess customer value, and coordinate a response.
  • A student uses SMART goals but still crams: explain why micro tools require macro milestones and distraction control.
  • A sales team promises features production cannot provide: explain why concurrent cross-functional coordination prevents rework and dissatisfaction.

Instructor emphasis to reproduce in answers

  • A qualification without demonstrated skill has little lasting value.
  • Time cannot be purchased; how it is used is an entrepreneurial advantage.
  • Speed does not mean blind haste; balance timely decisions with quality.
  • Ideas are common; execution and outcomes matter.
  • Being busy is not the same as moving forward.
  • Customer needs must shape design, production, delivery, sales, and satisfaction.
  • Quality exists everywhere: product, service, communication, and experience.
  • Managers must scan changing internal, micro, and macro environments and solve problems.

10. Rapid revision sheet

One-line recall

  • Time management: deliberately allocate protected time to high-impact work and execute it consistently.
  • Entrepreneurial time: decision + execution + balanced speed/quality → outcome.
  • Momentum: right allocation + low-friction efficiency + daily consistency.
  • Interruption cost: full refocus may take about 20–23 minutes.
  • SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
  • Cornell: cue left, detailed notes right, summary bottom.
  • Active learning: retrieve, apply, explain, solve, reflect.
  • Manager roles: informational, interpersonal, decisional.
  • Macro: economy, technology, society/demography, environment, politics/government/legal.
  • Micro: owners/managers/employees plus customers, competitors, suppliers, intermediaries.
  • Management levels: C-suite → middle → first-line.
  • Production: inputs/factors → conversion → valued output.
  • Business basics: Design → Produce → Deliver → Sell → Satisfy.
  • Entrepreneurship: organize opportunity/resources under uncertainty to create sustainable value.
  • Innovation: implement a valuable improvement; technology is optional.
  • Customer-centred development: insight → prototype → test → revise → align → launch → improve.
  • Cross-functional rule: collaborate early; do not throw work over departmental walls.
  • Sophia case: A protects focus, B fixes pacing, collaborative C supplies accountability; combine them.

Ten-second misconceptions check

  • More hours ≠ more progress.
  • A neat calendar ≠ effective time use.
  • Careful thought ≠ indefinite delay.
  • Effort ≠ rewarded outcome.
  • SMART goals alone ≠ distraction control.
  • Chunking alone ≠ deep learning.
  • Notes alone ≠ mastery; retrieval/application are required.
  • Innovation ≠ invention or technology only.
  • More features ≠ more customer value.
  • Competitors ≠ macro environment.
  • Customer feedback ≠ post-launch only.
  • Strong departments working separately ≠ a strong organization.

Review Week Augmentation

  • A good or technically advanced idea is insufficient: entrepreneurial success also requires a real customer need, usability, execution, scalability, adaptability and perceived value.
  • Keep invention (creating something new) distinct from innovation (implementing a new or improved idea to create value). Incremental improvements to process or experience still count as innovation.
  • Extend innovation analysis beyond product features to process, experience, business model and partnerships. FreshLoop is therefore more than an app: logistics, trust, support and partner coordination are part of the innovation.
  • Perceived value is multidimensional: benefits minus all experienced costs, including money, time, effort, uncertainty, inconvenience, switching and frustration. Higher price can win through reliability, personalization, responsiveness and trust.
  • Feedback is an innovation loop—feedback → insight → change → new experience → new feedback—not merely customer service.
