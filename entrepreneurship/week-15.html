Scope and instructor framing

Week 15 consolidates practical concepts that were not covered deeply in the textbook: venture finance, legal protection, founder/investor agreements, exit and liquidity, entrepreneurial communication, innovation methods, customer pain points, decision discipline, and investor readiness. The instructor repeatedly stressed understanding and application rather than rote memorization. Glossary study is useful only after the conceptual foundation is clear.

1. Venture capital and fundraising fundamentals

Venture capitalist (VC)

A venture capitalist is a professional investor who evaluates many startup opportunities and selects a very small number with superior return potential.

  • VCs often have industry experience and top-tier MBA backgrounds, strong memories, and the ability to absorb and connect information from several domains.
  • Selection judgment matters more than credentials once the person begins investing.
  • Illustrative funnel from the lecture: review 100 business plans, follow up with about 15, invite about 10 to present, and make offers to only 3–4.
  • A VC becomes “good” by choosing better ventures than competing investors and generating strong fund returns.
  • Successful professionals may become general partners (GPs) of the venture fund.
  • Instructor characterization: intellectually enjoyable but extremely hard work.

The ask

The ask is what the founder requests from an investor:

  • amount of money required;
  • ownership/equity offered in return; and
  • often the proposed use of funds—hiring, office setup, equipment, marketing materials, etc.

Use of funds matters because investors may restrict or approve how their money is deployed.

Term sheet

A term sheet is the preliminary summary of the principal terms on which an investor may invest. It is the negotiating blueprint, not the complete final contract.

Typical contents:

Area Examples
Economics investment amount, pre-money valuation, post-money valuation, ownership percentage
Security common equity, preferred equity, convertible preferred shares
Investor protection liquidation preference, anti-dilution protection
Governance voting rights, board representation, reserved/consent matters
Conditions due diligence, audit, regulatory clearance, documentation, milestones

After agreement on the headline terms, lawyers prepare long definitive/offering documents—sometimes 100+ pages—which everyone signs and which govern the legal relationship. A term sheet may be partly or largely non-binding, but it still has strong moral, commercial, and reputational weight. Renegotiation should therefore be in good faith, supported by new information, and communicated early.

Term sheet is not: the full business plan, a founders’ agreement, or the final investment agreement.

Valuation

Public-company market value is calculated as:

Market capitalization = price per share × shares outstanding

Lecture illustration: if TCS trades at ₹1,800 and has 1 billion shares outstanding, market capitalization is ₹1,800 billion (₹1.8 trillion).

For a private venture:

  • Pre-money valuation = negotiated company value immediately before the new money.
  • Post-money valuation = pre-money valuation + new investment.
  • Investor ownership after the round = investment ÷ post-money valuation.

Example from the case material:

  • Pre-money valuation = $8 million
  • New investment = $2 million
  • Post-money valuation = $10 million
  • Investor ownership = $2m ÷ $10m = 20%

A valuation should be a defensible conclusion rather than an aspirational number. Support it with:

  • revenue, growth, margins, retention and unit economics;
  • market size and realistic obtainable share;
  • comparable companies/transactions;
  • technology/IP and regulatory position;
  • team quality, traction, risk, and scenario assumptions.

Industry comparables support—but do not replace—company-specific analysis. A very high valuation can hurt a founder if it creates unrealistic expectations, makes a later down round more likely, or produces harsher investor rights.

Cap table (capitalization table)

A cap table records who owns what in the company. It should show shareholders, number and type of securities, issue price, percentage ownership, options, convertible securities, and changes after each financing.

Why it matters:

  • establishes current ownership and control;
  • calculates dilution and the effect of a proposed round;
  • reveals promised options, convertibles, or conflicting claims;
  • supports valuation and investment negotiation;
  • allows investors to assess whether the ownership structure is clean and understandable.

Every equity issue changes percentages. A founder must model the cap table before and after investment and consider special rights alongside headline ownership. Twenty percent ownership with preferential economics or voting rights can be more influential than an ordinary 20% holding.

Vesting and buyback clauses

Vesting makes equity ownership accrue over time so employees/founders remain committed.

  • Typical lecture example: 25% after year 1, 50% after year 2, and 100% after year 4.
  • It conserves scarce startup cash by supplementing salary with future upside.
  • It aligns rewards with company growth and discourages early departure.

A buyback clause permits the company to repurchase some shares when a founder leaves early or stops contributing.

  • A founder leaving with 20% should not automatically retain the entire stake until an IPO.
  • The repurchase price may vary by departure time; the person might receive some profit but much less than if they had stayed.
  • Lecture illustration: a non-performing founder’s 10% might be reduced to 5%, then potentially 1%, subject to the agreement.
  • Purpose: prevent “free-riding” founders from retaining outsized ownership and keep the venture driven toward its purpose.

Voting rights and board representation

Voting rights determine influence over:

  • election of the board;
  • selection/oversight of senior leadership; and
  • major strategic decisions.

Different share classes may carry different votes. The lecture used Google’s founders as an illustration: founder shares may have vastly more votes per share than ordinary public shares. Therefore, economic ownership and control are not always proportional.

Board representation gives an investor formal access to information and influence over strategy, senior appointments, financing, budgets, and major transactions. It is a governance right, not the same as ownership percentage.

Liquidation preference

A liquidation preference determines who is paid first, and how much, in specified liquidity events such as sale, merger, or liquidation.

  • It is separate from ownership percentage.
  • It can materially redistribute proceeds, especially in a low-value exit.
  • Founders must examine the preference multiple, participating/non-participating nature, and definition of covered events.

Anti-dilution protection

Anti-dilution provisions protect an investor economically when a later round is issued at a lower price (a down round), usually by adjusting the conversion price or share entitlement.

  • It does not mean the investor can never be diluted.
  • The precise formula matters: broad protection shifts more downside to founders and earlier holders.
  • Founders should model the effect before agreeing.

Vulture funds

A vulture fund invests in distressed or “broken” ventures at low valuations and attempts a turnaround.

  • Existing founders and investors may be heavily diluted.
  • The fund may replace senior management, remove founder privileges/assets, restructure operations, and impose discipline.
  • Byju’s was used as an illustration of a once highly valued company damaged by questionable sales/refund practices, business slowdown, and management mistakes.
  • The label is pejorative, but the lecturer emphasized that such investors are not necessarily evil: turnaround is their business. Distress may arise from uncontrollable shocks (e.g., pandemic effects) or avoidable managerial ineffectiveness.
  • Founder lesson: avoid reaching a desperate financing position where bargaining power and ownership collapse.

Burn rate and runway

Burn rate is the rate at which cash outflow exceeds cash inflow, measured daily, monthly, or annually depending on the decision.

Net burn = cash expenses/outflows − operating cash inflows

Investment cash and operating revenues add cash; equipment, hiring, marketing, and other expenses consume it.

Runway = available cash ÷ periodic net burn

Monitor burn and runway continuously. Do not wait until cash is exhausted before adopting Plan B, seeking finance, pivoting, or restructuring.

2. Exit, liquidity, and the IPO

Exit and liquidity strategies

Founders and investors eventually need a way to realize the value of their ownership. Routes discussed include:

  • acquisition/sale to another company;
  • merger or strategic transaction;
  • IPO;
  • sale/transfer of shares; and
  • liquidation of the business.

Liquidation means closing the business, selling assets, settling company obligations, and distributing any remainder to shareholders according to their rights.

Instructor distinction: an acquisition can be either:

  • a growth strategy when the company acquires another business to enter a market or add capability; or
  • an exit strategy from the perspective of the startup being acquired and its owners.

The transaction may look similar, but its strategic meaning depends on whose perspective is used.

Initial public offering (IPO)

An IPO is the first offering of a privately held company’s shares to public investors, after which its shares can be traded in the public market.

Before vs. after IPO:

Before IPO After IPO
Private ownership Publicly traded ownership
Fewer disclosure obligations Strict financial reporting and disclosures
Lower public scrutiny Greater regulatory/public scrutiny
Concentrated accountability Greater board and management accountability
Illiquid holdings Potential liquidity for existing holders

An IPO is transformational because public investors require transparency, accountability, scalability, stability, and evidence of organizational maturity. Not every company is capable of operating under public-market scrutiny.

An IPO does not automatically mean founders sell everything or the company closes. It creates a market for the shares and may provide liquidity, growth capital, credibility, and future financing options.

Class discussion mentioned Swiggy, Zomato, Shiprocket, and Milky Mist Dairy as examples/recent-IPO prompts; these were interactive examples rather than verified factual claims in the transcript.

3. Legal protection and internal governance

Non-compete agreement

A non-compete restricts a founder/employee from competing with the company for a stated period or in a defined field/market. It may prohibit joining a competitor or using company knowledge to start a competing business. The lecture described serious contractual penalties for breach.

Non-disclosure agreement (NDA)

An NDA protects confidential information by requiring the recipient not to disclose or misuse it.

Uses include discussions with prospective customers, advisers, partners, employees, and contractors. It can protect company plans, inventions, processes, patent strategy, and other information whose disclosure would help competitors. The lecturer memorably called it a “keep your mouth shut” agreement.

Distinction: non-disclosure restricts sharing/use of confidential information; non-compete restricts competitive activity.

Work-for-hire agreement

A work-for-hire/engagement agreement defines the work to be done, duration, hours, topics, direction, review, confidentiality, non-disclosure, non-competition/conflicts, and ownership of output.

  • Common for contractors (e.g., three months at specified hours), but the lecturer recommended equivalent protections for employees.
  • A worker should not reuse proprietary knowledge for another client in the same/related business.
  • Essential result: IP created for the venture belongs to the company, not casually to the individual creator.

Founders’ agreement

A founders’ agreement governs the relationship among founders. It should cover:

  • roles, duties, authority, and decision-making;
  • expected effort/time and performance/results;
  • compensation and incentives;
  • ownership allocation and vesting;
  • buyback/leaver provisions;
  • voting and dispute resolution;
  • confidentiality and non-compete obligations;
  • ownership/assignment of all company-related IP;
  • treatment of founder departure, disability, deadlock, or later entry.

Crucial distinction: a roles section allocates work; it does not by itself transfer code or inventions. Explicit IP-assignment language is required. Even if founders originally developed the asset, work connected with the company should belong to the company.

Term sheet vs. founders’ agreement

Founders’ agreement Term sheet
Among founders Between company/founders and investor
Internal ownership, roles, IP, vesting, departure Proposed financing economics, rights, governance, and conditions
Organizes founder relationship Structures an investment negotiation

Code of conduct

A code of conduct formally states acceptable ethical behavior. It must be real and enforced—not a marketing gimmick. The lecturer criticized companies that publish codes while stripping natural resources and polluting water.

Company culture

Company culture is the community of shared beliefs and expected behaviors that shapes why people join and how they work.

  • “Great place to work” does not necessarily mean comfort; one culture may emphasize hard work, overtime, execution, and becoming the best.
  • NVIDIA was cited as closer to an intense performance culture; a traditional large car company may differ.
  • An ambitious young employee may choose Google partly because of its culture.
  • Culture should be intentionally considered by founders and early employees.

4. Entrepreneurial communication

Corporate narrative

The corporate narrative is the company’s coherent story: how it grew, what it is uniquely good at, who contributed, and why it matters.

Possible elements:

  • founders’ backgrounds and character;
  • team and board contributions;
  • first customers and key turning points;
  • distinctive competence and growth journey.

Apple was used as an example of a story centered on making technology so simple that users did not need an instruction manual. Only a small fraction—perhaps 5–10 out of 100 professionally started firms—develop a truly compelling growth story.

Elevator/lift pitch

An elevator pitch is a highly engaging 20–30 second oral summary designed to earn the next conversation, not close the whole transaction.

  • Origin story: the time available while riding a skyscraper lift with an investor/general partner.
  • Desired investor response: “Here is my card; let us schedule an hour.”
  • Desired customer response: “Tell me more / present to my staff.”
  • It is a call to action.
  • Rehearse structure, but tailor content to the listener instead of reciting a generic memorized script.

Different audiences require different pitches:

  • investor: return, traction, market, team, ask;
  • customer: problem, value, use, proof;
  • employee: purpose, opportunity, culture;
  • supplier: why to extend credit now and the future preferred-supplier value.

A pitch deck is the slide presentation supporting a longer pitch. An entrepreneurial contest (pitch/entrepreneurship competition) lets ventures present ideas, gain feedback/visibility, and sometimes win resources or funding. The term was mentioned briefly rather than developed in detail.

Problem–solution narrative

A strong narrative tightly connects a real, urgent customer problem with the solution the customer values.

  • Marketing channels must match how customers want to buy.
  • The solution should answer the customer’s problem, not the inventor’s fascination.
  • Example: “a pen that lights in the dark” is irrelevant if the customer needs “a pen that keeps writing reliably until the ink is finished.”
  • Tailor it to an actual customer segment, not an imaginary one.

Value proposition

Customers do not buy the product as an object; they buy the benefit or value it produces.

Examples:

  • Water is valuable because it satisfies a survival need.
  • An online textbook eliminates carrying a heavy physical book—especially valuable to someone who must walk rather than take an auto.
  • Ola’s value is not merely an app/GPS; it is rapid, accurate rider–driver matching plus the total experience.

A company-level value proposition includes product plus marketing, operations, safety, cleanliness, maintenance, customer support, and every surrounding process the customer values.

Iceberg Principle

Only a small portion of entrepreneurial preparation is visible; a much larger body of evidence must exist beneath it.

Visible: elevator pitch, deck, headlines, market claim, traction numbers, valuation, founder story.

Hidden foundation: research, interviews, customer data, financial models, cap table, contracts, IP records, audit trails, regulatory status, risks, scenarios, milestones, raw documents, and operational knowledge.

The founder should not crowd all hidden detail into the pitch. Instead, make it retrievable through a URL, PDF, data room, model, or explanation when asked. Do not pretend hidden support exists when it does not—that is a lie and sophisticated investors will expose it.

Short communication + deep preparation.

5. Creativity, design thinking, and innovation

Brainstorming and creativity

Brainstorming requires participants to first know their domains, then combine diverse expertise in a free-form session where ideas are not immediately criticized but are built into bigger and better ideas.

Creativity is the more free-form process of generating new ideas, temporarily allowing people to dream rather than subjecting every thought to immediate critique.

Important caution: brainstorming without relevant domain knowledge is usually a dream state and may be worthless, though luck is possible.

Ideation

Ideation converts purpose into possible ways of achieving it. It is partly process-oriented but largely creative and rests on domain knowledge.

Purpose/goal → domain knowledge → generation of alternatives → selection and experimentation

Design thinking (“empathy thinking”)

The lecturer’s shorthand: be the customer, be inside the customer, spend the day as the customer does, and think like the customer.

Core cycle:

Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test → Learn → Iterate

  • Empathy precedes problem definition.
  • Observe behavior and context; do not rely solely on stated preferences.
  • Testing can reveal that the problem itself was incorrectly framed, requiring a return to empathy/definition—not merely a product tweak.
  • Design thinking does not protect an original idea; it protects commitment to solving the user’s real problem.

User interface (UI)

UI is how a user interacts with a product. It is good only relative to the application and user.

  • A mouse or simple camera may be easy for recording a lecture.
  • A sophisticated television-station camera may be powerful but a poor interface for that use.
  • Usability and fit matter more than technical sophistication.

Invention vs. innovation

An invention is not a company and guarantees nothing unless it solves a customer problem.

  • Nifty products are common in catalogues but are not necessarily viable businesses.
  • Commercial innovation emerges when an invention is turned into a product/process and embedded in a company solution.
  • Example: a wearable becomes valuable when designed around older users’ real needs.

Incremental, breakthrough, disruptive, and pioneering innovation

  • Incremental innovation: small improvement to an existing offering. Example: adding a simple video switch to a DSLR.
  • Breakthrough/major innovation: a substantial new capability or customer effect. Making the DSLR operate as a genuinely capable video camera may cross into breakthrough territory.
  • Disruptive innovation: enters from another/unrelated area, initially stays below incumbents’ radar, is often lower-price/lower-cost, expands into the main market, and becomes difficult for incumbents to answer by the time they notice.
  • Pioneering innovation: named in the video but intentionally skipped by the lecturer; do not infer a detailed Week 15 definition beyond being an innovation category.

Iterative development and MVP

A minimum viable product (MVP) is more than a rough prototype: it contains the bare minimum features necessary to solve the focal problem and generate meaningful customer learning.

Process:

  1. Build a small but usable solution.
  2. Ask users whether it solves the problem and what they would change.
  3. Check willingness to use/pay and feature economics.
  4. Make limited additions.
  5. Repeat until a fuller product emerges.

Lecture cost illustration: if the current cost is 5 and users want two additions, delivering both while keeping cost below 7 could be attractive.

Treat the startup as a science/business experiment. Customers often cannot predict what they will do in a future context because political, economic, social, and technological conditions cannot be fully simulated. Help them focus on the problem, use, and how/when/where they would use the product. Develop around utility, not “that is neat, let us build it.”

Open innovation

Open innovation makes innovation participative: useful ideas, knowledge, and technology can flow from outside the firm, not only internal employees/R&D.

Sources include:

  • universities and research institutions (often through tie-ups/MOUs);
  • customers through co-creation;
  • startup accelerators;
  • partners, suppliers, and other organizations.

The live class attributed/popularized the concept to Henry Chesbrough and tentatively referenced a 2003 book; the glossary video also pointed students interested in user/open innovation toward MIT work and “Dr. Hippel” (Eric von Hippel). The practical question is: who outside the organization can help us innovate faster?

Lateral thinking, linear thinking, Delphi method

These terms were named but expressly skipped/not explained in the Week 15 lectures. They may belong to the course glossary, but the transcript supplies no substantive Week 15 definition; avoid inventing one in a transcript-based answer.

6. Customer pain points and organization-wide solutions

Pain point

A pain point is the total difficulty, frustration, loss, delay, risk, or inconvenience a customer experiences in doing something—not just physical or mental pain.

A good pain point is:

  • specific rather than generic;
  • recurring or sufficiently consequential;
  • experienced by an identifiable customer/segment;
  • feasible to address within the ecosystem;
  • strong enough to overcome habitual behavior and make the customer switch.

Weak/grandiose: “solve all society’s transport problems” or “transport is unreliable.”

Precise: “transport on this route to this destination regularly arrives X minutes late.”

The lecture’s Kerala-train example shows both attraction and feasibility: everyone may want all trains on time, but a startup must ask whether that is solvable in the present ecosystem. Start with a manageable part of a problem in a locality.

Useful test:

Problem → Frequency → Consequence → Current workaround → Switching trigger

Do not treat customers merely as statistics. Understand subjective motives and purchase behavior. Finding both the pain point and the condition that overcomes an entrenched habit goes a long way toward defining a successful business.

Organization as a system

The solution is not the product alone. It is the coordinated company offering:

Function Exam question to ask
Production Can we actually create it?
Marketing Can we communicate it and reach the right audience/channel?
Operations Can we deliver consistently?
Supply chain Can we obtain the right inputs at the right cost?
Customer support Can we help customers use it and resolve issues?
Finance Can we fund and sustain inflows/outflows?

Entrepreneurs must think systemically—individual excellence in one function cannot compensate indefinitely for failure elsewhere.

Human-centered design and social enterprise mission

Human-centered design starts with the lived needs and dignity of the people served, not with a preferred technology.

  • Technology-first: choose a technology, then search for a use.
  • Problem-first: understand people → identify need → define problem → develop/test solutions.

Mission statements are especially essential for social enterprises. The lecture example was not to maximize money from people with disabilities, but to make them employable and succeed with differently abled bodies and minds, and to become known as excellent trainers for them.

A mission supplies a decision criterion: does the proposed action improve the intended beneficiary outcome?

7. Diffusion chasm: early adopters to the mainstream

Early adopters

Early adopters are the small share of people willing to experiment with new and still-developing products. They tolerate imperfection and risk. The live lecture used a rough classroom illustration of only 1–2% being willing experimenters; most people do not want to risk money on an unproven product.

Mainstream customers

Mainstream buyers typically want:

  • reliability and completeness;
  • proof, references, and low perceived risk;
  • easy use and support;
  • established channels and predictable price/value.

Diffusion chasm

The diffusion chasm is the difficult transition after early-adopter demand is exhausted but before the mainstream accepts the product.

  • Early adopters and mainstream customers think, buy, pay, and evaluate risk differently.
  • Early traction is not complete market validation.
  • The company may need product hardening, different messaging, pricing, channels, onboarding, service, and operational scale.
  • The transition can feel like falling off a cliff and starting again.
  • Some ventures cross it; many fail there.

Exam application: if a product is loved by experimenters but mainstream sales stall, do not automatically abandon it or simply spend more. Diagnose the chasm and redesign the whole system for mainstream requirements.

8. Persistence, escalation of commitment, sunk cost, and fail-safe points

Helpful persistence vs. escalation of commitment

Persistence is continued effort when new evidence still supports the opportunity and learning improves execution.

Escalation of commitment is increasing investment because one is psychologically attached to a prior choice or because much has already been spent—even when current evidence is poor.

The glossary description acknowledged that learning can justify increased effort. The live class added the critical boundary: additional commitment must follow evidence, not stubborn attachment.

Helpful persistence Harmful escalation
Responds to evidence Ignores unfavorable evidence
Experiments and learns Repeats the same failing action
Changes method while retaining purpose Protects ego/original plan
Uses future costs and benefits Uses past spending as justification

Sunk-cost fallacy

A sunk cost is a past expenditure that cannot be recovered. It should not determine whether to invest further.

Decision test emphasized in class:

If I were starting this business today, knowing what I know now, would I still invest?

If not, the fact that money/time has already been spent is not a rational reason to continue. Founders may initially reject investors/M&A and insist on self-funding, yet later evidence may require external capital, restructuring, or a pivot. Mindset must change with the situation.

Fail-safe point

A fail-safe point is a predefined threshold that triggers corrective action to restore the venture to a healthy position.

Possible thresholds:

  • revenue not reaching a target by a date;
  • insufficient customers or retention;
  • cash falling below a defined amount;
  • runway dropping below a minimum;
  • unacceptable cost, quality, safety, or regulatory metric.

Process:

Define threshold in advance → measure accurately → monitor periodically → compare actual with threshold → trigger corrective action

Corrective action might include changing strategy, pivoting the business model, restructuring operations, reducing cost, raising finance, or moving from Plan A to Plan B.

Important distinctions/cautions:

  • Fail-safe planning is responsible preparation, not planning to fail.
  • A fail-safe threshold triggers recovery action, not necessarily shutdown; a separate shutdown threshold may exist.
  • Verify data before triggering. Do not react to a measurement error.
  • Do not redefine a metric after it becomes unfavorable (“move the goalposts”).
  • Decide thresholds before emotional attachment grows—this is precommitment.
  • Do not wait for all the money to disappear.
  • Consider the worst-case scenario while remaining committed to the goal.

Integrated decision discipline

  1. Define what success looks like before committing heavily.
  2. Identify metrics, dates, and worst-case limits.
  3. Monitor routinely.
  4. Ask whether new evidence justifies more commitment.
  5. At the threshold, verify the signal.
  6. Pivot/restructure/recover promptly.
  7. Shut down only if a separately defined stopping condition or unavoidable reality warrants it.

9. Investor due diligence and the GreenLeaf/Maya case

Case problem

Maya/GreenLeaf has a compelling investment story and interest from Harbor, but the visible pitch contains optimistic market/financial claims that require stronger support. The strategic choice is between:

  • Option A: preserve optimistic numbers and hope scrutiny is light; or
  • Option B: supply evidence, state uncertainty transparently, revise projections where necessary, and negotiate from credible facts.

The correct path is Option B. The case is about evidence-oriented entrepreneurship, not merely preparing documents.

Pitch vs. due diligence

Pitch Due diligence
Creates interest Tests claims
Condenses narrative Examines underlying records
Highlights opportunity Evaluates risks and inconsistencies
Begins relationship Determines whether conditions are satisfied

Due diligence may cover:

  • Financial: statements, cash, revenue quality, burn, liabilities, projections, audit trail.
  • Legal: incorporation, contracts, disputes, compliance.
  • IP: ownership, assignments, patents, licences, contractor-created work.
  • Commercial: market evidence, customers, pipeline, churn/retention, competitors.
  • Operational: production, people, supply chain, systems, scalability.
  • Ownership: cap table, options, convertibles, promised securities.
  • Regulatory: licences and approvals, especially in regulated industries.

An audit independently checks financial assertions and records. Raw documents are valuable, but stronger when accompanied by context explaining what they mean, their limitations, and how they connect to strategy.

Investment conditions

Conditions are checkpoints that must be satisfied before closing, such as:

  • satisfactory due diligence/audit;
  • corrected cap table;
  • execution of definitive agreements;
  • IP assignment;
  • regulatory approval;
  • required corporate resolutions;
  • achievement/verification of a milestone.

Conditions do not necessarily mean the investor is backing out; they allocate risk and prevent funding before critical facts are verified.

Realistic projections and market sizing

Projections should be tied to explicit assumptions: customer acquisition, pricing, conversion, retention, capacity, costs, timing, and regulation.

Use scenarios:

  • Base case: most probable assumptions.
  • Upside case: favorable but supportable assumptions.
  • Downside case: adverse but plausible assumptions.

Market-size hierarchy:

  • TAM: total addressable market—the broad theoretical demand.
  • SAM: serviceable available market—the part the venture can serve with its model/geography/capability.
  • SOM: serviceable obtainable market—the realistic share it can capture.

A large TAM does not prove achievable revenue. Evidence should connect the segment, customer behavior, channel, economics, capacity, and time horizon to SOM.

Economic vs. governance terms

Economic terms Governance terms
valuation, investment, ownership, liquidation preference, anti-dilution, dividends/conversion board seats, voting rights, consent matters, information rights, founder restrictions

Founder analysis must combine both. A seemingly acceptable valuation may come with control or downside terms that materially alter the deal.

Credibility and information asymmetry

Founders know more about the venture than investors, producing information asymmetry. Due diligence and contractual rights reduce this gap.

Transparency does not require revealing every secret indiscriminately. It means accurately disclosing material strengths, weaknesses, risks, assumptions, and uncertainty through appropriate protected channels (e.g., NDA/data room).

Credibility is a strategic asset because it affects:

  • whether this round closes;
  • later fundraising and renegotiation;
  • partnerships, customers, and talent;
  • board relationships and founder reputation.

Founder credentials strengthen a case but cannot substitute for evidence. The best fundraising story is not the most optimistic one; it is the optimistic story that survives scrutiny.

Maya’s investor-readiness decision questions

  1. Can every pitch claim be proved? If not, gather evidence or qualify it.
  2. Can the valuation methodology and assumptions be explained?
  3. Is the cap table complete, accurate, and understandable?
  4. Are all investor economic and governance rights understood and modeled?
  5. Can every investment condition be satisfied?
  6. Are projections realistic under base/upside/downside cases?
  7. Will commitments remain credible after closing?
  8. Are regulatory and IP gaps disclosed and addressed?

Investment sequence:

Compelling story → evidence → due diligence → valuation/rights negotiation → conditions satisfied → definitive documents → investment → ongoing trust

Pre-money vs. post-money Value before investment vs. value after adding investment
Ownership vs. voting/control Economic stake vs. decision influence; share classes may differ
Ownership vs. liquidation preference Residual percentage vs. priority payout rule
Pitch vs. due diligence Generate interest vs. verify claims and risks
Elevator pitch vs. Iceberg Principle Brief visible communication vs. deep hidden preparation
Invention vs. innovation/business New artifact vs. customer-solving, commercially organized application
Early adopter vs. mainstream buyer Experiment-tolerant minority vs. risk-averse majority seeking proof
Persistence vs. escalation Evidence-based continued effort vs. sunk-cost/ego-driven overcommitment
Fail-safe vs. shutdown Trigger for recovery action vs. trigger to cease operations
Acquisition as growth vs. exit Acquirer enters/adds capability vs. acquired owners realize/transfer value
Product vs. company solution Artifact alone vs. product plus marketing, operations, supply chain, support, finance
Transparency vs. indiscriminate disclosure Honest material explanation through proper safeguards vs. revealing everything

11. Likely exam angles and answer skeletons

“Explain the Iceberg Principle in fundraising.”

  1. Define visible pitch vs. hidden analytical foundation.
  2. List hidden evidence: research, model, cap table, IP, contracts, risks, approvals.
  3. Explain that the pitch creates interest; due diligence tests it.
  4. State that hidden support must be real and quickly retrievable.
  5. Apply to GreenLeaf: revise unsupported optimism, show assumptions and scenarios.
  6. Conclude with credibility/trust.

“Discuss term-sheet terms and founder implications.”

Cover investment, pre/post-money valuation, ownership, security type, liquidation preference, anti-dilution, board/voting rights, conditions, and later definitive documents. Show that economics and governance must be analyzed together.

“How can a startup avoid sunk-cost escalation?”

Define sunk cost and escalation; set measurable thresholds/dates in advance; monitor regularly; verify data; apply the fresh-start question; trigger pivot/restructuring/Plan B; distinguish recovery from shutdown; never move goalposts.

“Why can early-adopter traction fail to become a mass market?”

Define both segments and the chasm; compare tolerance, proof, usability, channel, price, and support; explain required product/company changes; warn that early traction is incomplete validation.

“Explain customer pain point and system-level solution.”

Use specificity, frequency, consequence, feasibility, and switching behavior. Then integrate production, marketing, operations, supply chain, customer support, and finance. Use unreliable route transport rather than the vague “transport is unreliable.”

“What should a founders’ agreement contain?”

Roles, effort, performance, pay, equity/vesting, buyback, voting, dispute/departure, confidentiality, non-compete, and explicit IP assignment. Emphasize that company-related inventions/code belong to the company only when ownership is properly documented.

Case answer method

Identify concept → cite facts/evidence → expose risk/misconception → compare alternatives → recommend action → give metric/condition → explain credibility consequence

12. Common misconceptions to avoid

  1. Any preference is a pain point. No: pain must be specific and consequential enough to motivate action.
  2. One unusual incident proves a market. No: seek recurring evidence across a defined segment.
  3. Design thinking protects the original solution. No: it may redefine the problem.
  4. Testing only validates a product. No: it also tests customer/problem understanding.
  5. A short pitch needs little preparation. No: it needs an iceberg of support.
  6. Early-adopter success proves the mainstream. No: the buying logic differs.
  7. Persistence means never changing. No: retain purpose while adapting method.
  8. Money already spent justifies more spending. No: sunk costs are irrelevant to forward choice.
  9. Founder roles protect IP. No: use express assignment.
  10. A term sheet contains the entire plan. No: it summarizes investment terms.
  11. A fail-safe breach means immediate closure. No: it normally triggers corrective action.
  12. An unfavorable metric may be redefined. No: verify it, then honor the precommitment.
  13. A high valuation is always best. No: expectations, down-round risk, and terms matter.
  14. Liquidation preference equals ownership. No: priority payout and percentage stake differ.
  15. Anti-dilution prevents all dilution. No: it operates under specified financing conditions.
  16. Board rights matter only after IPO. No: they influence private-company governance immediately.
  17. Conditions mean an investor is withdrawing. No: they are risk-control checkpoints.
  18. A non-binding term sheet can be ignored. No: trust and reputation remain at stake.
  19. Optimistic projections are always superior. No: defensible projections are superior.
  20. Founder pedigree replaces proof. No: credentials complement, never replace, evidence.

13. Rapid revision sheet

One-line definitions

  • Ask: money requested, equity offered, and intended use of funds.
  • Term sheet: preliminary summary of proposed investment terms.
  • Cap table: record of who owns which securities and how much of the company.
  • Pre-money: value immediately before investment.
  • Post-money: pre-money plus new investment.
  • Vesting: staged earning of equity over time/performance.
  • Buyback clause: company right to repurchase shares on specified founder events.
  • Voting rights: formal power to influence board and major decisions.
  • Burn rate: periodic excess of cash outflows over inflows.
  • Runway: cash divided by net burn.
  • Liquidation preference: priority payout in specified liquidity events.
  • Anti-dilution: contractual adjustment for specified lower-priced future rounds.
  • NDA: restriction on disclosure/misuse of confidential information.
  • Non-compete: restriction on competitive activity.
  • Founders’ agreement: internal rules on founder ownership, work, rights, exit, and IP.
  • Corporate narrative: coherent story of the company’s journey and competence.
  • Elevator pitch: tailored 20–30 second call-to-action summary.
  • Iceberg Principle: short visible story backed by deep invisible evidence.
  • Design thinking: empathy-led, iterative problem solving.
  • MVP: smallest usable problem-solving version that enables valid learning.
  • Pain point: specific customer difficulty strong enough to motivate change.
  • Diffusion chasm: gap between early-adopter traction and mainstream adoption.
  • Open innovation: use of external as well as internal knowledge/technology.
  • Escalation of commitment: continued/increased investment despite contrary evidence.
  • Fail-safe point: pre-set threshold that triggers recovery action.
  • IPO: first public offering of a private company’s shares.

Must-remember formulas

Market capitalization = share price × shares outstanding

Post-money valuation = pre-money valuation + new investment

Investor ownership = new investment ÷ post-money valuation

Net burn = cash outflows − operating cash inflows

Runway = available cash ÷ periodic net burn

Master flows

Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test → Learn → Iterate

Pain point → MVP → evidence → company-wide solution → scaling → mainstream adaptation

Pitch → evidence → due diligence → valuation and rights → conditions → definitive agreement → closing → trust

Predefine threshold → monitor → verify breach → corrective action/pivot → recover or, only if warranted, stop

Instructor emphasis

  • Understand terms; do not merely memorize glossary wording.
  • Know the customer before brainstorming.
  • Solve a real, precise problem—not a grandiose abstraction.
  • Build the whole company solution, not just a clever product.
  • Keep the visible pitch short but the hidden evidence deep.
  • Never fabricate the iceberg beneath the presentation.
  • Respect agreements even when not fully binding; reputation persists.
  • Do not let sunk costs, ego, or an original plan overpower evidence.
  • Maintain accurate cap tables, contracts, IP ownership, and financial discipline.
  • The strongest optimism is optimism that survives scrutiny.

Final integrative principle

Entrepreneurship is an evidence-led system: understand a precise human problem, create and iterate the smallest useful solution, organize the entire company around delivering value, protect ownership and relationships contractually, finance growth on transparent and defensible terms, and precommit to evidence-based action when reality differs from the plan.

Review Week Augmentation

  • Use this integrated case structure: problem → customer → opportunity → value proposition → innovation → validation → business model → operations → competition → scalability → adaptation → sustainability.
  • A technically superior product can fail when need, usability, execution, economics, partnerships, adaptability, perceived value or operational readiness is weak.
  • Investors evaluate evidence, founder execution, cash and margins, operational scale, defensibility and the venture’s capacity to learn—not just the pitch or social appeal.
  • For FreshLoop, retain six evaluation tensions: social impact vs profit; low price vs economics; rapid expansion vs control; standardization vs local adaptation; innovation vs stability; and acquisition vs trust/retention.
  • Exam cautions: do not invent FreshLoop’s revenue model, assume automatic network effects, equate revenue with profit or profit with cash, label every change radical, or recommend scale before capacity and unit economics are validated.
