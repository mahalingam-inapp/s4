1. The central idea: marketing makes selling easier

Marketing and sales are related but distinct activities, and an entrepreneur must do both well and coordinate them.

  • Marketing understands customers, creates and communicates value, creates awareness and trust, and makes the eventual purchase easier. It is the larger, long-term process.
  • Sales converts interest into a purchase. It is the refined process of phrasing the value attractively enough for the customer to commit money now.
  • A useful shorthand is that marketing creates a pull and prepares the ground; selling is the final conversion and may involve a push.
  • A customer merely liking an explanation is not enough for sales: the customer must actually pay.
  • In a young venture the founder may have to be marketer, salesperson and customer-service representative because there is no established team.

Modern marketing philosophy

The correct starting point is not “What can we manufacture and push?” but:

  1. Who is the customer?
  2. What are the customer’s needs, pain points, bottlenecks and unmet demands?
  3. What solution or experience will make the customer’s life easier or better?
  4. How can the venture create, communicate and deliver that value profitably?
  5. What must be learned and changed after observing the market response?

Value is therefore not merely the physical product. It may be a solution, convenience, reassurance or experience.

2. Why entrepreneurial marketing is different

Early startups face three fundamental problems:

  1. No established brand: no reputation, trust or loyalty; everything must be built from scratch.
  2. Limited resources: little money for mass advertising and few or no dedicated salespeople. Every rupee and every activity must contribute to brand building and progress.
  3. Zero awareness: even an outstanding product cannot sell if customers do not know it exists.
Dimension Traditional marketing Entrepreneurial marketing
Resources Budget, staff, advertising money and data exist Founder may initially have only personal effort and judgment
Main job Manage and remind customers about an established brand Create awareness, story, trust and the first customer base
Goals Profit and market-share targets Early awareness, trade-show interest, reactions, first purchases and reviews
Customers Manage an existing base, often through mass communication Find innovators/early adopters and know them personally
Product Manage a relatively settled 7P mix Continue developing the product and discovering the MVP
Pricing Established prices/bundles Experiment with price points, subscriptions, bundles and negotiation
Risk/learning Continue proven methods with limited risk Run trial-and-error “science experiments” until product–market fit emerges
Communication Broadcast to a large market Direct, persistent interaction to understand why people buy

Entrepreneurial marketing is harder, but if it is done exceptionally well, a resource-rich traditional competitor may struggle against it. The entrepreneur must be:

  • Ingenious: find unconventional access to the right audience (for example, arranging a relevant display in a hospital).
  • Energetic: enthusiasm can carry other people into the venture’s story.
  • Persistent: a new name is easy to forget among hundreds of alternatives.
  • Credible and honest: trust cannot be built through false claims.
  • Selective: scarce money and time cannot chase every segment, channel or campaign.

Entrepreneurial marketing mindset

  • Deep customer understanding: reports and spreadsheets help, but founders must leave the room, speak to customers and watch their real behavior.
  • Personal interaction: a founder replying on LinkedIn or directly answering a customer builds trust and obtains unfiltered learning.
  • Creative problem solving: ask, “Given what I have, what can I do?” Resource scarcity should trigger unconventional thinking, not become a permanent excuse.
  • Efficient resource allocation: deliberately choose the segment, channel and activity with the highest likely impact.
  • Adaptation and iteration: preferences, markets and competitors change. Learning that is not converted into action has little value.

3. Product viability and the MVP

A venture cannot survive by giving customers everything they could conceivably want; it also cannot survive by offering junk. The answer is a viable product: a genuinely useful, high-quality starting solution that may not yet contain every desired feature.

Minimum Viable Product (MVP)

An MVP is the simplest version of the product that:

  • solves the central customer problem;
  • delivers real value and is credible enough for actual use or purchase;
  • enables rapid market feedback;
  • avoids spending scarce resources on unvalidated extras; and
  • supplies evidence for the next iteration.

An MVP is not a defective or careless product. “Minimum” refers to scope, not to quality of the core solution.

Mattress illustration: customers principally need a mattress that keeps them cool at night. The venture can launch that viable benefit even if every preferred colour is not yet available; colours can be added after learning from the market.

Dry Bar illustration: the transcript describes a then-novel non-alcoholic bar concept whose alcohol-free environment promised better-behaved patrons and appealed particularly to women visiting alone or in pairs. The innovation was not merely the drink; it was the safer, more comfortable social context. Success still depended on executing every element around that concept.

Lean-startup logic

The operating cycle is:

Build a focused solution → measure actual behavior → learn → modify product/price/channel/message → repeat.

Actual sales are stronger evidence than imaginary plans or stated intentions.

Airbnb trial-and-error case

  • The founders first tested whether cash-constrained travelers would sleep in sleeping bags or spare spaces rather than hotels.
  • Initial demand existed, but homeowners disliked letting strangers into their homes.
  • The real barrier was trust, and the founders learned that their own claims could not create enough of it.
  • They actively used their own and friends’ homes to generate stays and reviews.
  • Two-sided reviews mattered: guests reviewed homes/hosts, and hosts reviewed guests.
  • The instructor highlighted that about six or more positive reviews without negative reviews created sufficient confidence; after that discovery, revenue rose sharply.
  • Airbnb subsequently expanded from low-cost stays to luxury and unusual stays such as tree houses and boats.

Lesson: entrepreneurial marketing is experimental. Discover the true adoption barrier, build the missing trust mechanism and scale only after evidence.

4. The marketing mix: 4Ps, 4As and 7Ps

4Ps: the producer’s view

P Entrepreneurial question Important details
Product What value does the good/service create? Make, features, functionality and, above all, the benefit to the customer
Price What is the product worth and what will the customer pay? Affordability, value perception, bundles, subscription fee, discounts and competitive position
Place Where and how will it be obtained? Online/offline, direct/indirect, distributors, wholesalers, retailers, warehouses and channel fit
Promotion How will value be communicated? Advertising, PR, social media, direct mail, promotions and personal selling

The instructor cautioned that the 4Ps are a useful teaching device but are written from the company/manufacturer perspective.

4As: the customer’s view

Customers do not think “What is the distribution channel?” They think “Where can I get it?” They do not think primarily about a product specification; they ask “What can it do for me?” The customer-centred mirror is commonly expressed as:

  • Acceptability: does the solution meet functional and psychological needs?
  • Affordability: can the customer bear its monetary and other costs?
  • Accessibility: can the customer obtain it conveniently?
  • Awareness: does the customer know, understand and remember it?

The deeper distinction is perspective: 4Ps describe what the seller manages; 4As describe what the buyer experiences.

The additional 3Ps in the 7P mix

Additional P Meaning and questions
Process Is the website, ordering, instruction, payment, delivery, returns and support process easy and dependable?
Physical environment/evidence What do the store, site, package and visible cues communicate—quality, fashion, sustainability or disorder?
People Are employees rational, respectful, knowledgeable and effortless to deal with, or do they hound and frustrate customers?

Service examples and cautions:

  • Amazon was praised for easy ordering, reliable delivery and accessible intervention when delivery failed.
  • Flipkart and furniture sellers were criticized in the instructor’s experience for poor logistics, excuses and vague delivery commitments (“Wednesday,” but effectively not which month).
  • Tata/Titan’s eyewear service was described as easy to deal with.
  • Repeated calls about an abandoned cart create annoyance and can cause customers to block the brand.
  • A premium Mercedes-type car cannot credibly be sold in an ugly outlet; channel and physical environment must reinforce positioning.

5. Promotion and channel choices

Major promotion tools

Tool Strength Limitation/caution
Advertising Rapid reach and awareness Expensive; wasted if the message does not match what customers value
Public relations Event sponsorship, banners/hoardings, press conferences and favourable editorial coverage can create credibility People-intensive; does not necessarily produce a sale next day
Social media Potentially targeted and shareable Everyone is using it; attention is scarce, so cost per genuinely reached customer may still be high
Direct mail/email Builds a list of interested prospects Spam filters, blocking and uncertain human open/engagement data reduce effectiveness
Sales promotion Limited-time offers, coupons and free samples stimulate trial and conversation Can be expensive; a sample does not guarantee purchase
Personal selling Allows diagnosis of individual needs and tailored explanation Labour intensive; requires skill and empathy

Examples:

  • BYJU’S built a model around heavy advertising followed by salespeople pursuing the leads; it raised money to fund this after reaching scale.
  • At a store, almost everyone may taste a free food sample, though some only want a snack. Even non-buyers may talk about it.
  • Good personal selling resembles a trusted friend helping a decision—even if “do not buy yet” is the honest answer. Following a shopper while repeatedly asking “May I help?” is pressure, not selling.

Place/distribution

  • Direct channel: manufacturer sells to consumer through e-commerce, company stores or mail; a bakery can sell directly.
  • Indirect channel: intermediaries such as retailers, hypermarkets or warehouses.
  • Multi-level indirect channel: wholesaler → retailer → smaller retailer → customer.

Channel choice must match the product’s positioning, economics and desired customer experience. Retailers stock what moves. A familiar snack such as Lay’s can yield only ₹1–₹2 on a ₹10 sale but sell 100 units daily; an unknown product that sits for 2–3 weeks ties up space and will be rejected.

6. Customer discovery and market research

What research can and cannot do

Market research is not magic and cannot reliably state what customers will buy for the next five years. It is descriptive and diagnostic, not perfectly predictive.

It can reveal:

  • what works now;
  • current unmet needs;
  • neglected product areas;
  • detailed evidence from which the founder can make a forecast; and
  • information that reduces, but never eliminates, uncertainty.

Instructor emphasis: research gives information, not the final answer. Data does not make the decision. The entrepreneur’s interpretive judgment determines whether the same evidence appears to be an opportunity or a risk. Common sense and informed human judgment often matter more than a supposed mathematical “answer.”

Primary and secondary research

Type Definition Examples Important caveat
Primary New, first-hand data collected for the present question Customer conversations, observation, interviews, surveys, watching mall behavior Often more useful and potentially more expensive; only valuable if the researcher understands the question and method
Secondary Existing data collected/published by someone else Industry reports, datasets, government databases, publications, census, competitor funding databases, TV/Nielsen data, credit-rating data and paid Google data Faster and cheaper, but may not fit the exact target/question

Live-class classification examples:

  • Interviewing 20 gig-economy riders about pay anxiety — primary.
  • Reading an industry report on India’s D2C market — secondary.
  • A five-question survey of the first 100 customers — primary and need not be expensive.
  • Regional household-income census data — secondary because it is already collected, verified and published.
  • Watching shoppers’ actual mall behaviour — primary observation.
  • Reviewing a competitor’s funding history in a database — secondary.

Informal discovery conversations

Founders can go where prospective customers are and politely ask a buyer about frequency, motives or usage: “I saw you buy that travel product; I am considering a venture in this area—do you buy one or two a year?” A short permitted conversation can reveal more than a questionnaire the person would never complete.

However, vague “Why do you buy this?” questions often produce rationalisations such as “I just thought of it.” Research must still be purposeful and well designed.

Customer interviews: good practice

  • Ask about past and present behaviour, problems, workarounds, frequency, costs and consequences.
  • Use neutral language; do not ask customers to praise the proposed solution.
  • Probe with “Tell me about the last time…” and seek concrete examples.
  • Observe what people do, because behaviour may contradict what they say.
  • Avoid pitching during discovery; pitching contaminates evidence.
  • Record patterns, contradictions and exact problem language.

Leading question: “Don’t you agree our app would make budgeting easier?” It signals the desired response and creates bias.

Neutral question: “How do you currently manage your budget, and what is difficult about it?” It permits an authentic answer.

Sampling and reliability

Because the venture cannot question an entire population, it chooses a sample and uses it to infer behaviour in the population.

Three critical requirements for reliable research are:

  1. Representativeness: the sample must reflect the broader target population. Feedback on a premium fitness app only from fitness influencers/fanatics cannot represent ordinary potential users.
  2. Question design: distorted, unfamiliar or leading wording produces distorted answers and, therefore, poor judgment.
  3. Interpretive judgment: two founders may see the same data differently; judgment must connect evidence to the venture context.

The recorded lesson illustrated distribution assumptions: if the customer mean age is 30, the researcher might design a sample in which roughly two-thirds fall between 25 and 35. A technically impressive sample that represents the wrong population gives confident but irrelevant conclusions.

Market-research process

  1. Define a specific research goal like a science experiment.
  2. Define the population connected to the value proposition.
  3. Choose the research design and primary/secondary methods.
  4. Choose a representative sample and a practical way to access it.
  5. Collect data consistently.
  6. Analyse patterns and limitations.
  7. Apply entrepreneurial judgment.
  8. Act, test in the market and update the conclusion.

High-net-worth housing illustration: if the offer is a house affordable to people worth at least ₹5 crore, the founder might use residence on MG Road as an access/location proxy, gain permission to leave a flyer in suitable buildings, and then sample willing prospects.

Secondary sources in India: Ministry of Statistics, state agricultural statistics, local/district offices, internet searches, officials’ advice, industry data, Nielsen/TV audience data, credit-rating agencies and paid Google datasets.

Opportunity recognition

Opportunity recognition comes from identifying a meaningful, inadequately served customer problem—not merely noticing a broad trend.

  • “Climate change” is too broad by itself.
  • “Heat prevents a customer from sleeping at night” is a specific pain point that can guide a solution.
  • “Daytime heat” may be less important to a customer who works in an air-conditioned office.

Always translate a large trend into a defined customer, context, pain and willingness to act/pay.

7. Market sizing: TAM, SAM and SOM

Level Core question Meaning
TAM — Total Available Market Who could potentially use/buy? Largest relevant opportunity, not literally everyone on Earth
SAM — Serviceable Available Market Who can this offer and business model actually serve? TAM narrowed by geography, language, segment, channel, capability and offer scope
SOM — Serviceable Obtainable Market / Share of Market What can we realistically capture in a stated period? Reachable share after considering budget, awareness, distribution, competition and evidence

The recorded lesson uses SOM as “share of market,” while the live class expands it as serviceable obtainable market. In practice both point to the realistically captured portion of SAM. The instructor also cautioned that market-sizing terminology is not defined identically by everyone; state the definition used in an exam answer.

Key principles

  • TAM should align closely with the value proposition; “everyone with two legs” is not a defensible jeans market.
  • A very large TAM looks attractive in a presentation but does not prove business viability.
  • SAM reflects realism: not “Who could buy?” but “Who can we actually serve?”
  • SOM is especially important to investors because it connects opportunity to execution.
  • Investors ask: exactly who is the target, how many can current marketing reach, and what evidence shows they will choose this venture rather than competitors?

Online-learning illustration

  • TAM: everybody interested in acquiring a skill or short certification.
  • SAM: English-speaking working people seeking management courses, within the platform’s actual reach.
  • SOM: the portion the startup can capture during a defined period with its budget, campaigns and distribution.

Organic-cotton jeans illustration

  • Product idea: mid-priced, 100% organic-cotton jeans.
  • A reasoned initial TAM could be Indian consumers aged roughly 15–50, able to afford jeans, with a likely concentration in Tier 1 and Tier 2 cities and an affinity for the environmental value proposition.
  • It can later be expanded internationally through e-commerce, but early plans should limit scope credibly.
  • SAM can be constructed bottom-up (qualified customers city by city) or top-down (filter a broader market by location, profile and psychographics reachable in the first 2–3 years).
  • SOM/share assumptions might be 2% in year 1, 10% in year 2 and 15% in year 3, provided the assumptions are evidenced.

Market penetration

Market penetration = (venture’s customers or sales in the target market ÷ total customers or sales in that target market) × 100.

It measures captured share, not total opportunity. Small firms should usually focus on SAM and credible penetration before claiming broad expansion.

8. Resource-efficient entrepreneurial marketing techniques

8.1 Guerrilla (jugaad) marketing

Guerrilla marketing seeks maximum impact at minimum cost through surprise, creativity, speed and unconventional media. The term borrows from guerrilla warfare: a small force avoids direct confrontation with a larger force and uses agile, unexpected tactics.

Possible tactics include street entertainers, sidewalk light displays, music-festival participation, signs on cars, QR codes and concentrated activity where the desired audience already gathers. Airbnb placed a large, timely advertisement near computer trade shows because many attendees were resource-constrained entrepreneurs likely to need cheaper accommodation.

Frontline flea-treatment case:

  • Challenge: ordinary pet advertisements showing a scratching dog were repetitive and did not make fleas feel urgent.
  • Frontline printed a huge image of a scratching dog on a shopping-mall floor.
  • From the ground it was unclear; from upper levels, people walking over the image looked like fleas crawling on the dog.
  • Shoppers unknowingly became part of the advertisement, stopped to look, photographed/recorded it and shared it online.
  • Cost was mainly the one-time large print, rather than repeated television airtime.
  • It succeeded because it was unexpected, used an unconventional medium, privileged creativity over budget and stimulated word of mouth.

Caution: guerrilla marketing is not automatically successful; the idea, location and target concentration must fit.

8.2 Relationship marketing

Relationship marketing prioritises trust, retention and long-term customer value over isolated transactions.

Its characteristics are:

  • personal interaction;
  • trust building;
  • continuous engagement;
  • listening and responsiveness;
  • personalised service;
  • retention and advocacy rather than only acquisition.

If a startup has 10 customers, protecting those 10 is usually more important than prematurely chasing 100 strangers. Retention is less costly than repeated acquisition, but its larger value is trust and word of mouth.

Zoho example: Zoho competed with Microsoft, Oracle and Salesforce in CRM, HR and project-management software. Early customers who consistently received strong service recommended Zoho to others; relationships and credible advocacy helped it expand against much larger firms.

8.3 Speeding-up marketing

Create and present variants suited to different users (smallest, fastest, etc.), then watch which version customers actually buy. Use real purchasing behaviour to accelerate learning rather than debating hypothetical preferences.

8.4 Real-time marketing (RTM)

RTM monitors current events, live customer conversations, social-media trends, market developments, clicks and sales, then responds immediately while attention is high.

Process: monitor → identify a relevant moment → check brand fit and risk → craft a timely response → publish quickly → measure reaction → learn.

Zomato/Chandrayaan-3 anecdote: after the successful 2023 lunar moment, Zomato posted “delivered even on the moon.” It was humour and participation in a national moment, not a literal delivery promise. Timeliness and creative relevance drove likes and shares; waiting a week would have destroyed the opportunity.

8.5 Viral and digital marketing

  • Digital marketing: deliberate use of websites, email, search engines, social media, Google/Instagram ads, words and images. It does not assume exponential sharing.
  • Viral marketing: people voluntarily propagate a message, meme or experience through their networks, producing unusually large reach. It cannot simply be ordered into existence; the audience must want or be motivated to share.
  • Best advocacy: customers telling others from authentic experience.

Wakefit “sleep intern” anecdote: while competing with Sleepwell and Kurlon, Wakefit advertised an unusual sleep-intern role/reward involving sleeping on its mattress for about 100 days. Large numbers applied and the idea spread widely.

Solar-panel channel exercise: environmental consciousness may be the main motive; economics were described as only partial in India because parts were still sourced from China and incentives were less aggressive than elsewhere. Newspapers, hoardings and TV are costly, and younger consumers watch less TV. Given pervasive smartphone use, digital/local targeting may be more suitable—but every channel must be evaluated for the particular customer.

9. Branding: the cumulative perception

Branding is the deliberate creation of a memorable, credible impression of who the venture is and what it consistently promises. A famous endorser, a distinctive name or logo may help, but none alone constitutes a brand.

Every touchpoint contributes:

  • product performance and experience;
  • price and distribution;
  • packaging and website;
  • email tone and speed;
  • employee conduct;
  • complaint handling;
  • consistency between claims and reality.

Brand, tagline, mission and vision

  • A brand is the total perception and association held by customers.
  • A tagline is a short, repeated memory cue; it is only one part of the brand.
  • A tagline is not the mission or vision.

Examples in the transcript include Snapchat (“life is more fun when you live in the moment”), Nike (“Just do it”), Apple (“Think different”), Subway (“Eat fresh”) and the credit-card idea that some things cannot be bought while the card handles the rest.

Ola used its identity as an Indian taxi-sharing service to differentiate from Uber even though both employed Indian people locally. MakeMyTrip and Yatra compete through positioning, with MakeMyTrip described as focusing more on hotels.

Brand-building sequence

  1. Understand: identify what customers truly find important and what the segment identifies with.
  2. Craft: write a fuller brand-positioning/promise statement, then distil a tagline. Communicate the promise consistently across interactions.
  3. Align: adjust all 7Ps and operations to deliver the promise. A “great quality” claim cannot coexist with poor service.
  4. Build the website, social media, emails and other touchpoints around that promise.
  5. Test market response, learn and refine without creating contradictory identities.

Consistency across every touchpoint creates trust: the company cannot say one thing today and something incompatible tomorrow.

Brand-recognition exercise

Clues Brand Association illustrated
Rare discounts, anticipated launches, premium pricing, loyal ecosystem, global launch attention Apple Innovation, premium identity and ecosystem loyalty
Multiple industries, confidence, ethics, generations of reputation Tata Trust
Affordable air travel, operational efficiency, paid add-ons, on-time performance, consistency over luxury IndiGo Reliable, efficient low-cost operation
Safety promise, reliability over speed, family consideration, engineering-backed premium Volvo Safety and dependable engineering

The class identified these brands without seeing logos or products, demonstrating that a strong brand is a stable set of associations.

Other examples:

  • Method differentiated through unusual packaging and vegetarian/vegan ingredients.
  • A flexible juice pouch can create a playful, squeezeable childhood association even without a major functional difference.
  • IKEA’s “joyous storage” activity rewarded customers for sharing storage photographs, turning customers into participants.

Positioning and distribution

Positioning answers: Who is the brand for, what category/need does it address, what distinctive benefit does it promise, and why should customers believe it?

Premium positioning requires consistent evidence: price, outlet, package, service, engineering and availability must all reinforce premium value. Selling a supposed premium brand through poorly maintained environments or constant discounting weakens the signal.

10. Marketing strategy and marketing plan

  • Strategy is the coherent set of choices and steps used to reach the desired market result.
  • Marketing plan is the written document/presentation that records the current situation, market evidence, customers, goals, strategy, actions and financial implications.
  • A marketing plan is essential before starting, but it will not be perfectly right. Change it through low-cost learning rather than defending it rigidly.
  • It is distinct from the overall business plan and from merely listing the 7Ps.

What a strong entrepreneurial marketing plan should contain

  1. Situation, problem and opportunity.
  2. Detailed customer/segment definition—spend disproportionate time here.
  3. Customer psychology and stories about what the customer does and where the offering fits.
  4. Primary and secondary research, assumptions and limitations.
  5. TAM, SAM and realistic SOM.
  6. Value proposition, positioning and brand promise.
  7. 7P decisions.
  8. Acquisition, relationship, real-time, guerrilla and digital tactics as relevant.
  9. Schedule, owners and resource allocation.
  10. Metrics, experiments and iteration rules.
  11. Financial impact.

Johnson’s baby shampoo example: the product and name were strong, but purchase depended significantly on pediatricians recommending it. Mothers trusted pediatricians. The decisive customer influence may therefore sit outside the person who ultimately pays; deep interviews must uncover that psychology.

Basic marketing-plan economics

Revenue = Units sold × price actually received by the venture

Net revenue (simplified) = Gross revenue − returns

Operating profit / simplified EBITDA
  = Revenue
  − Cost of goods sold (COGS)
  − Selling, general and administrative expenses (SG&A)
  − Research and development expense (R&D)

Use the price the venture receives, not the final retailer’s price.

COGS water-bottle illustration: water, bottle/package, label, cap and outer cardboard are direct costs. A dedicated factory or machine may also be allocated when directly attributable, though the instructor noted that such allocation is often handled later.

EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. For the marketing plan, the instructor emphasised reaching revenue minus COGS minus SG&A/R&D rather than getting lost in later financial deductions.

11. Sales as a consultative six-step process

The six-step model shown is useful but not universal:

  1. Prospecting: define and locate qualified people. Example persona: a middle-aged, environmentally conscious, frequent cyclist in a Tier 1 city.
  2. Sales call/opening: initiate through telephone, email or in-person contact depending on the product.
  3. Present the proposal/value: communicate benefits, not merely features.
  4. Handle objections: listen, clarify and supply credible evidence.
  5. Close: explicitly invite the decision and remove remaining legitimate barriers.
  6. Follow up: ensure delivery, satisfaction, repeat use and referrals.

Features versus benefits

  • A feature is an attribute: “100% organic cotton.”
  • A benefit is the resulting customer value: less itching/healthier use, support for farmers returning to traditional natural methods, and a lower environmental burden.

Organic-jeans sales illustration

  • Proposal: environmentally friendly jeans can reduce irritation, be healthier for the family and support farmers taking a risk on natural methods.
  • Objection: “How do I know the cotton is pure?” Response: show certification.
  • Closing barrier: customer has only ₹500. If the issue is timing rather than lack of value, accepting a credit card may complete the sale.

Consultative selling diagnoses and solves; pushy selling pressures. Even when no immediate purchase occurs, trust can preserve a future relationship.

12. Customer service is part of marketing and branding

Customer service can turn a buyer into a repeat buyer and advocate. The instructor’s mattress company called on the first day to ask how the mattress was performing; the care created affection and led to another purchase partly as a reward to the venture.

Designing customer service

  1. Decide the affordable service level: full hand-holding, partial support, manual, YouTube video or a combination.
  2. Recruit and train employees in the required language, product knowledge, communication style, content and respect—not merely on low wage.
  3. Give support staff incentives, recognition, awards or prizes for excellent work; some firms reward strong customer reviews.
  4. Make service an organisation-wide responsibility, modelled by the MD/founder.
  5. Appreciate staff: repeatedly handling complaints is emotionally demanding and can become drudgery.

Cautions/anecdotes:

  • An incomprehensible support interaction can reduce interest in a large company and may destroy a young single-product venture.
  • Address conventions must suit customer expectations. The instructor, aged 67, used a young employee’s first-name greeting as a teaching moment and preferred respectful forms such as “Sir,” “Mr.” or culturally appropriate “-ji.” The larger principle is to train for the customer’s communication context rather than act absent-mindedly.
  • Customer service is not a back-office afterthought: it directly creates or erodes the brand.

13. Relationship marketing, retention and lifetime value

Customer retention

Retention means keeping existing customers satisfied and engaged so that they repurchase, stay and recommend the firm. It normally costs less than continuously replacing lost customers and supports stable revenue.

Customer lifetime value (CLV)

CLV is the total economic value expected from a customer over the relationship, not merely the first transaction. A conceptual form is:

CLV ≈ (average contribution per purchase × purchase frequency × relationship duration)
      − customer-specific acquisition/service costs

Retention raises CLV through repeat purchases and may add referral value. Long-term trust should not be sacrificed for a short-term sale.

Word of mouth and social proof

  • Recommendations from satisfied users are more credible than self-praise.
  • Reviews reduce perceived risk and uncertainty.
  • A founder story can humanise the brand and explain motive, struggle and purpose, but cannot substitute for product evidence.
  • Testimonials, adoption numbers, certifications and reviews are forms of social proof.

14. Case study: Neighborhood Bites

Case situation

Neighborhood Bites is a local meal-delivery startup serving one city neighbourhood. Maya leads customer relations. The venture has limited money and manpower and must choose a growth approach that fits its resources and need for sustainable loyalty. Its stated target is to increase repeat orders by about 10% within three months.

The strategic alternatives are:

  • Option A — relationship marketing: personal follow-ups, thank-you messages and regular check-ins.
  • Option B — guerrilla marketing: attention-getting unconventional promotion designed for broad awareness at low cost.

Its case-defined TAM is broad, while its SAM is the neighbourhood’s urban, middle-income consumers. The case uses small initial penetration (about 2%) followed by hoped-for steady growth (about 10% and 15%) as illustrative planning figures, not guaranteed outcomes.

Recommended strategy: relationship marketing

Option A is superior for this case because the venture needs depth of trust, repeat patronage and advocacy more than a one-time awareness spike. Existing customers are an asset that a small business cannot afford to lose.

Practical weekly actions:

  1. Personal follow-up call/message: ask about the recent order or visit and listen to problems.
  2. Personalised thank-you: recognise the customer as an individual rather than sending only a generic promotion.
  3. Regular check-in: maintain contact without waiting for the next sale.

These actions create personal interaction, trust and continuous engagement. They also gather useful feedback, increase retention and CLV, and stimulate referrals.

Why guerrilla marketing is not the primary choice here

Guerrilla marketing can create surprise, awareness and reach, but attention does not guarantee repeat business. It remains a possible supporting tactic after the service and relationship foundation is strong. The trade-off is breadth of attention versus depth of relationship.

Feedback cycle in the case

  1. Ask neutral, understandable questions.
  2. Listen empathetically and record data consistently.
  3. Identify recurring service/product patterns.
  4. Make a visible improvement.
  5. Tell customers what changed because of their feedback.
  6. Follow up and measure satisfaction/repurchase.

Data collection is both:

  • a science—representative sampling, consistent measures, reliable records and unbiased questions; and
  • an art—rapport, timing, empathy, probing and interpreting context.

Feedback itself can strengthen the relationship because it signals that the customer matters, but asking without acting can damage trust.

Market sizing for Neighborhood Bites

  • TAM: everyone who could potentially purchase food in the broad category/geography.
  • SAM: customers the outlet can realistically serve given location, cuisine, hours, delivery radius and capacity.
  • SOM/penetration: realistic customers or sales captured from that SAM.

The small venture should concentrate limited resources on SAM, measure penetration and expand only after loyal demand is established.

Measures of success

  • repeat-purchase/visit rate;
  • retention and churn;
  • frequency and average order value;
  • complaint resolution and satisfaction;
  • referrals and positive reviews;
  • response rate to follow-ups;
  • CLV and revenue from existing customers;
  • market penetration in the reachable local segment.

16. Likely exam angles and answer scaffolds

Assertion–reason questions

For this format, test two things separately: (1) factual correctness of the assertion and reason and (2) whether the reason actually explains the assertion. The possible classifications are: both true with a correct causal link; both true but unrelated; assertion true/reason false; or assertion false/reason true. Do not infer causality merely because both statements concern the same topic.

A. “Differentiate traditional and entrepreneurial marketing.”

Define both; compare resources, goals, customers, communication, product/pricing experimentation and risk; explain why personal interaction, creativity, persistence and MVP iteration matter; close with Airbnb.

B. “Explain the 7P marketing mix for a startup.”

Define 4Ps and add people, process and physical evidence. Explain the customer-centred 4A perspective. Use Amazon/Flipkart, premium outlet, personal-selling and retailer-stock-turnover examples.

C. “Can market research predict startup success?”

Answer no. Explain information versus answer, primary/secondary research, representativeness, question design and interpretive judgment. Add fitness-app sampling and leading-question cautions.

D. “Explain TAM, SAM and SOM with an example.”

Draw nested circles; define all three; note alternate SOM terminology; use online learning or organic jeans; explain investor focus and calculate penetration if data are given.

E. “Discuss low-cost marketing techniques for entrepreneurs.”

Cover guerrilla, relationship, real-time, viral, digital and speeding-up marketing; compare mechanisms and risks; use Frontline, Zomato, Zoho and Wakefit.

F. “Branding is more than a logo.” Discuss.

Define brand as cumulative perception; distinguish tagline/mission/vision; explain understand–craft–align and consistency; use Apple, Tata, IndiGo and Volvo clues plus service/website/packaging touchpoints.

G. “Describe the sales and customer-service process.”

Present the six stages, features versus benefits, objection handling and closing; then explain service-level design, training, respect, incentives, organisation-wide responsibility and referrals.

H. “Evaluate Neighborhood Bites’ marketing options.”

State constraints and objective; compare relationship and guerrilla marketing; recommend relationship marketing; propose weekly actions, feedback cycle, measures, TAM/SAM/SOM and the supporting role of guerrilla tactics later.

I. Numerical/financial angle

Be prepared to compute:

  • revenue = units × venture’s realised price;
  • net revenue after returns;
  • simplified EBITDA/operating profit after COGS, SG&A and R&D;
  • market penetration percentage; and
  • a basic CLV estimate.

17. Rapid revision

One-minute framework

Understand → create value/MVP → research → size TAM/SAM/SOM → position and brand → choose 7Ps and low-cost techniques → sell consultatively → serve and retain → measure → adapt.

The transcript’s fuller strategic flow is: customer discovery → research → MVP → customer feedback → brand positioning → marketing → sales → customer experience → relationship marketing → retention → real-time optimisation → growth.

Must-remember lines

  • Marketing’s purpose is to make selling easier.
  • Start with the customer, not the product.
  • MVP = minimum scope, not minimum core quality.
  • 4Ps are the seller view; 4As are the buyer view.
  • 7Ps = product, price, place, promotion, people, process and physical evidence/environment.
  • Research gives information; judgment produces the answer.
  • Primary = newly collected first-hand evidence; secondary = existing published evidence.
  • Reliable research requires representativeness, good question design and sound interpretation.
  • TAM is the broad relevant opportunity; SAM is realistically serviceable; SOM is realistically obtainable/share captured.
  • Large TAM impresses; credible SOM convinces.
  • Guerrilla = unexpected impact with low cost.
  • RTM = relevant response while the moment is live.
  • Viral = voluntary large-scale sharing; digital does not necessarily become viral.
  • Relationship marketing protects trust, retention, CLV and word of mouth.
  • Brand = every consistent promise and experience, not merely logo/tagline.
  • Branding process = understand, craft and align.
  • Sales is consultative conversion; customer service is ongoing marketing.
  • Every employee creates or damages the brand.
  • Scarcity demands creativity and prioritisation; learning must lead to adjustment.
