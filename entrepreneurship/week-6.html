1. Week 6 at a glance

Week 6 connects two capabilities that determine whether an entrepreneurial idea becomes a viable business:

  1. Solving difficult problems under resource constraints. Entrepreneurs usually lack the budgets, staff, consultants, market-research capacity and certainty available to large firms.
  2. Generating revenue. A good idea, solution or process is commercially meaningful only when customers—or a viable third party—will pay for it.

The week’s core message is that startup problems are usually unstructured, cross-functional and uncertain. They must be diagnosed creatively and tested practically. The most common continuing startup problem is then: How can the venture generate more revenue? The B2C and B2B revenue-model cards supply a menu of possible answers.

2. Entrepreneurial problem solving in a resource-constrained world

2.1 Why entrepreneurial problems are different

Large companies may respond to a challenge by increasing the budget, hiring people, commissioning research or using consultants. A startup generally has:

  • limited money;
  • few people, each with competing priorities;
  • incomplete information;
  • little formal market research;
  • high uncertainty; and
  • pressure to act quickly.

The entrepreneur therefore has to prioritize carefully, use what is available and frequently work out the answer while moving forward. Confusion, slowing progress and apparent backward or sideways movement are normal. A stall does not necessarily mean failure; it often shows that the team has reached the limit of its current understanding and must change perspective.

2.2 Bricolage

Bricolage means accomplishing something with very little by creatively recombining and leveraging resources already available. The lecturer said the term itself was less important than the behavior.

Examples:

  • Give the lawyer preparing an intellectual-property agreement common stock instead of cash.
  • Share premises with another party and offer that party first rights to buy equipment or technology if it is later sold.
  • Use low-cost channels—WhatsApp customer groups, social-media status posts, word of mouth or pamphlets—when a local bakery cannot afford television advertising.

The principle is not merely “spend less.” It is find an implementable exchange or arrangement that makes scarce resources go further.

2.3 Unstructured problems require a general-manager view

Problem solving here does not mean a closed arithmetic question such as “If I sell two here and five there, how many have I sold?” It refers to complicated problems with no obvious answer.

Example: sales work well in Kerala and Karnataka but fail in Tamil Nadu. Labeling this a “marketing problem” too early narrows the search incorrectly. Possible causes include:

  • marketing execution;
  • product quality;
  • the product name;
  • packaging;
  • distribution relationships;
  • operations or shipping;
  • finance or pricing;
  • the suitability of sales personnel; or
  • a relationship/personnel issue, such as distributors holding a grudge after being insulted by senior management.

Entrepreneurs are general managers even when their formal title is in marketing or another function. They must scan every box on the organization chart rather than assume that the function in which the symptom appears also contains the cause.

2.4 Unlearning dependence on instructions

Many employees are trained to wait for a boss to define a task and give steps 1–5. An entrepreneur cannot assume that anyone will state either the real problem or the route to the answer. Independent diagnosis is essential. The instructor contrasted this with settings where open-ended problem solving is taught from childhood and emphasized that practice is necessary to move beyond instruction dependence.

2.5 Perspective shift and root cause

The live session framed the entrepreneurial sequence as:

Progress stalls → shift perspective → identify the root cause → act and learn

  • If one approach is not working, change the angle from which the problem is viewed.
  • A product problem may really be a segmentation, accessibility, adoption or finance problem.
  • Falling sales are a symptom, not an explanation.
  • Treat the root cause, not merely the most visible manifestation.

Medical analogy: a doctor does not treat “fever” blindly; the doctor asks about symptoms and diagnoses the underlying condition. Similarly, a sales decline should not trigger an automatic discount or campaign before evidence is gathered.

Possible causes of a 20% sales drop include poor customer service, a competitor’s better launch or changed customer preferences. Analytical thinking means slowing down, collecting evidence and identifying the real cause before acting.

3. Skills for effective entrepreneurial problem solving

3.2 Heinz ketchup-pouch anecdote

In the instructor’s first job after college, Heinz ketchup pouches were leaking. Although a heat-sealed plastic pouch sounds simple, solving it required learning detailed material and process knowledge. The instructor studied the domain, contributed successfully in a group and was promoted to headquarters largely because of that assignment. Exam lesson: domain understanding converts generic analytical ability into an effective solution.

3.3 Communication as implementation capacity

A solution has no operational value if it stays in a PowerPoint. Business solutions require approval and cooperation from managers, colleagues, teams and stakeholders. Effective communicators translate a complex issue into clear, relevant terms so others understand both the logic and the value. Communication is therefore not presentation polish; it is part of problem solving itself.

3.4 Additional skills raised in the live discussion

Students identified critical and analytical thinking, time management, adaptability, flexibility, emotional intelligence, fast action, evaluation, collaboration, teamwork, SWOT analysis, resilience, root-cause analysis and daily/weekly/monthly monitoring. These largely fit under the four live-session clusters:

  • Analytical thinking — evidence and root cause.
  • Resourcefulness — best use of available resources.
  • Communication — intelligible, persuasive coordination.
  • Practice and reflection — improvement through experience.

4. Problem-solving styles

Different people can face the same complaint, delay or performance problem and respond differently: one gathers data, one asks opinions and another draws diagrams. This difference is a matter of style, not necessarily intelligence. A capable managing director may prefer one style but understands the others and silently classifies the information being presented.

Style Process Strengths Limits / best use
Independent / self-regulating Identifies, analyses, solves and may implement alone Fast, focused, avoids group noise Best for small problems or when the person has strong reputation/resource power; weak for large change requiring broad buy-in
Framework/theory-based Fits the issue to a known model such as 4Ps/4As or a published case and applies its logic Gives structure, prompts systematic analysis, leverages prior knowledge Real businesses are messy; customers and competitors do not behave exactly as books predict. Test the framework rather than obey it mechanically
Collaborative / group-acceptable Discusses the problem with affected and knowledgeable parties and develops a solution they can support Multiple perspectives, stronger buy-in, smoother implementation Can be slower or noisy; should not substitute talk for evidence
Hybrid/adaptive Chooses style according to problem scale and context Combines private analysis with stakeholder knowledge Requires judgment about when to switch; often the strongest practical approach

Production-line union anecdote

The instructor once redesigned the slow end of a production line and obtained management approval. Implementation failed because unionized workers refused to cooperate and threatened a grievance; they had not been included. The technically “right” answer was operationally useless without stakeholder participation. A collaborative/group-acceptable style would have been superior.

Caution about theory

Theory can be valuable, especially when developed by someone with substantial operating experience (for example, a former CEO who later generalizes from practice). But academic work based on limited practical understanding may not fit the situation. Distinguish a useful abstraction grounded in experience from a model treated like a laboratory law.

5. Five-step creative problem-solving process

Step 1 — Clarify the problem

  • Spend substantial time understanding what is actually wrong.
  • Challenge the way the initial reporter has framed it.
  • Separate symptoms from root causes.
  • Establish scope, timing, stakeholders, location, process and evidence.
  • If the problem definition is wrong, everything downstream can be executed well and still fail.

Step 2 — Ideate

Do not begin with an uninformed brainstorming session. First learn enough about the issue so the brain has relevant material. Ideation may include:

  • brainstorming;
  • writing notes on a board;
  • building on others’ comments;
  • observation in the field;
  • customer conversations; and
  • input from knowledgeable networks.

Some ideas will be practical and others strange. Keep the space broad before evaluation.

Step 3 — Develop the solution incrementally

Avoid announcing a complete answer based on status, IQ or credentials. Develop the explanation with others, using emerging evidence. Example: if sales in a region were once strong and suddenly fell to zero, a gradual discussion may reveal that a relationship rupture is more plausible than a normal demand decline. Incremental development increases both insight and buy-in.

Step 4 — Implement

Implementation is not simply issuing an order—even a managing director needs a change plan. Identify:

  • who must support the solution;
  • who should be approached first;
  • what each stakeholder must be shown or told;
  • incentives or bonus changes required;
  • sequence, ownership, resources and timing.

A storyboard can visually represent the actions, communications and organizational path. The lesson’s phone-redesign example illustrated how a visual sequence can make implementation concrete.

Step 5 — Evaluate and improve

  • Measure whether the intervention solved the clarified problem.
  • Compare outcomes with the target/baseline.
  • Modify the solution where necessary.
  • Feed the learning into another cycle.

Creative problem solving is therefore iterative rather than a straight, guaranteed line. A facilitator may be useful if the team is genuinely stuck.

Experimentation, persistence and learning from feedback

Because the answer is uncertain, implementation should often begin as a small experiment rather than a full irreversible commitment. State the assumption, choose an observable result, test at low cost and use the result to change the next action. Feedback is not merely approval or criticism; it is information that updates the venture’s understanding.

  • Persistence means continuing toward the objective while learning and changing tactics.
  • Stubbornness means continuing the same unsupported tactic despite evidence that it is not working.
  • A failed experiment can therefore represent progress if it removes a false assumption cheaply.
  • A positive result should be replicated and measured before being treated as proof.

6. Diagnostic tools

6.1 Fishbone/cause-and-effect logic

The fishbone diagram breaks a broad problem into main causal categories and then into smaller, addressable causes. In the lesson’s material example, a “tin” or material branch might be decomposed into tin, copper, aluminium, composite or glue-related subcauses. Once evidence points to one category, the team drills down within it.

Use: prevent one-factor thinking, make cause hypotheses visible and locate a manageable cause.

6.2 Root-cause analysis and the Five Whys

Ask “why?” repeatedly and constructively across people connected with the process. The purpose is not to badger an employee, but to move from a symptom through successive causal layers until the controllable underlying mechanism is found.

6.3 The 5W1H question set

Question Diagnostic purpose Example prompts
Who? Identify affected parties, users, decision makers and influencers Which customer segment is affected? Which employees? Who decides and who influences?
What? Define the precise issue and distinguish root cause from symptom What changed? What outcome is failing?
Where? Locate the problem All branches or particular regions? Nationwide or local?
When? Find timing, sequence and patterns First or second quarter? After which event? At what process stage?
Why? Explain existence of the problem Why are employees leaving? Why are they dissatisfied?
How? Understand the current response/process Are feedback forms used? Are salary, work environment and manager communication being handled properly?

For declining sales, the “who” includes the company, sales team, managers and distributors. For employee turnover, merely observing departures is insufficient; question pay, work conditions, feedback mechanisms and management communication.

7. Customer discovery, immersion and design thinking

7.1 “Go and be the customer”

When other methods fail, mentally, physically, locationally and operationally enter the customer’s situation. Customers may know something the entrepreneur does not, may be unwilling to say it or may already be saying it in a way the entrepreneur is not hearing.

7.2 Customer discovery versus customer validation

  • Customer discovery: learn who the customer is, what problem matters, how it is currently handled and what assumptions need testing.
  • Customer validation: obtain behavioral evidence that the proposed solution and business model work—most powerfully, willingness to pay.

What people say and what they do can differ sharply. Early e-commerce surveys could receive random positive answers, while consumers remained skeptical at the payment step. Treat actual purchase, use, renewal and referral behavior as stronger evidence than stated interest.

7.3 Customer immersion

Immersion means sustained observation and participation in the customer’s real context rather than relying only on an interview. It reveals workarounds, subtle behavior and unspoken constraints.

Village anecdote: residents often already know the practical solution, but outsiders struggle to elicit it. The instructor walks with villagers, admits not knowing, helps with household tasks and builds trust. Residents eventually describe workable approaches; the entrepreneur/researcher adds wider context, helps obtain panchayat acceptance and may build research from it.

7.4 Design thinking

Design thinking begins with inspiration from customers and users: observe what they say, what they actually do and the nuances of use. The entrepreneur understands their challenges and local solutions, then builds on them with broader technical or institutional knowledge. The lesson referred to IDEO and use of this approach by the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation.

An alternative design tool is exhaustive questioning: Who benefits or resists? What are we solving? Where and when does it occur? How is it handled now? Why does it matter? The instructor’s strongest practical advice was to ask good questions of customers and knowledgeable network members. Producing the best questions and interpretations requires extensive practice and some luck.

8. Lean problem solving

8.1 Core idea

Lean looks for small details that can create large effects, including the combined effect of many small improvements. Copying visible procedures is insufficient because the capability lies in trained observation and accumulated subtle knowledge.

Toyota invited competitors to see its first US factory. Competitors thought Toyota was foolish to reveal its assembly practices, but their attempts to copy the visible process did not reproduce Toyota’s quality. They could see the steps but not the fine-grained understanding behind them.

8.2 “Go and see” (Gemba-style observation)

Toyota’s starting point is direct, deep observation:

  • study the process from many angles;
  • use slow-motion and fast-motion video;
  • inspect touch, feel and interaction of parts;
  • discuss what each observer sees;
  • challenge interpretations; and
  • continue until the team understands the event at its core.

A team might spend 20 minutes studying a bolt being tightened, viewing perhaps 100 angles and physically examining the bolt and nut to understand why they break. This is much more rigorous than a quick visual check or generic research on similar materials.

8.3 Lean sequence

  1. Go and see; understand the current condition in detail.
  2. Discuss observations and define the problem accurately.
  3. Set a specific target improvement.
  4. Ask why repeatedly and identify root causes.
  5. Develop and demonstrate a countermeasure.
  6. Implement it.
  7. Monitor the result.
  8. Standardize the improved process if it works.
  9. Continue improving.

8.4 Campus Café case synthesis

The embedded Week 6 case material applies Lean Thinking to a campus café with delays and operational inefficiency.

Correct improvement sequence

Observe → map the process → identify waste/bottleneck → find root cause → make one small improvement → measure → standardize/refine → repeat

Lean concepts in the case

  • Customer value: what the café customer actually values, such as correct food delivered without unnecessary delay.
  • Process mapping: visualize steps, handoffs, movement, queues and waiting from order to delivery.
  • Bottleneck: the capacity-constraining step that governs total flow.
  • Waste (muda): work that consumes resources without adding customer value.
  • Kaizen: ongoing, incremental improvement rather than a one-off transformation.
  • Parallel processing: carry out independent steps simultaneously rather than sequentially.
  • Standardization: make a successful new method the repeatable baseline.

Likely wastes in the café:

Waste Café manifestation
Waiting Customer/order/employee idle time
Motion Unnecessary walking or reaching
Transportation Unnecessary movement of ingredients/orders
Overprocessing Duplicate or needless steps/information entry
Defects Wrong orders and rework
Unused talent Staff insight ignored during diagnosis

Why observation comes first

Assumptions and broad external advice cannot reveal the café’s actual timing, movement, queue or handoff pattern. Observation supplies the baseline and makes later questions precise. Root-cause analysis follows observation; it should not precede evidence.

Small improvement and measurement

Lean favors a low-risk, fast, measurable change—such as reorganizing a workstation, removing a duplicated step or moving suitable tasks into parallel—over an expensive redesign. Measure cycle time, waiting time, errors, throughput or customer satisfaction before and after. A SMART target should be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.

Networks in the café case

Entrepreneurial networks complement rather than replace internal diagnosis. After the café team observes, tests and measures one improvement, it can ask experienced contacts for one quick, targeted refinement. This order:

  • provides a factual baseline;
  • produces better questions and better advice;
  • avoids crowdsourcing a mass of speculative solutions;
  • reduces analysis paralysis;
  • preserves implementation speed; and
  • uses external knowledge for refinement rather than surrendering responsibility for diagnosis.

Integrated cycle: Observe → Analyze → Improve → Measure → Learn → ask the network a targeted question → Refine → Standardize → Repeat.

9. Entrepreneurial networks

Networks add breadth and depth to the entrepreneur’s knowledge. Contacts rarely disclose every secret, but reciprocal sharing can give both parties useful direction without enabling simple imitation.

Sources include:

  • classmates, alumni and academic communities;
  • colleagues and contacts across functions and industries;
  • professional associations;
  • LinkedIn and other digital platforms;
  • customers, suppliers, managers and employees; and
  • mentors and experienced entrepreneurs.

Relationships formed now may later generate a job referral, business partnership, client introduction, investment or specialist advice. LinkedIn expands geographic and professional reach and supports both opportunity seeking and hiring. WhatsApp groups may work well for some people’s networks, though the live instructor noted that channel effectiveness is personal and context-dependent.

Mentors can shorten learning because they have already navigated similar challenges. Their thinking may appear old, but their accumulated warnings about expansion, money and resource use can prevent repeated mistakes. The appropriate posture is neither blind obedience nor dismissal: extract relevant wisdom and test it against current evidence.

Stakeholder analysis and decision roles

Before implementation, map everyone who affects or is affected by the change. Distinguish:

  • the user of the product/process;
  • the economic buyer who controls the budget;
  • the formal decision maker;
  • influencers and advisers;
  • implementers and operational staff;
  • beneficiaries; and
  • parties who may resist or lose power.

A useful map considers both interest and power: closely manage high-power/high-interest stakeholders, keep high-power/low-interest parties satisfied, keep high-interest/low-power parties informed and monitor low-power/low-interest parties proportionately. The union production-line story demonstrates why overlooking an implementation stakeholder can defeat an approved solution.

In B2B markets, users and buyers are frequently different. A staff member may use the software, a manager may sponsor it, IT/security may evaluate it, procurement may negotiate it and a senior executive may approve it. Revenue and problem-solving plans must address this buying group, not only the end user.

10. Revenue fundamentals

10.1 Revenue is the ultimate market test

A startup remains a “nice idea” until customers buy. Payment is a higher level of commitment than saying “I like it.” A LinkedIn Premium subscriber demonstrates that the platform’s added value is worth money; a free account alone does not.

Revenue matters because it demonstrates:

  1. Customer acceptance: someone regards the offering as valuable enough to sacrifice money.
  2. Market validation: actual behavior confirms interest; word of mouth and credible market evidence can reduce customer uncertainty.
  3. Business viability: revenue must support salaries, technology, marketing and other continuing expenses.

An education app can receive many downloads but little use and few subscriptions. Downloads are not sustainability. A great product that cannot generate sufficient money will struggle to survive.

10.2 Basic calculation

Gross revenue = number of units sold × price per unit

Net revenue = gross revenue − returns and allowances

Returned products require refunds and reduce revenue, so returns should be minimized at a reasonable cost.

10.3 What a revenue model decides

A revenue model specifies how the venture sells, prices and captures value. It answers:

  • Who uses the product?
  • Who pays?
  • What value is monetized?
  • What unit or event triggers payment?
  • Is income one-time, recurring, usage-based, commission-based or indirect?

Do not assume one model is automatically best. Select four or five plausible models, test them and retain or combine those that fit customer behavior and economics.

10.4 Four broad revenue-model families

  1. Value proposition: charge for a valued outcome, feeling, risk reduction or convenience.
  2. Third party pays: the user may pay little or nothing because another party values access, data, referrals or transactions.
  3. Free/non-monetary for the user: obtain contribution, data, barter, community value or conversion to paid use.
  4. Pricing tactics: change the timing, unit, bundle or pattern of payment.

There is no truly “free” business in aggregate: somebody supplies money or another economically useful resource.

Model Mechanism Example
Flat rate One predictable price for a defined business service B2B equivalent of consumer flat-rate pricing
Subscription Recurring fee for continuing access Common software/service model
Leasing Ongoing payment for asset access rather than ownership Equipment/property access
Pay per use Charge for each job/time/use Atlas Copco-type tunneling equipment is rented for the highway tunnel and returned rather than retained
Dynamic pricing Price follows demand, availability or system conditions Tesla commercial solar Powerpack stores surplus energy and makes it available when required, enabling time/condition-sensitive value
Pre-sales Discount early access before general release; convert later to regular purchases A selected company tests and learns the offering, then may purchase more at normal price
Upselling Sell higher capacity/performance BASF/agriculture seller shows how equipment can handle two hectares in one hour rather than four
Razor-and-blade / lifecycle revenue Discount initial hardware/access and earn recurring fees later T-Mobile/Vodafone-type enterprise cellular deals: discounted devices/equipment first, then higher regular service/equipment fees over the relationship
Co-investment Customer and provider share investment/cost where continuing usage or recurring expense creates mutual value Related to recurring-consumable/lifecycle arrangements; the lecture treated it briefly as similar to other models

13. High-value distinctions and common traps

Distinction Correct interpretation
Symptom vs root cause Sales decline, delay or turnover is observable; the root cause explains why it occurs
Problem definition vs solution design First define and bound the real problem; only then generate/design interventions
Customer discovery vs validation Discovery learns problems and assumptions; validation obtains behavioral/market evidence, especially payment
Interview vs immersion An interview reports what a person says; immersion observes the real setting, workarounds and unspoken behavior
Observation vs assumption Observation establishes the actual process and baseline; assumptions are hypotheses to test
Bottleneck vs waste A bottleneck constrains total throughput; waste consumes resources without customer value. A step may be one, both or neither
Persistence vs stubbornness Persistence continues learning and adapts the method; stubbornness repeats an unsupported method despite contrary evidence
Independent vs collaborative solving Independent analysis is efficient for bounded issues; collaboration is vital where knowledge and implementation power are distributed
Lean diagnosis vs network advice Lean creates evidence and a tested baseline; networks then provide focused refinements
B2C vs B2B buying B2C often involves an individual user/buyer and emotion/status/convenience; B2B commonly has multiple users, influencers, decision makers, procurement constraints and switching/process costs
Revenue vs profit Revenue is incoming sales value before all expenses; profit remains after costs. Revenue validates demand but must ultimately support viability

Common misconceptions to avoid:

  • “A marketing symptom must have a marketing cause.”
  • “Brainstorm immediately, before learning the context.”
  • “The smartest person’s technically correct answer will be implemented automatically.”
  • “A framework guarantees a solution.”
  • “More downloads, survey interest or compliments equal market validation.”
  • “Free means nobody pays or contributes value.”
  • “Pricing means only selecting one sticker price.”
  • “Lean means cost cutting only.”
  • “External networks should solve the problem before the team observes its own process.”
  • “A large redesign is more innovative than a small measurable improvement.”

14. Practical answer frameworks for exams

14.1 If asked to solve a startup problem

Use this sequence:

  1. State the visible symptom without accepting it as the cause.
  2. Apply 5W1H and define scope, timing and stakeholders.
  3. Observe customers/process directly and gather proportionate data.
  4. Map causes (fishbone) and use Five Whys to locate the root cause.
  5. Shift perspective across marketing, finance, operations, people, distribution and technology.
  6. Generate multiple ideas using an appropriate independent/collaborative/hybrid style.
  7. Screen for feasibility under resource constraints; use bricolage.
  8. Develop one solution incrementally with stakeholder buy-in.
  9. Set a measurable target and run a small experiment.
  10. Measure, standardize if successful, consult the network for a targeted refinement and repeat.

14.2 If asked to design a revenue model

  1. Define the user, buyer, payer and beneficiaries.
  2. State the value: convenience, risk reduction, privacy, status, savings, access, outcome, etc.
  3. Generate alternatives from all four families.
  4. Specify the payment unit and timing.
  5. Show an example and why the customer/third party will pay.
  6. Estimate revenue: units × price, less expected returns/allowances.
  7. Identify customer-acquisition, delivery, switching, legal/privacy and sustainability implications.
  8. Test four or five plausible models with real behavior, not only stated preference.

14.3 Likely exam questions

  • Explain why entrepreneurial problem solving differs from corporate problem solving.
  • Define bricolage and give entrepreneurial examples.
  • Discuss the skills required for effective problem solving.
  • Compare independent, theory-based and collaborative styles.
  • Explain the five-stage creative problem-solving process.
  • Apply fishbone, Five Whys and 5W1H to declining sales or employee turnover.
  • Explain customer immersion/design thinking with the village example.
  • Describe Toyota’s lean problem-solving approach and why competitors could not simply copy it.
  • Apply Lean/Kaizen to the Campus Café case, including sequencing and the role of networks.
  • Explain why revenue is the ultimate test of a venture.
  • Calculate revenue and explain returns and allowances.
  • Compare B2C and B2B revenue models with suitable examples.
  • Distinguish freemium, subscription, pay per use, dynamic pricing, fractional ownership, crowdfunding and razor-and-blade.
  • Explain negative working capital using Dell/Amazon.
  • Discuss ethical/legal cautions in data selling, lock-in, transparency and platform practices.

15. Rapid revision

Fifteen points to remember

  1. Entrepreneurs solve uncertain, cross-functional problems with limited resources.
  2. Bricolage = make do creatively with what is available and leverage others’ resources.
  3. A stall marks the boundary of current understanding; shift perspective.
  4. Treat sales decline, delay and turnover as symptoms until root cause is established.
  5. Entrepreneurs think like general managers, not isolated functional specialists.
  6. Effective solving requires detail, communication, feasibility, decisiveness, data, domain knowledge, resourcefulness and practice.
  7. Match the problem-solving style to the context; hybrid approaches are often strongest.
  8. Creative process: Clarify → Ideate → Develop incrementally → Implement → Evaluate/improve.
  9. Diagnostic set: Fishbone + Five Whys + 5W1H + direct observation.
  10. Design thinking starts by being with customers and observing real use.
  11. Lean: Go see → target → root cause → countermeasure → implement → monitor → standardize → repeat.
  12. Networks complement evidence-based internal analysis; use them for targeted refinement.
  13. Revenue is behavioral proof of customer acceptance and a prerequisite for viability.
  14. Revenue = units × price − returns/allowances.
  15. Explore and test multiple revenue structures; do not default automatically to simple unit sales.

Revenue-model memory map

  • Value: privacy, loss avoidance, vanity, gifting, exclusivity, convenience, surprise, satisfaction, risk reduction, cost savings, sustainability, transparency.
  • Third party: advertising, marketplace, data, referral, matchmaking, cross-subsidy.
  • Free/access: contribution/data, tip jar, freemium, barter, partnerships, standards.
  • Price/timing: flat rate, subscription, lease, pay per use, dynamic pricing, one-time fee, upsell, pre-sale/crowdfunding, fractional ownership, razor-and-blade, co-investment.

One-line integrated model

Observe reality → define the root problem → create and test a resource-feasible solution → secure stakeholder/customer acceptance → choose and validate how value will be paid for → measure, learn and improve continuously.
