2. Why Vision Transformers?

Before ViTs, image classification, object detection, visual question answering, image captioning, and segmentation were handled mainly by convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and related architectures. Computer-vision attention was usually attached to a CNN or used to replace selected CNN components while retaining the convolutional structure.

The original ViT paper, “An Image Is Worth 16×16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale” (Alexey Dosovitskiy et al.), demonstrated that this dependence on convolution is not necessary. A pure Transformer encoder can operate directly on a sequence of image patches and perform image classification. It was the first work highlighted in the lecture to train a Transformer encoder successfully on ImageNet and obtain results competitive with established convolutional architectures.

2.1 CNN processing

A CNN processes an image hierarchically:

  • early convolutional layers detect local primitives such as edges and corners;
  • intermediate layers combine them into textures and shapes;
  • deeper layers combine those into objects and higher-level patterns;
  • pooling progressively aggregates information and expands the effective receptive field.

This local, layer-by-layer inductive bias is excellent for local feature extraction. The weakness is not that CNNs cannot ever represent global context; rather, distant spatial regions communicate only through multiple layers or deliberately designed global mechanisms. Capturing a subtle relationship between far-apart regions may therefore be indirect, deep, and computationally demanding.

2.2 ViT processing

A ViT stacks self-attention layers that relate representations across all spatial regions. In principle, every patch can directly attend to every other patch from the first Transformer layer. This makes global context and long-range dependencies explicit and often more effective than the hierarchical route in a CNN.

The central shift is:

\[ \text{pixels processed by local convolution} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{patch tokens related by global self-attention}. \]

Important caveat: patchification alone does not create global reasoning; multi-head self-attention across the patch sequence does. CNNs remain strong at local features and can model global information with sufficient depth or architectural additions. The comparison is about inductive bias and path length, not an absolute capability/no-capability distinction.

3. ViT architecture: turning an image into a token sequence

3.1 Patchification

For an input image of height \(H\), width \(W\), and channels \(C\), divide it into non-overlapping square patches of size \(P\times P\). The number of patches is

\[ N=\frac{H}{P}\times\frac{W}{P}=\frac{HW}{P^2}. \]

Example: a \(224\times224\) RGB image split into \(16\times16\) patches gives

\[ N=\frac{224}{16}\times\frac{224}{16}=14\times14=196 \]

patches. Each raw patch contains \(P^2C=16\times16\times3=768\) values.

3.2 Flattening and linear projection

Each 2-D patch is flattened into a vector and mapped through a learned linear projection to an embedding of dimension \(D\):

\[ \mathbf{x}_p\in\mathbb{R}^{P^2C} \quad\rightarrow\quad \mathbf{x}_p\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}. \]

The resulting patch embeddings play the role that word/token embeddings play in NLP. This is the lecture’s central analogy:

NLP Transformer Vision Transformer
Sentence split into tokens Image split into patches
Token embedded into a vector Flattened patch projected into a vector
Positional encoding preserves word order Positional embedding preserves patch location
Self-attention relates words Self-attention relates image regions
Head predicts language/task output Classification head predicts image category

Patch embedding also converts a 2-D spatial input into a 1-D sequence suitable for a standard Transformer encoder. Calling it “dimensionality reduction” is intuitive but incomplete: the learned projection primarily puts every patch into a common representation space.

3.3 Position and classification token

Because attention by itself does not know spatial order, positional embeddings are added to patch embeddings. A learnable classification token ([CLS]) is commonly prepended. After encoding, its output representation summarizes the image for classification. Some variants instead pool patch representations.

3.4 Transformer encoder

The token sequence passes through repeated encoder blocks containing:

  • layer normalization;
  • multi-head self-attention;
  • residual/skip connections;
  • a feed-forward multilayer perceptron (MLP);
  • another residual path and normalization according to implementation.

Scaled dot-product attention is

\[ \operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V) =\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V. \]

Queries ask what information a patch needs; keys describe what each patch offers; values contain the information aggregated. Multi-head attention learns several relationship types simultaneously—for example shape, texture, object part, or far-apart contextual relationships.

3.5 Global representation and classification head

The encoder produces globally informed representations. A classification head, often a fully connected layer, maps the learned image representation to output categories. The essential pipeline is:

\[ \text{image}\rightarrow\text{fixed patches}\rightarrow\text{flatten/project} \rightarrow\text{add position}\rightarrow\text{Transformer encoder} \rightarrow\text{global representation}\rightarrow\text{class head}. \]

No convolution is required in a pure ViT. With large-scale pretraining, pure ViTs achieved state-of-the-art accuracy and transferred effectively to mid-sized or smaller benchmarks such as ImageNet, CIFAR-100, and VTAB, while the paper reported substantially fewer training resources than comparable state-of-the-art CNNs in that setting.

3.6 Architectural capability versus training regime

Keep two questions separate:

  • Architectural capability: can global attention capture long-range relationships? Yes.
  • Training regime: does the model have enough suitable data, regularization, compute, and pretraining to learn useful attention? Not necessarily.

ViTs have weaker built-in locality/translation inductive biases than CNNs and may perform poorly on small datasets when trained from scratch. Their strongest original results depended on pretraining on very large datasets followed by transfer learning. A hybrid CNN–Transformer can use a CNN front end for local features and a Transformer for global relationships, but this is not the definition of ViT.

4. Major ViT variants

4.1 DeiT — Data-efficient Image Transformers

Developed by Facebook AI/Meta AI, DeiT uses more efficient training and knowledge distillation so ViT-style models can learn effectively with less data. The released distilled models can be used with ViT/image-classification tooling.

Lecture-listed variants:

  • deit-tiny-patch16-224
  • deit-small-patch16-224
  • deit-base-patch16-224
  • deit-base-patch16-384

Use the DeiT image processor to prepare images in the expected format. DeiT is not the same as DINO: DeiT is data-efficient/distillation-oriented supervised training; DINO is self-supervised representation learning.

4.2 BEiT — BERT Pre-Training of Image Transformers

Developed by Microsoft Research. BEiT adapts BERT-like masked modeling to images: image patches/tokens are masked and the model learns to predict discrete visual tokens, with tokenization linked to a VQ-VAE. The lecture states that this self-supervised method can outperform supervised-pretrained ViTs.

4.3 DINO — self-distillation with no labels

DINO is Facebook AI’s self-supervised training method for ViTs. A student network learns to match a teacher network’s output under different augmented views; teacher parameters are updated from the student rather than by ordinary labeled supervision. DINO representations show emergent properties not generally observed in conventional CNN training—notably, attention maps can delineate/segment objects even though the model was never explicitly trained for segmentation. Checkpoints are available on model hubs.

4.4 MAE — Masked Autoencoders

MAE pretrains a ViT by masking a high proportion of image patches (the lecture uses about 75%) and reconstructing their pixel values. It uses an asymmetric design: the encoder processes visible patches while a lighter decoder performs reconstruction. After fine-tuning, this simple self-supervised method can outperform supervised pretraining.

4.5 Swin Transformer

Mentioned in the diabetic-retinopathy case as a more recent alternative. Swin uses local/windowed attention and a hierarchical structure, reducing the quadratic cost of global attention and providing multi-scale features. It can be a good choice where fine detail and computational efficiency matter, though increased architectural complexity must be justified empirically.

4.6 Comparison worth memorizing

Method Main training idea Labels required for pretraining? Characteristic strength
ViT Patch tokens + Transformer encoder Usually in original supervised setup Global context without convolution
DeiT Efficient training + distillation Generally yes/distillation signal Data-efficient ViT training
BEiT Predict masked discrete image tokens No BERT-style visual pretraining
DINO Teacher–student self-distillation No Strong semantic features; emergent segmentation
MAE Reconstruct heavily masked patches No Efficient representation learning with ~75% masking

5. Vision applications and practical use cases

5.1 Image classification

Input: an image. Output: one or more image-level labels. Examples include normal/abnormal medical scans, disease severity from retinal fundus images, or product-category recognition. A classification head maps the global representation to class probabilities.

5.2 Object detection with DETR

Input: an image. Output: a set of object classes plus bounding boxes. Practical examples include localizing tumors or lesions, road users in autonomous driving, defects in manufacturing, or pests in crop images.

5.3 Semantic segmentation

Input: an image. Output: a class for each pixel. The lecture names SETR (transcribed inconsistently as SCTR/SATR), a Transformer-based semantic-segmentation model:

  1. encode the image using patches;
  2. apply a Transformer encoder to learn global dependencies between patches;
  3. decode feature maps into pixel-level segmentation masks.

It can be trained on a large dataset such as ADE20K. At inference, the model returns a mask predicting each pixel’s class. Microsoft Research results were cited as attaining state-of-the-art performance on segmentation benchmarks. Medical uses include outlining anatomical structures, tumors, and pathological regions for diagnosis and treatment planning.

5.4 Medical image analysis

Target modalities include X-ray, CT, MRI, and fundus images. Three task formulations are distinct:

  • classification: normal versus abnormal, disease type, or severity;
  • detection: identify/localize lesions or tumors with bounding boxes;
  • segmentation: trace anatomy or pathology at pixel level.

Core challenges:

  • data scarcity: medical datasets are limited, yet AI models are data hungry;
  • labeling complexity: accurate annotation requires experts and is time-consuming;
  • class imbalance: rare severe disease may be clinically important but statistically scarce;
  • interpretability: clinicians must understand and trust model decisions;
  • domain shift: scanners, hospitals, populations, lighting, and acquisition protocols differ;
  • compute: ViTs can be demanding.

Responses include data augmentation/synthetic data, transfer learning from large natural-image or medical-image datasets, domain-specific pretraining, explainable-AI/attention visualization, accelerator hardware, optimized implementations, and external validation.

6. DETR: end-to-end object detection with Transformers

6.1 Motivation

Traditional detection is a complex pipeline with region/anchor generation, candidate scoring, box refinement, and non-maximum suppression (NMS) to remove duplicate overlapping boxes. These hand-designed components encode prior knowledge and increase engineering and computational complexity.

DETR (DEtection TRansformer, from Facebook AI Research) reframes detection as direct set prediction. It uses a CNN backbone in the original implementation to create image features, plus a Transformer encoder–decoder and a fixed set of learned object queries. Thus, DETR is Transformer-based but the original DETR is not a pure patch-only ViT detector.

The research motivation is especially intuitive for occluded or partially visible objects: correct detection may depend on relationships between one part of an image and distant parts. Global attention allows features from all regions to interact.

6.2 Architecture and inference

  1. An image enters the feature extractor/backbone.
  2. The Transformer encoder forms globally contextualized image features.
  3. A fixed number of learned object queries enters the decoder.
  4. Each query produces a class prediction and bounding-box prediction.
  5. Predictions form an unordered set; unused queries predict “no object”/background.

The lecture implementation uses 100 queries, hence up to 100 predicted boxes per image; this is configurable. Object queries are learned slots, not proposals generated by a region-proposal network.

6.3 Bipartite/Hungarian matching

Training must decide which predicted slot corresponds to which ground-truth object. DETR computes a one-to-one assignment minimizing a matching cost:

\[ \hat\sigma=\arg\min_{\sigma}\sum_i \mathcal{C}(y_i,\hat y_{\sigma(i)}). \]

The Hungarian algorithm solves the bipartite assignment. A prediction is matched to at most one ground-truth object and vice versa. Unmatched predictions learn the no-object class. This unique assignment discourages duplicate predictions and is a principal reason DETR can eliminate conventional NMS.

Do not confuse stages:

  • Hungarian matching is primarily a training-time assignment mechanism.
  • NMS is conventionally an inference/post-processing mechanism.
  • DETR’s set-based training makes NMS unnecessary in its standard formulation.

6.4 Losses

The hands-on material emphasizes three components:

  1. classification loss for object labels;
  2. bounding-box regression loss;
  3. loss/no-object coefficient for the background class.

The lecture calls the combined objective a set-based global loss and uses HungarianMatcher plus SetCriterion. Bounding boxes must be normalized correctly, and the update log notes the main class should use label 0. An earlier demonstration accidentally visualized target boxes instead of predicted boxes; this was corrected—an important warning when validating output code.

6.5 DETR versus traditional detectors

Aspect Traditional detector DETR
Candidate generation Anchors/region proposals Learned object queries
Duplicate control NMS One-to-one matching/set loss
Context Often local/hierarchical Transformer global context
Pipeline Multiple designed stages End-to-end set prediction
Output Candidate boxes then filtering Fixed prediction set + no-object slots

The LMS case suggests training on a large detection dataset such as COCO and reports competitive performance against approaches such as Faster R-CNN. COCO-pretrained DETR originally has 91 object classes plus a background/no-object class; adapt the classification head to the custom number of classes.

7. Case study: building a medical-image ViT system

7.1 General workflow

For an organization such as Amrita Hospital:

  1. Define the task and clinical consequence. Decide whether the system classifies disease, detects lesions, segments pathology, or combines tasks.
  2. Assemble data. Collect a large, diverse dataset with pathologies and expert annotations.
  3. Preprocess. Standardize image size, format, channels, and normalization.
  4. Augment. Apply clinically safe transforms such as rotation, flipping, and cropping; use augmentation to increase diversity and help class imbalance.
  5. Select architecture. Choose original ViT, Swin/hierarchical ViT, or another variant; tune layer count and attention heads to task complexity and compute.
  6. Pretrain/transfer. Use large natural-image or medical-image pretraining to learn broad representations.
  7. Fine-tune. Train on the target disease classification/detection task, e.g. cross-entropy with Adam.
  8. Split properly. Maintain train, validation, and held-out test sets, preferably by patient rather than image to prevent leakage. The lecturer demonstrates trying a range of split proportions (from roughly 50:50 toward 99:1) and selecting empirically, but sound practice requires a sufficiently large untouched test set rather than choosing the split solely by maximum accuracy.
  9. Evaluate. Report accuracy, precision, recall/sensitivity, F1, ROC-AUC, confusion matrix, and class-specific errors.
  10. Compare baselines. Compare with a CNN on identical splits and evaluation rules.
  11. Interpret and validate. Visualize attention/attributions, review errors with clinicians, and test on external data.
  12. Deploy cautiously. Use optimized code/hardware acceleration and monitor safety, drift, latency, and resource cost.

The lecture cites lung-cancer diagnosis from CT scans as an example where ViTs achieved performance comparable or superior to CNNs. The stated advantage is better capture of long-range dependencies, subtle visual patterns, and whole-image context; the caveat is greater computational demand.

7.2 Diabetic-retinopathy screening

Objective: develop an accurate and efficient early-screening system using retinal fundus images and diabetic-retinopathy (DR) severity labels.

Pipeline:

  • gather fundus images with expert severity labels;
  • standardize size, format, color/normalization, and image quality;
  • augment with rotation, flipping, and cropping when these preserve clinical meaning;
  • select a ViT or Swin Transformer and tune layers/heads based on task and compute;
  • use pretrained representations;
  • extract high-level features and attach a fully connected classification head for DR severity;
  • train/validate/test with an appropriate loss and optimizer;
  • evaluate class-wise, compare against a CNN, and assess operational cost.

7.3 Radiomed review case: data and decision problem

The review case asks whether Radiomed Center should retain an efficient original ViT or move to a more expensive hierarchical-attention variant for DR grading. The key issue is not “which architecture is theoretically newer?” but which validated system meets the clinical requirement at acceptable cost.

The data layer includes two sources:

  • a smaller local dataset, valuable because it reflects the deployment population and equipment;
  • a larger public dataset, useful for scale and representation learning but potentially different in labeling policy, prevalence, camera, image quality, or patient demographics.

Combining sources without auditing them may create domain shift or inconsistent labels. Split by patient and, where possible, preserve site/source information. Evaluate local and public-domain performance separately as well as jointly.

7.4 Why accuracy is inadequate

DR severity is typically imbalanced. A model can score high overall accuracy by predicting common mild/normal categories while missing rare severe cases. In screening, a severe false negative may delay referral and treatment, so sensitivity/recall for clinically severe classes is usually more important than raw accuracy.

For a positive/severe class:

\[ \text{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN} \]

\[ \text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} \]

\[ \text{Recall/Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]

\[ F_1=2\frac{\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}} {\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}} \]

Specificity is \(TN/(TN+FP)\). AUC summarizes ranking/discrimination across thresholds but does not choose the clinical threshold. Radiomed must select an operating threshold that gives acceptable severe-class sensitivity while keeping false-positive referrals operationally manageable.

7.5 Class-weighted loss

One lower-cost intervention is to weight rare/severe classes more heavily in cross-entropy:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{weighted}=-\sum_c w_c y_c\log p_c. \]

This makes errors on high-weight classes contribute more to optimization and can improve severe-class recall without changing the base architecture. But weighting cannot repair mislabeled images, absent visual evidence, poor-quality scans, or severe domain shift. Excessive weighting may also increase false positives or destabilize calibration.

7.6 Label noise is a data-governance problem

If the public dataset contains noisy severity labels, a more complex model may learn that noise more confidently. Complexity should not hide unresolved data quality. Audit labels through:

  • expert review of a stratified sample, especially severe cases and model disagreements;
  • inter-rater agreement and adjudication;
  • duplicate and leakage checks;
  • image-quality checks;
  • source-specific confusion matrices;
  • review of high-loss and high-confidence disagreement cases.

7.7 Augmentation caveat

Augmentation expands the effective training distribution and regularizes the model, but it does not create new pathology. A transformation is valid only when it preserves the diagnosis. Aggressive crop, blur, color alteration, or geometric distortion could remove lesions or change clinically relevant appearance. Validate augmentation policy with domain experts.

7.8 Decision workflow for Radiomed

  1. establish a clinically reviewed evaluation set;
  2. evaluate current ViT class-wise, not only by overall accuracy;
  3. analyze severe false negatives and their causes;
  4. test class-weighted loss, threshold tuning, balanced sampling, and calibration;
  5. compare a hierarchical variant under identical data/splits;
  6. run a controlled pilot;
  7. measure clinical outcomes and computational outcomes (GPU cost, latency, training time, maintenance).

If the efficient original ViT plus weighting/threshold tuning reaches the required sensitivity, retain it. If sensitivity remains clinically inadequate and the hierarchical model produces a reproducible improvement, its additional cost becomes justifiable. The governing principle is:

Use the least resource-intensive validated solution that satisfies the clinical requirement.

The lecture sketches an ImageRetrievalModel initialized with an indexed image set. retrieve_images(query_image):

    def retrieve_images(self, query_image):

Cosine similarity is a practical comparison:

\[ \cos(\mathbf q,\mathbf x)= \frac{\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}{\|\mathbf q\|\|\mathbf x\|}. \]

9.3 AverageMeter

The helper stores current value, running sum, item count, and average:

class AverageMeter:
    def __init__(self):
        self.reset()

    def reset(self):
        self.val = self.avg = self.sum = self.count = 0

    def update(self, val, n=1):
        self.val = val
        self.sum += val * n
        self.count += n
        self.avg = self.sum / self.count

It is used to average losses/metrics across batches or epochs.

9.5 Reproducibility

Seed Python random, PYTHONHASHSEED, NumPy, PyTorch CPU, and CUDA RNGs. Configure cuDNN flags. The lecture sets both deterministic and benchmark flags to true; in practice those goals can conflict, so prioritize deterministic settings when strict reproducibility is required.

9.6 Data preparation and stratified folds

Read train.csv. Bounding boxes are stored as strings; strip brackets, parse numeric values with np.fromstring, stack them, and assign columns such as x, y, w, h, then drop the original bbox column in place.

Create image-level folds so boxes from the same image do not leak across train and validation:

  1. copy unique image_id values;
  2. count bounding boxes per image;
  3. attach the image source;
  4. form a stratification group combining source and a bucket derived from box count (the lecture uses box count modulo 15);
  5. apply StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42);
  6. assign each validation index its fold number.

The purpose is to preserve both source distribution and approximate object-count distribution across folds.

  1. Build the custom dataset and data loaders.
  2. Load COCO-pretrained DETR.
  3. Replace/adapt the classification head for the target class count.
  4. Retain or change the 100-query setting.

10. Common exam traps and corrections

  1. “ViT is a CNN with attention.” False. A pure ViT patchifies pixels and uses Transformer encoders without convolution.
  2. “ViT requires a CNN feature extractor.” False for pure ViT; true only for some hybrid designs.
  3. “CNNs cannot capture global context.” Too strong. They capture it indirectly with depth/receptive-field growth; ViT provides shorter direct attention paths.
  4. “CNNs are poor at local features.” False; locality is a CNN strength.
  5. “Patch tokenization causes long-range reasoning.” False; self-attention across tokens does.
  6. “ViT always beats CNN on small data.” False; ViTs often need large-scale pretraining/transfer.
  7. “DINO is supervised classification.” False; it is self-supervised teacher–student distillation.
  8. “MAE predicts labels for masked patches.” False; it reconstructs masked visual content/pixels.
  9. “DETR is merely a Transformer after a traditional detector.” False; it reformulates detection as end-to-end set prediction, although original DETR does use a CNN image backbone.
  10. “Object queries are region proposals.” False; they are learned decoder slots.
  11. “DETR needs NMS.” Standard DETR avoids NMS through one-to-one set matching.
  12. “Hungarian matching filters boxes at inference.” False; it assigns predictions to targets during training.
  13. “High medical-image accuracy proves safety.” False under imbalance; inspect severe-class sensitivity and false negatives.
  14. “AUC chooses the deployment threshold.” False; choose a clinically justified operating point.
  15. “More model complexity fixes bad labels.” False; data-quality defects require auditing/adjudication.

11. High-yield answer templates

Explain ViT in five steps

  1. Divide the image into fixed-size patches.
  2. Flatten and linearly embed each patch; add position information and usually a class token.
  3. Apply repeated multi-head self-attention/MLP encoder blocks.
  4. Learn global relationships because every patch can attend to every other patch.
  5. Feed the global/class representation to a prediction head; note data/compute requirements and the value of pretraining.

Explain why ViT can outperform a CNN

Mention direct global attention, long-range dependencies, transfer from large-scale pretraining, rich global representations, and state-of-the-art benchmark performance. Then qualify: CNNs have beneficial locality biases and may be superior with limited data; attention cost and compute can be substantial.

Explain DETR

Define end-to-end set prediction; describe backbone, encoder, decoder, learned object queries, class/box heads; explain Hungarian one-to-one matching, no-object slots, and why NMS/anchor engineering are unnecessary; mention custom class-head adaptation and matching/class/box losses.

Solve a medical-ViT case

State the clinical task and cost of errors; audit/collect data; prevent patient leakage; preprocess and clinically validate augmentation; use transfer learning; compare ViT/CNN/hierarchical choices; address imbalance with weighting/sampling; report class-wise sensitivity, precision, F1, AUC, calibration, and confusion matrices; tune threshold; explain predictions; conduct external validation and a controlled pilot; include compute and workflow impact.

Final integrated picture

Week 15 presents three linked transitions:

\[ \boxed{\text{local convolution}\rightarrow\text{patch tokens + global attention}} \]

\[ \boxed{\text{label-dependent pretraining}\rightarrow\text{self-supervised visual learning}} \]

\[ \boxed{\text{multi-stage detection + NMS}\rightarrow\text{end-to-end set prediction}} \]

Architecture alone does not determine success. A defensible system joins four layers:

  • data: label quality, image quality, class balance, source/domain, augmentation;
  • model: ViT/self-attention or hierarchical attention, transfer learning, loss design;
  • evaluation: accuracy plus class-wise precision, recall, F1, AUC, thresholding, and error analysis;
  • operations: safety, explainability, GPU cost, latency, training time, and deployment feasibility.

For an exam case, the strongest conclusion is evidence-based and conditional: choose the simplest validated architecture that achieves the task’s required clinical or business outcome, and never use model complexity to conceal unresolved data-quality or evaluation problems.
