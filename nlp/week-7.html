1. Week overview

Week 7 develops the complete supervised text-classification pipeline: turn raw text into numerical features, learn a classifier from labelled examples, evaluate it correctly, interpret its mistakes, and deploy it for a practical business problem. The principal running examples are 20 Newsgroups topic classification, IMDb movie-review sentiment analysis, and customer-feedback/incident/document classification case studies.

The core idea is:

Raw text → cleaning/normalisation → feature representation → train/validation/test split → model training → prediction → evaluation, error analysis, refinement and deployment.

Text classification assigns one or more predefined labels to a document. It is a supervised-learning task because training examples contain both the input text and the correct label. Prediction comes only after the model has learned from labelled examples.

Typical applications mentioned in the week are:

  • Sentiment analysis: positive, negative or neutral mood in social-media posts, product/movie reviews or feedback.
  • Spam/fraud detection: classify mail or messages as wanted versus unsolicited/malicious.
  • Customer-query auto-tagging and routing: direct billing, technical, delivery or complaint queries to the correct team.
  • Topic/document categorisation: sports versus science news; policy, budget, legal or HR documents.
  • Intent detection: infer what a user wants in a chatbot or virtual assistant.
  • Public labelling and content organisation: impose useful categories on large collections of free-form text.

Structured versus unstructured data

Free-form reviews, chats, posts, surveys, reports and message bodies are difficult for conventional ML models to consume directly. Classification gives them useful labels and therefore makes them easier to search, aggregate and analyse. Structured data normally has a consistent schema—rows, columns and aligned fields/labels—whereas unstructured text does not have a fixed feature layout.

Important caveat: the live lecture sometimes called an email or named customer feedback “structured” merely because metadata such as sender/name exists. More precisely, the metadata may be structured while the message/review body remains unstructured. Likewise, classification does not literally convert prose into a database table; it produces structured labels or numerical representations for the prose.

2. Feature representation

Machine-learning classifiers cannot use raw strings directly. Each document must be transformed into a numerical feature vector.

2.1 Bag of Words (BoW)

BoW constructs a corpus vocabulary and represents each document by token counts.

Example: delay delay broken gives delay = 2, broken = 1.

Strengths: simple, fast, sparse, interpretable and often a useful baseline.
Limitations: ignores word order and context; treats synonyms such as ship and vessel as unrelated dimensions; raw counts can overemphasise words common everywhere.

2.2 TF–IDF

TF–IDF means Term Frequency × Inverse Document Frequency. It rewards a word that is frequent in a particular document but discounts a word that occurs in many documents.

For term (t), document (d), and corpus (D):

\[ TF(t,d)=\frac{\text{count of }t\text{ in }d}{\text{total terms in }d} \]

\[ IDF(t,D)=\log\left(\frac{N}{df(t)}\right) \]

where (N) is the number of documents and (df(t)) is the number containing (t). Implementations commonly add smoothing, for example \(\log((1+N)/(1+df))+1\), to avoid division/zero-edge problems.

\[ TFIDF(t,d,D)=TF(t,d)\times IDF(t,D) \]

  • A term repeated within one document receives higher TF.
  • A term present in nearly every document receives low IDF and is penalised.
  • A rare, document-specific term receives high IDF.
  • The resulting TF–IDF values form the feature vector fed into the classifier.

Lecture check: if election appears in 900 of 1,000 news documents, its IDF is low and it is less discriminative within that corpus. “Rare” does not always mean genuinely useful, however; typos and IDs can also be rare, so cleaning and frequency thresholds matter.

Example: in SmartHome reviews, the should receive little weight, while delay, broken or connectivity can receive greater weight when they distinguish a subset of reviews.

max_df=0.7 removes terms occurring in more than 70% of documents. Stopword removal and document-frequency filtering reduce vocabulary and noise before final weighting.

2.3 What TF–IDF does not do

TF–IDF represents statistical importance, not meaning. It does not inherently know that ship and vessel are related. Lowercasing and stemming reduce surface variation but still do not solve synonymy.

For semantic similarity, use distributed word representations such as Word2Vec or GloVe, where semantically related words lie near one another in vector space. Pre-trained embeddings transfer relationships learned from a large external corpus. Contextual models can go further by representing a word differently according to its sentence context.

3. Text preprocessing and normalisation

The demonstrated IMDb pipeline performs the following operations:

  1. Load the CSV and inspect shape, head() and describe().
  2. Inspect sentiment.value_counts() to check class balance.
  3. Remove HTML with BeautifulSoup and get_text().
  4. Remove bracketed content with a regular expression.
  5. Remove special characters and, optionally, digits.
  6. Tokenise (the lecture uses ToktokTokenizer; word_tokenize/sent_tokenize are also imported).
  7. Normalise case where appropriate.
  8. Stem with PorterStemmer (or use LancasterStemmer) and/or lemmatise with WordNetLemmatizer.
  9. Remove English stopwords.
  10. Apply the same learned transformation consistently to train, validation and test data.

Illustrative corrected functions:

import re
from bs4 import BeautifulSoup
from nltk.tokenize.toktok import ToktokTokenizer
from nltk.corpus import stopwords
from nltk.stem import PorterStemmer

tokenizer = ToktokTokenizer()
stopword_list = set(stopwords.words("english"))

def strip_html(text):
    return BeautifulSoup(text, "html.parser").get_text()

def remove_between_square_brackets(text):
    return re.sub(r"\[[^]]*\]", "", text)

def denoise_text(text):
    return remove_between_square_brackets(strip_html(text))

def remove_special_characters(text, remove_digits=True):
    pattern = r"[^a-zA-Z\s]" if remove_digits else r"[^a-zA-Z0-9\s]"
    return re.sub(pattern, "", text)

def simple_stemmer(text):
    stemmer = PorterStemmer()
    return " ".join(stemmer.stem(word) for word in text.split())

def remove_stopwords(text, preserve_case=True):
    tokens = [token.strip() for token in tokenizer.tokenize(text)]
    kept = [t for t in tokens if t.lower() not in stopword_list]
    return " ".join(kept if preserve_case else [t.lower() for t in kept])

If original case must be preserved, lowercase only for stopword lookup and output the original token. Lowercasing everything reduces duplicate vocabulary (Ship/ship) but can destroy useful cues such as acronyms or named entities.

Stemming applies rules to strip affixes and may produce non-words; it is fast but crude. Lemmatisation maps a word to a dictionary base form, is linguistically cleaner, and may need part-of-speech information.

Cleaning is important because a model can learn spurious HTML/noise patterns. But preprocessing must be task-aware: removing punctuation can erase sentiment (!), removing digits can erase model numbers/dates, and removing negators such as not can reverse sentiment. Never fit the vocabulary or IDF on the test set—doing so leaks information.

4. Basic implementation: 20 Newsgroups + linear SVM

The first demonstration classifies two 20 Newsgroups categories: rec.sport.baseball and sci.space.

import pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import fetch_20newsgroups
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, classification_report

newsgroups = fetch_20newsgroups(
    subset="all",
    categories=["rec.sport.baseball", "sci.space"],
    shuffle=True,
    random_state=42,
)

df = pd.DataFrame({"text": newsgroups.data, "label": newsgroups.target})
X_train_text, X_test_text, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    df["text"], df["label"], test_size=0.30, random_state=42,
    stratify=df["label"],
)

vectorizer = TfidfVectorizer(stop_words="english", max_df=0.7)
X_train = vectorizer.fit_transform(X_train_text)
X_test = vectorizer.transform(X_test_text)

clf = SVC(kernel="linear")
clf.fit(X_train, y_train)
y_pred = clf.predict(X_test)

print(accuracy_score(y_test, y_pred))
print(classification_report(
    y_test, y_pred, target_names=newsgroups.target_names
))

Why this version is safer than the spoken ordering: split raw documents first; fit TF–IDF only on training text; merely transform test text. Fitting on all data before splitting allows test-document frequencies into training.

The lecture’s five-step workflow is: (1) load/prepare data, (2) TF–IDF vectorisation, (3) train/test split, (4) train linear SVM, and (5) predict/evaluate. A linear decision boundary is effective for very high-dimensional sparse text. It does not itself “reduce dimensionality”; it handles sparse high-dimensional features efficiently. Common splits are 70:30 train:test or 60:20:20 train:validation:test, chosen according to dataset size and tuning needs.

5. IMDb sentiment-analysis workflow

The IMDb CSV has 50,000 reviews and two columns, with 25,000 positive and 25,000 negative examples: a balanced binary-classification benchmark. The demonstration slices the first 40,000 for training and the final 10,000 for testing (80:20).

Caveat: contiguous slicing is safe only if row order is suitably random. Prefer a shuffled, stratified split with random_state for reproducibility.

  • NumPy/Pandas: numerical work and tabular manipulation.

  • NLTK: tokenisation, stopwords, stemming and lemmatisation.

6. Classifiers compared

Logistic Regression

A linear probabilistic classifier. It estimates class probability through the logistic/sigmoid function:

\[ P(y=1\mid x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(w^Tx+b)}} \]

It is fast on sparse features, interpretable through coefficients, naturally supplies probabilities, and often balances precision and recall well. Regularisation strength, solver, penalty and class weights are tunable.

Multinomial Naive Bayes

A fast probabilistic text classifier based on the conditional-independence assumption. It is especially natural for non-negative count/TF–IDF features, needs little memory and provides a strong baseline. Its simplifying independence assumption can limit accuracy.

SGD-based linear classifier / linear SVM

SGDClassifier optimises a linear model incrementally and scales to very large sparse datasets. With hinge loss it behaves as a linear SVM; with log loss it resembles logistic regression. Results depend on loss, regularisation, learning schedule, stopping and random seed.

Linear SVC / SVC(kernel="linear")

SVM learns a maximum-margin separating hyperplane. Linear kernels are particularly suitable for sparse, high-dimensional text. Standard SVC may train more slowly and consume more resources than specialised linear/SGD implementations; probability estimates are not native unless enabled/calibrated.

Random Forest and deep-learning alternatives

The domain case studies also suggest Random Forest (an ensemble of decision trees) and, for sufficient data/complexity/resources, RNNs, LSTMs and CNNs. Model choice depends on data volume, label quality, sequence/context requirements, computational budget, latency and interpretability—not algorithm prestige.

7. Evaluation

For a designated positive class:

  • TP: actual positive, predicted positive.
  • TN: actual negative, predicted negative.
  • FP: actual negative, predicted positive.
  • FN: actual positive, predicted negative.

\[ Accuracy=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN} \]

\[ Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP} \qquad Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN} \]

\[ F1=2\frac{Precision\times Recall}{Precision+Recall} \]

  • Accuracy: overall fraction correct; most meaningful when classes and error costs are reasonably balanced.
  • Precision: reliability of positive predictions; high precision means few false alarms.
  • Recall/sensitivity: coverage of actual positives; high recall means few missed cases.
  • F1: harmonic balance of precision and recall; useful when both error types matter.
  • Confusion matrix: exposes exact error counts and prevents an aggregate score from hiding failure modes.

Always state which label is treated as “positive.” If “negative review” is the operational target, a false negative commonly means a genuine complaint predicted as non-negative; that missed complaint can cause dissatisfaction, support escalation and churn.

Correct model-development discipline

  • Train on the training set.
  • Select preprocessing, features and hyperparameters on a validation set or by cross-validation.
  • Use the test set once for an unbiased final estimate.
  • Do not repeatedly tune from test results; that is test leakage.
  • After final evaluation, a production model may be retrained on all available labelled development data using the frozen choices.
  • Conduct error analysis on misclassified examples, tune hyperparameters (e.g., regularisation/learning rate; epochs for iterative/neural models), consider ensembles, and monitor drift after deployment.

8. ShopVista case study

ShopVista sells SmartHome devices. Nora analyses the latest 5,000 reviews after Liam notices more mentions of delays and connectivity issues. She compares TF–IDF with a BoW baseline using Logistic Regression, Multinomial NB, SGD-based Linear SVM and standard linear SVC.

Observed results/details:

  • Logistic Regression: negative-class F1 = 0.82, with balanced precision and recall.
  • Multinomial NB: faster training but slightly lower negative-class F1 = 0.78.
  • SGD-based SVM: 15% fewer false positives than the BoW baseline but 10% more false negatives, demonstrating a precision–recall trade-off.
  • Linear SVC: highest precision option, but longer training and more computation.
  • Positive word cloud (green): terms such as durable and easy.
  • Negative word cloud (red): delay and broken.

A word cloud is descriptive EDA, not a feature extractor, classifier or evaluation metric. It helps business teams notice themes but frequency can be misleading without context or negation.

Business decision: early detection of delay complaints may reduce support costs. Missing genuine negative feedback is likely costlier than investigating some false alarms, so negative-class recall and F1 deserve emphasis. With a two-week launch deadline, Logistic Regression is the most balanced recommendation: best stated F1, probability output, practical speed and balanced error profile. Choose NB if resource/training-time limits dominate; choose Linear SVC when minimising false positives/high precision is worth the added cost. Training occurs offline, so production inference latency, memory, calibration, reliability and monitoring also matter.

9. Domain case studies and solution blueprint

E-commerce feedback

Problem: inefficient service/product improvement because feedback sentiment is not understood.
Goal: positive/negative/neutral classification.
Data: product reviews, social-media comments and support tickets.
Method: remove HTML/special characters/stopwords, lowercase, stem/lemmatise; use BoW/TF–IDF and optionally Word2Vec/GloVe; compare NB, SVM and Random Forest; evaluate accuracy, precision and recall; split data, tune, analyse errors and deploy.

Maritime incident reports

Problem: large report volumes obscure patterns, trends and root causes.
Labels: collisions, groundings, fires and equipment failure.
Data: maritime authorities, insurers and shipping organisations.
Domain processing: remove irrelevant/noisy/inconsistent data; lowercase, stem/lemmatise; explicitly handle maritime abbreviations and acronyms. Use TF–IDF and domain-sensitive embeddings. Compare NB, SVM, Random Forest and—where justified—RNN/LSTM/CNN. Select by dataset size, complexity and resources; evaluate, cross-validate, tune and ensemble for robustness. Integrate into the reporting system for real-time classification, rapid response, prevention and continuous monitoring.

Government documents

Problem: daily document volume slows retrieval and decisions.
Labels: policy, budget, legal and HR.
Data: official websites, internal databases and physical archives (after digitisation/OCR where needed).
Method: clean inconsistencies/noise; lowercase, stem/lemmatise; handle government abbreviations/acronyms; build word-frequency/TF–IDF features or Word2Vec/GloVe semantics; compare NB, SVM, Random Forest and data-appropriate deep models. Evaluate accuracy, precision, recall and F1; cross-validate, tune learning rate/epochs where relevant, inspect errors, consider ensembles, deploy for real-time retrieval and retrain when document patterns change.

Reusable practical blueprint

  1. Define labels, operational action, latency and the costs of FP/FN.
  2. Collect a broad, representative, legally permissible dataset.
  3. Create consistent labelled examples and audit class balance/annotation quality.
  4. Split before learning corpus-dependent transformations.
  5. Clean and normalise with domain-aware rules.
  6. Establish BoW/TF–IDF linear baselines before more complex models.
  7. Tune by cross-validation and use metrics aligned to business cost.
  8. Analyse errors by class/source/language/time and fix data/labels/features where appropriate.
  9. Deploy the complete preprocessing-vectoriser-model pipeline, not just the estimator.
  10. Monitor accuracy proxies, latency, drift and label distribution; collect feedback and retrain.

12. Likely exam questions and answer points

  1. Define text classification and list applications. State supervised assignment of predefined labels; include sentiment, spam, query routing, topic and intent examples.
  2. Explain TF–IDF with formula and example. Define TF, IDF and product; show why a corpus-common term gets low IDF and a distinctive term is emphasised.
  3. Compare BoW, TF–IDF and embeddings. Counts versus importance weighting versus semantic geometry; clearly say TF–IDF does not learn meaning.
  4. Describe the full sentiment-analysis pipeline. Data/labels → split → clean → vectorise → train → validate/tune → predict → metrics/confusion matrix → error analysis/deploy/monitor.
  5. Why linear SVM for text? Sparse, high-dimensional features and an effective maximum-margin linear boundary; do not claim SVM automatically reduces dimensionality.
  6. Precision versus recall. Give formulas and business costs; connect FP reduction to precision and FN reduction to recall.
  7. Recommend a ShopVista model. Use the given numbers and two-week/business-error context; recommend Logistic Regression unless a different cost assumption is explicitly justified.
  8. How do the three domain cases differ? Same general pipeline, but labels, sources, abbreviations, error costs, deployment and monitoring requirements are domain-specific.
  9. Why not use the test set for tuning? It biases the final estimate; use validation/cross-validation and reserve the test set.
  10. What is the role of preprocessing? Reduce irrelevant variability/noise without deleting task-relevant cues; transformations must be consistent and learned from training only.

13. Final memory sheet

  • Labels first; prediction last.
  • Split before fitting vocabulary/IDF.
  • TF is within-document frequency; IDF is inverse corpus prevalence; TF–IDF is their product.
  • max_df=0.7 discards terms in over 70% of documents.
  • BoW/TF–IDF are sparse statistical features; embeddings encode semantic relationships.
  • IMDb: 50,000 reviews, 25,000 per sentiment; demonstration uses 40,000/10,000.
  • 20 Newsgroups demo: baseball versus space; English stopwords, max_df=.7, 70:30, linear SVM, accuracy + classification report.
  • Metrics: accuracy for total correctness, precision for FP control, recall for FN control, F1 for balance, confusion matrix for exact errors.
  • ShopVista: LR F1 .82; NB F1 .78 and faster; SGD SVM FP −15%, FN +10%; Logistic Regression is the balanced deployment recommendation.
  • Word clouds describe frequent words; they do not predict.
  • Choose a model by data, resources, latency and error cost; monitor and retrain after deployment.
  • Lecturer references: Foundations of Statistical Natural Language Processing (Manning & Schütze) for a strong NLP foundation, and Natural Language Processing with Python (Bird, Klein & Loper) for practical work.

Review-Week Augmentation

The review case applies TF–IDF to customer-support intent classification:

\[ TF(t,d)=\frac{f(t,d)}{|d|},\qquad IDF(t)=\log\left(\frac{N}{df(t)}\right),\qquad TFIDF=TF\times IDF. \]

TF–IDF makes discriminative words such as refund more influential than ubiquitous terms such as order, but it is lexical: it does not inherently know that package ≈ parcel or money back ≈ refund. Dense embeddings supply that semantic signal, and a hybrid model can combine both.

Multinomial Naive Bayes is a fast probabilistic baseline naturally suited to count/TF–IDF features (P(C|X) ∝ P(X|C)P(C)). Random Forest aggregates many decision trees and may capture nonlinear interactions, though sparse high-dimensional text often favours linear/probabilistic models. Select empirically on the same held-out data.

Evaluation must go beyond accuracy. Precision is TP/(TP+FP), recall is TP/(TP+FN), and F1 is 2PR/(P+R). The harmonic mean penalizes imbalance. Inspect per-class scores, macro/weighted averages, and a confusion matrix; a high overall accuracy can conceal minority-intent failure or repeated refund ↔ return confusion.
