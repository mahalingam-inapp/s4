Learning map

This week connects three levels of NLP structure:

  1. Tokens and entities: which spans of text refer to people, organizations, places, dates, quantities, etc.?
  2. Dependencies: which word is grammatically related to which other word, and what is the relationship?
  3. Retokenization: how can an already-created spaCy Doc be corrected by merging or splitting tokens while preserving linguistic structure?

The central practical idea is that NER tells us what important things occur, while dependency parsing tells us how those things participate in the sentence. Together they support relation extraction, question answering, summarization, sentiment analysis, machine translation, knowledge graphs, search, and information retrieval.

1. Dependency parsing

Definition and representation

Dependency parsing analyzes a sentence’s grammatical structure by finding related words and labeling the nature of every relationship. Each relation contains:

  • a head: the controlling word;
  • a dependent: a word that modifies, complements, or is subordinate to the head;
  • a labeled, directed arc/edge describing the relation.

The labels are standardized in frameworks such as Universal Dependencies (UD). A dependency analysis is normally represented as a rooted directed tree:

  • tokens are nodes;
  • labeled arcs represent syntactic relations;
  • there is one ROOT, usually the main verb or predicate;
  • every other token has exactly one head (one incoming head relation);
  • dependents may occur before or after their heads—dependency is grammatical, not merely positional.

Direction convention caveat: linguistic diagrams and libraries may draw arrows differently. The course describes the dependent as pointing to its head, while some visualizers draw head-to-dependent arcs. The invariant exam idea is the labeled head–dependent relation, not the screen direction.

Examples:

  • black car: car is the head; black is its dependent; amod means adjectival modifier.
  • She reads books: reads is ROOT, She is the nominal subject (nsubj), and books is the object (obj/direct object).
  • The cat sat on a mat: sat is the root; cat is the subject; the prepositional phrase attaches through the appropriate preposition/case and oblique relation in UD.
  • I saw a dog: saw is root, I is subject, and dog is object.

Common dependency relations

Label/concept Meaning Example
root Main sentence predicate bark in “Dogs bark”
nsubj Nominal subject Alice in “Alice writes code”
obj / direct object Entity directly affected by action books in “She reads books”
iobj Indirect object Sam in “Give Sam the file”
amod Adjectival modifier of a noun blue → bike
advmod Adverbial modifier quickly modifying ran
obl Oblique nominal, often prepositional office in “worked in the office”
det Determiner the modifying cat
neg Negation modifier not modifying good

The live session also referred generally to a prepositional modifier, illustrated by “sat on a mat.” Exact labels can vary by annotation scheme/version, so answer with the scheme requested.

Why dependency trees matter

Humans do more than read a word sequence: on reading “She reads books,” we infer who performs the action and what is acted upon. Dependency parsing is a computational representation of that understanding. It helps:

  • identify subjects, objects, predicates, and modifiers;
  • recover syntactic and semantic structure for NLU;
  • answer questions and retrieve facts;
  • translate between languages;
  • extract relations/events and build knowledge graphs;
  • preserve core meaning in summaries;
  • determine sentiment scope, especially negation and aspect-specific opinion.

Agent–action–patient extraction

For “The engineer repaired the machine,” dependencies recover:

engineer (agent/subject) → repaired (action/root) → machine (patient/object).

If an exam asks for exactly one triple:

  1. find the main-clause ROOT verb;
  2. find its subject dependency;
  3. find its object dependency;
  4. ignore embedded clauses unless the question asks for them.

For “John said Mary repaired the bike,” the main-clause triple begins with John–said–…; Mary–repaired–bike belongs to an embedded clause. Never use “nearest noun + verb + next noun”: it fails with distance, embedding, and passive voice. In “The bike was repaired by John,” surface order changes but the grammatical roles remain recoverable.

Projectivity

  • A projective dependency tree has no crossing arcs when drawn over the sentence. Most English constructions are projective.
  • A non-projective tree permits crossing arcs. These occur more often in languages/constructions such as Czech, Dutch, German, and Turkish.

Projective transition systems cannot correctly express genuinely non-projective analyses. Options include swap-based transition systems, graph-based parsers, and maximum-spanning-tree (MST) parsers. UD standardizes annotation; it does not eliminate non-projective structures.

2. Arc-standard transition parsing

An arc-standard parser incrementally constructs a dependency tree using three data structures:

  • Stack: partially processed tokens.
  • Buffer: remaining input tokens.
  • Arc set: dependency relations constructed so far.

It repeatedly chooses one of three operations:

  • SHIFT: move the next token from the buffer to the stack.
  • LEFT-ARC: create the appropriate leftward head–dependent arc and pop the completed dependent from the stack.
  • RIGHT-ARC: create the appropriate rightward head–dependent arc and pop the completed dependent from the stack.

This process continues until the input has been consumed and a tree is built. The lecturer specifically emphasized practicing this on longer exam sentences, not only easy examples such as “I saw a dog”: identify the root, subject, object, and the sequence/structure of arcs.

Major parser families

Family Core idea Strengths Limitations
Rule-based Apply hand-written grammatical rules Precise in covered cases; no training data needed Time-consuming to build, brittle on unusual structures, no learning/adaptation
Statistical Learn decisions from annotated corpora (e.g., treebanks) Handles variation and predicts from observed data Needs a large, accurately annotated corpus for training/testing/validation
Transition-based Predict a sequence of actions such as SHIFT/ARC Very fast, efficient, suitable for modern/interactive applications Greedy errors can propagate; projectivity depends on transition set
Graph-based Score candidate arcs/trees and select a high-scoring global tree Global optimization; can support non-projectivity Usually more computationally expensive

“Corpus” means a collection of language data; a treebank is a corpus annotated with syntactic trees.

3. Implementing dependency parsing in Python

Method 1 — spaCy

spaCy is an open-source Python NLP library. The lecture installed spaCy and downloaded its small English model (en_core_web_sm, described as roughly 12 MB), then restarted the notebook session so the new dependencies loaded.

pip install -U pip setuptools wheel
pip install -U spacy
python -m spacy download en_core_web_sm

import spacy
from spacy import displacy

nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")
sentence = "Deemed universities charge huge fees."
doc = nlp(sentence)

print(f"{'TOKEN':<15}|{'RELATION':<10}|{'HEAD':<15}|{'CHILDREN':<20}")
print("-" * 70)
for token in doc:
    print(f"{token.text:<15}|{token.dep_:<10}|{token.head.text:<15}|"
          f"{str([child.text for child in token.children]):<20}")

displacy.render(doc, style="dep", jupyter=True,
                options={"distance": 120})

nlp(sentence) returns a Doc containing token-level linguistic features and relationships. Relevant attributes are token.text, token.dep_, token.head.text, and token.children. In the course example, charge is the root; universities is its subject; fees its object; Deemed and huge are adjectival modifiers. displacy.render(..., style="dep") generates the dependency graph.

Method 2 — NLTK with Stanford CoreNLP

Stanford CoreNLP is a Java NLP toolkit that can be accessed from Python through NLTK. The demonstrated workflow was:

  1. download/unzip the CoreNLP distribution and English model JAR;
  2. import StanfordDependencyParser;
  3. provide the CoreNLP JAR and model-JAR paths;
  4. call raw_parse(sentence) and take the first parse;
  5. inspect dependency.triples().

Each dependency triple has the form:

((head_word, head_POS), relation, (dependent_word, dependent_POS)).

The output for the running example includes relations connecting charges/VBP to universities/NNS and fees/NNS.

Visualization options:

  • dependency.nx_graph() creates a labeled directed NetworkX graph;
  • dependency.to_dot() creates Graph Description Language/DOT output, viewable using Graphviz.

The course reversed nx_graph() because it returned visually inverted edges, split the original sentence into words, mapped numeric nodes (starting at 1) back to words, and drew the graph with NetworkX/Matplotlib (with_labels=True, labels dictionary, node size around 2500, a chosen hexadecimal node color such as #B5EAD7, and font size 10).

Method 3 — Stanza

Stanza, developed by the Stanford NLP Group, supplies a neural NLP pipeline and a Python-friendly route to Stanford NLP features without manually managing CoreNLP JAR files.

pip install stanza

import stanza
stanza.download("en")
nlp = stanza.Pipeline(
    "en",
    processors="tokenize,mwt,pos,lemma,depparse"
)
doc = nlp("Deemed universities charge huge fees.")
for sentence in doc.sentences:
    sentence.print_dependencies()

processors may be a dictionary or comma-separated string. Processor order/dependencies matter: for example, POS processing requires tokenization and MWT (multi-word-token) processing first. doc.sentences contains sentence objects with token data and linguistic features; print_dependencies() emits triples containing the token, head index, and dependency relation.

4. Named Entity Recognition (NER)

Definition, purpose, and pipeline

Named Entity Recognition—also called entity identification, entity chunking, or entity extraction—is an information-extraction task that:

  1. detects entity mentions in unstructured text;
  2. classifies them into predefined semantic categories.

An entity is a consistently referenced thing or expression. Categories can include PERSON, ORG, GPE, LOC, PRODUCT, EVENT, DATE, TIME, money/quantities, and percentages. NER converts unstructured text into structured information and answers “who, what, where, and when?”

A simplified pipeline is:

tokenization → POS tagging/contextual representation → entity detection and classification.

Entity mentions are spans of one or more consecutive tokens, not isolated characters. “Google” has length 1; “New York” has length 2; “United States of America” has length 4. Spans preserve the full meaning of multiword names.

Applications and practical use cases

  • question answering, search, and information retrieval;
  • text summarization and machine translation;
  • knowledge-graph construction and relation extraction;
  • improving downstream POS tagging/parsing precision;
  • résumé parsing: candidate name, experience, qualifications, skills;
  • financial analysis: companies, percentages, monetary amounts, report facts;
  • news/topic classification: “President Trump” suggests politics, while “Leonardo DiCaprio” suggests movies;
  • customer/support analytics, document indexing, compliance, biomedical term extraction.

spaCy NER implementation

The lecture used spaCy, pandas, Requests, and Beautiful Soup; set pandas display rows to 200; processed a sentence; printed entity text, character offsets, and predicted label; and visualized entities with colored highlights.

import pandas as pd
import spacy
from spacy import displacy

nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")
pd.set_option("display.max_rows", 200)

content = ("Trinamool Congress leader Mahua Moitra has moved the "
           "Supreme Court against her expulsion from the Lok Sabha.")
doc = nlp(content)

for ent in doc.ents:
    print(ent.text, ent.start_char, ent.end_char, ent.label_)

displacy.render(doc, style="ent", jupyter=True)

doc.ents gives detected entity spans. Character offsets permit exact highlighting or linking back to the source. displacy uses colors to show recognized spans and categories. Colab was recommended for convenient free compute (GPU/TPU availability was mentioned), while a local environment may be preferable for some visualization workflows.

Statistical NER vs EntityRuler

Statistical NER learns from training data, generalizes to unseen names, and is flexible, but may miss fixed domain terms such as product codes or company abbreviations. spaCy’s EntityRuler injects entities using explicit token/phrase patterns without retraining.

Good EntityRuler cases: ABC-204 as PRODUCT, HbA1c in medicine, NASDAQ-100 in finance, ISO-9001 in manufacturing, and Section 302 in law. It is best when terminology is predictable and domain rules are reliable. It complements, rather than necessarily replaces, statistical NER; pattern coverage limits what it can recognize.

5. Sequence labeling schemes for NER

NER is often formulated as sequence tagging: every token receives a boundary/category label.

Scheme Labels Single-token entity Multi-token example “New York”
IO I, O I-GPE I-GPE I-GPE
BIO / IOB2 B, I, O B-GPE B-GPE I-GPE
BILOU / BIOES B, I, L, O, U U-GPE B-GPE L-GPE
  • B: beginning; I: inside; L: last; O: outside; U: unit/single-token entity.
  • IO cannot explicitly distinguish beginnings of adjacent entities and is rarely preferred today.
  • In BIO/IOB2, even a single-token entity receives B.
  • BILOU gives richer boundary supervision by distinguishing final and unit tokens.

Example, “Google opened an office in New York”:

  • BIO: Google/B-ORG, New/B-GPE, York/I-GPE.
  • BILOU: Google/U-ORG, New/B-GPE, York/L-GPE.

Tag validity depends on tokenization. If tokens previously labeled B-ORG I-ORG are merged into one Google token, the BILOU label must become U-ORG. Merely converting annotations from BIO to BILOU does not automatically improve a model; the training pipeline and model must use the scheme correctly.

6. Real-world article workflow: scraping and entity analysis

The course processed a New York Times opinion article about Trump and America/allies:

  1. import BeautifulSoup from bs4, requests, and spacy;
  2. download a URL with requests.get(url_string);
  3. parse response.text with Beautiful Soup using the html5lib parser;
  4. remove script, style, and aside elements with .extract();
  5. obtain text using soup.get_text() and normalize whitespace with " ".join(text.split());
  6. load the medium spaCy English model (en_core_web_md);

Beautiful Soup extracts text from HTML and XML. Web scraping was presented as an integral way to obtain real NLP input, but production use must also respect site terms, copyright, and robots/access rules.

Analysis steps:

from collections import Counter

len(doc.ents)                                  # number of entity spans

Counter(labels)                               # frequency by entity type

7. Merging and splitting tokens in spaCy

Why retokenize?

A tokenizer may split a multiword named entity when one application-level unit is desired, or keep an unusual/typo token together when it should be separated. spaCy’s Doc.retokenize() context manager is the intended tool for merging and splitting spans. Retokenization is not restricted to entity spans.

Merge example: “New Delhi”

Initial sentence: She lived in New Delhi.

The initial tokens are She, lived, in, New, Delhi, .—length 6; doc.ents can still contain the span “New Delhi.” The slice doc[3:5] includes indices 3 and 4 but excludes 5.

doc = nlp("She lived in New Delhi.")

with doc.retokenize() as retokenizer:
    retokenizer.merge(doc[3:5], attrs={"LEMMA": "New Delhi"})

After merging:

  • “New Delhi” is one token;
  • document length shrinks from 6 to 5;
  • the entity remains “New Delhi”;
  • the assigned lemma is “New Delhi.”

attrs is a dictionary of properties assigned to the new token and can include lemma, POS/tag, entity type, and other supported token attributes. Without an explicit lemma, the resulting lemma may not be what the application expects.

Useful checks:

[(t.text, t.i) for t in doc]
len(doc)
doc.ents
[t.lemma_ for t in doc]

Split example: correcting “NewDelhi”

Splitting is harder because it changes the dependency tree and requires new linguistic attributes. Given She lived in NewDelhi., the uncorrected Doc has four word tokens before punctuation handling as shown in the lecture; introducing the conceptual split yields New and Delhi as distinct tokens.

doc = nlp("She lived in NewDelhi.")

heads = [(doc[3], 1), doc[2]]  # choose heads/subtoken as required
attrs = {
    "TAG": ["NNP", "NNP"],
    "DEP": ["compound", "pobj"]
}

with doc.retokenize() as retokenizer:
    retokenizer.split(
        doc[3],
        ["New", "Delhi"],
        heads=heads,
        attrs=attrs
    )

Important rules:

  • the concatenated orths/new token texts must exactly match the original token text; attempting to split a token containing a space incorrectly triggers “newly split tokens must match the text of the original token”;
  • assign a head to each new token, including subtoken index when the head lies within the split token;
  • set appropriate POS/fine tags, dependency labels, lemmas, and entity attributes;
  • inspect the new dependency tree after the operation.

The lecture’s intended analysis makes New a proper-noun compound and Delhi the object of the preposition (pobj in spaCy’s scheme), with both fine tags NNP. Verify using [(t.text, t.lemma_, t.i) ...] and print token.text, token.pos_, token.tag_, and token.dep_.

Most important retokenization principle: merging is relatively straightforward; splitting requires deliberate reconstruction of linguistic attributes and dependency heads. A syntactically invalid split may silently degrade every downstream task.

8. Dependency vs constituency parsing and the LinguaTech case

Two representations

  • Dependency parsing: word-to-word, labeled head–dependent arcs; answers “who depends on whom?” A compact tree has one root and is strong for relations, summaries, sentiment, and information extraction.
  • Constituency parsing: hierarchical phrase brackets/nodes such as S, noun phrase (NP), verb phrase (VP), prepositional phrase (PP), adjective phrase (ADJP), and adverb phrase (ADVP); answers “what phrases exist?” It explicitly represents nested phrases but is generally heavier and more language/grammar specific.

For “The boy kicked the ball,” constituency parsing yields approximately S → NP VP, where NP = The boy and VP = kicked the ball. Dependency parsing instead links the subject and object directly to kicked.

Negation and summary/sentiment quality

LinguaTech’s summarizer mishandles “The movie was not happy for critics,” potentially outputting “The movie was happy.” A dependency not --neg→ happy identifies the scope that reverses meaning. Ignoring negation causes incorrect sentiment, misleading summaries, bad answers, and faulty recommendations.

Dependency trees also support aspect-based sentiment:

  • “The battery is excellent but the screen is disappointing” attaches excellent to battery and disappointing to screen.

For summarization, “The committee finally approved the revised proposal yesterday” has the core committee–approved–proposal; finally, revised, and yesterday may be secondary depending on summary goals.

Architecture options

Option Design Best properties Costs/risks
A spaCy’s default dependency parser Highest speed, low memory, easy maintenance, UD-based multilingual consistency and scale Less explicit nested phrase detail
B Dependency + constituency parser Richest syntax and nested phrase analysis Lowest throughput, higher latency/memory, language-specific grammar adaptation, integration and maintenance burden
C Dependency parser + custom negation rules Directly fixes the known sentiment/summary failure with modest overhead; retains speed and multilingual UD core Rules require design, testing, and maintenance; may miss unfamiliar negation patterns

Stakeholder priorities: engineers Alice and Raj value speed, PM Sofia prioritizes sentiment accuracy, and QA lead Marcus wants cross-lingual robustness. Therefore:

  • choose A for maximum throughput/scalability;
  • choose B only when nested phrase structure materially improves the feature enough to justify its cost;
  • choose C as the practical compromise for this identified negation problem.

UD gives consistent labels such as nsubj, obj, amod, and neg across many languages and helps transfer/multilingual engineering. It standardizes labels and annotation conventions; it does not define every language’s grammar or guarantee identical parser accuracy. Constituency grammars often require language-specific rules (e.g., an English-style NP → Det + N does not transfer unchanged everywhere).

9. Integrated practical patterns

Relation extraction / knowledge graph

Sentence: “Google acquired DeepMind.”

  • NER: Google/ORG, DeepMind/ORG.
  • Dependencies: Google/nsubj → acquired/ROOT ← DeepMind/obj.
  • Structured fact: (Google, acquired, DeepMind).

Domain extraction with rules

Sentence: “ISO-9001 improves manufacturing quality.”

  • EntityRuler: ISO-9001/STANDARD.
  • dependency parse: ISO-9001 is connected through improves to quality.
  • resulting domain fact can feed compliance search or a knowledge graph.

Robust production workflow

  1. acquire and clean text;
  2. tokenize and run linguistic pipeline;
  3. add domain entity rules where needed;
  4. validate entity spans and offsets;
  5. inspect dependency labels/heads;
  6. retokenize only when application semantics require it, preserving attributes;
  7. extract structured facts/sentiment/summaries;
  8. test passive voice, negation, embeddings, long dependencies, multilingual inputs, malformed HTML, and tokenization edge cases.

10. Exam-focused distinctions and pitfalls

  1. NER vs parsing: NER classifies entity spans; parsing models grammatical relations. Neither substitutes for the other.
  2. NER span: may contain one or many tokens; it is not a character label, although APIs expose character offsets.
  3. EntityRuler: rule-based complement to statistical NER, especially useful for fixed domain vocabulary; it does not learn unseen forms beyond its patterns.
  4. BIO vs BILOU: BIO single-token entity = B-X; BILOU single-token entity = U-X. Tokenization changes require relabeling.
  5. Dependency tree: directed, one root, and one head per non-root token. Head order is not fixed.
  6. amod: adjective is dependent; noun is head (“blue” modifies “bike”).
  7. Surface order is unsafe: use dependency roles for passive voice, embeddings, and distant arguments.
  8. One root does not imply projectivity: crossing arcs can exist even with one root.
  9. UD: standardized multilingual annotation, not a parser algorithm and not a cure for all language-specific syntax.
  10. Dependency vs constituency: direct relations and efficiency versus explicit phrase hierarchy and added cost.
  11. Negation: locate what not/never modifies; do not merely flip the sentiment of the whole sentence without scope analysis.
  12. Retokenization: merge changes token count; split changes count plus heads/attributes. New split texts must concatenate exactly to the original token.
  13. Tool choice: spaCy for fast practical work; Stanza/CoreNLP for strong multilingual neural pipelines; AllenNLP for deep-learning integration; UDPipe for lightweight multilingual processing. Benchmark on the actual language/domain.
  14. Scraped data: an empty entity list may indicate failed fetching/cleaning, not that the article has no entities.

  • Head + dependent + labeled arc = dependency relation.
  • One root; every other token has one head.
  • Arc-standard = stack + buffer + arcs; SHIFT + LEFT-ARC + RIGHT-ARC.
  • NER = detect mention + classify category.

  • Doc.retokenize() merges/splits while maintaining Doc consistency.

Review-Week Augmentation

The review makes the syntax/entity distinction especially exam-relevant:

  • Dependency parsing identifies grammatical relations: ROOT, nsubj, direct object (dobj in the course terminology), prep, pobj, and modifiers such as amod.
  • NER extracts actionable information such as ORDER_ID, PRODUCT, location, date, or payment reference.
  • Intent classification determines the user’s goal. Thus “Cancel order AB12345” has intent ORDER_CANCELLATION and entity ORDER_ID=AB12345; “Track order AB12345” retains the entity but changes the intent.

Action-object structure is often the strongest syntactic clue: cancel + order, track + shipment, return + product, and refund + payment. A transitive verb takes a direct object; an intransitive verb, as in “She slept,” need not. Syntax supplies evidence but does not by itself map a parse to a business intent.

For upward tree walking, spaCy’s root points to itself (root.head == root). A function such as reach_parent() repeatedly follows token.head; it succeeds if it encounters the destination ancestor and returns None when it reaches the root first. “No children” is irrelevant because traversal moves child → parent.
